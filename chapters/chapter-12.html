They didn’t speak much at first. There was nothing to organize. The morning had already taken what it wanted, and David’s body, guarding his injured ribs, did not reward conversation.

A few blocks in, Linn said, almost casually, “You don’t have a meeting.”

He stated it as plain fact, without the lift of a question.

David shook his head. “No. Just needed some… buffer zone.”

“Good,” Linn said, and after a beat: “We’ve got time.”

They kept going.

A minute later, long enough that it shouldn’t have belonged to the same exchange, Linn made a small, involuntary sound. A laugh that escaped him before he could decide whether it was appropriate.

David felt it tug at his ribs and stopped, one hand braced at his side, waiting for the pain to pass.

Linn tried to swallow the laugh and failed, the attempt making it worse. He bent forward slightly, hand on his thigh, shaking his head like he couldn’t believe what had returned to him.

“Jesus,” David said, through his teeth. “What—”

Linn wiped at his eyes. “The locker room,” he said. “Remember? Seventh grade gym.”

David closed his eyes.

“The stalls,” Linn added. “Those low ones that always got stuck.”

A laugh tore out of David before he could stop it. It hurt immediately; he folded forward and let it happen anyway, the sound coming in short, broken bursts that cost him breath.

“Yeah, but not like this. I mean… that door was done!”

“Oh God, I tried everything. It was not gonna budge.”

“And then I’m changing on the other side of the room. I hear this terrified, whispering, ‘Psst…Dave!’”

“I remember I was trying to channel you. I thought maybe if I focused, I could get your attention without anyone else noticing.”

“Oh man, did that fail. Every guy in the room was glued to that stall door, watching me try to break it down.”

“Yeah, and then the worst idea in the world.”

“Hey, it was your idea.” David choked on a laugh that sprayed into the cold air. “I just did what I was told and pulled your legs when you got stuck.”

Neither of them could stop it now.

“You said, ‘Okay, big pull!’” Linn’s voice cracked as another round of laughter took over.

“And I grabbed the towel,” David said.

Linn covered his face. “And it was the only thing that came with you.”

They stood on the pavement, laughing until their breath smoked in the cold air—the easy, worn-smooth grace of friends who had known each other too long for dignity to matter.

“You went from boy in towel locked in bathroom stall… to coach standing over your legs—”

“All ass, no towel,” Linn jumped in, “just wedged up under the door with Coach right over the top of me, trying to crowbar the door open.”

“I mean, you were just kicking and screaming, and I was standing there cheering you on like—”

“Oh, you were not. You were laughing your ass off with the rest of them,” Linn protested.

“You’re absolutely right,” David said. “As much of an asshole as the next twelve-year-old.” Then the memory struck him, and it took five tries to wedge the words between gasps. “We… we called you Crowbar for the rest of middle school!”

“You did not!” Linn protested, then burst into laughter again.

David nodded vehemently. “Oh, we sure as hell did! God, when I told my dad about it later… full-on spit take with his favorite lager.”

“Come on. You’re just making that up now.”

“No. No, I swear he laughed for an hour straight. Also came up with the better nickname too. Linn-bar.”

They collapsed into laughter again, loud and helpless against the cold.

Linn straightened, perfectly deadpan. “I just assumed that was because I’d always show up for my lessons with a couple of brews for us to share after. You know? Linn… Bar.”

“Not sure he ever called you anything else the rest of his life.” David’s laughter caught in his throat, turning into a sharp hiss. He clamped a forearm against his ribs, waiting until the spasm ebbed.

When they finally straightened, David’s breathing had changed. It was slower now, even deeper.

Linn’s face flattened as he turned to David. “You know, your dad would’ve had a few things to say about that rehearsal today.”

David straightened. “Yeah. Would have never let me live it down.”

“Disagree. He would’ve loved it. He spent thirty years yelling at you to stop playing like a mathematician and just hit the damn string. For once, you actually sounded like you had something to lose.”

Silence sat sober between them for a moment. “Can’t teach that.”

David turned his shoulder, blinking up at the grey sky until his eyes cleared. “Wish I’d learned more from him.”

“Yeah,” Roger smirked. “Don’t get too hard on yourself, Dave. He gave away most of his best tricks to me, anyway.”

David put his hand on Linn’s shoulder and squeezed tightly, then nodded. “You needed all the help you could get.”

“Linn-bar,” Roger shook his head. “Can’t believe that.” He looked at his watch. “We’ve got a couple still,” Roger said.

David wiped his eyes with the back of his hand. “I’m sure she’s in there already.”

“Oh, I guarantee you she is,” Roger smirked. “Better deliver you for the interrogation.”

“Yeah… okay. Hey,” he paused to make sure the proper point was made. “I don’t know why the hell you put up with me all these years, but… you’re a good man, Roger. A good friend.”

“Yeah. You’re kinda a shithead, but I’m loyal to a fault, so… you’re pretty much stuck with me.”

“Thanks… Crowbar.”

Roger gave a low, mock-urgent whistle.

“Don’t look now. We’ve been spotted.”

David snapped his spine straight—wincing at the cost—and pivoted with exaggerated military precision toward the cafe window, where Celeste sat watching them with an arched brow.

12.2 The Cafe

Afternoon thinned across the cafe windows, laying amber strips over the stone floor. Celeste had claimed a corner table by the radiator, two steaming mugs already waiting on the wood.

Linn guided David to the seat with better back support, then stepped away. “I’ll be at my office,” he said, addressing both of them. “Text if you need anything.”

David nodded. Linn gave his shoulder a brief, firm squeeze, then turned and left, the cafe door clicking shut behind him.

Celeste pushed one of the cups toward David. “I ordered for you. Black, no sugar.”

“Thanks, as long as you didn’t make it yourself.”

She gave him a look of mock indignation. “I made decent coffee for seven years, Lang.”

As their laughter settled, she reached out, quickly at first and then more carefully, slipping the sunglasses from David’s face and setting them on the table. He allowed it with a look cast toward the floor, as though the injuries themselves had become something to apologize for. The damage was worse without the dark lenses to conceal it: the swollen eye socket, the split above his eyebrow stitched together with butterfly bandages, the bruising that spread across his cheekbone in mottled purples and fading yellows.

“I’m only gonna ask this once. Now. Are you in trouble?”

“No,” he said, looking down. “No trouble.”

“Pain?”

“Nothing I can’t handle.”

She smiled. “Professor tough guy, huh.”

“You look… different,” she said.

He managed half a smile, though it pulled at the split lip. “You mean aside from the obvious?”

“No. Because of it.” She leaned forward slightly. “You let Roger drag you up the hill. You didn’t lock the door.”

David’s hand found the coffee cup, fingers wrapping around its warmth. “Roger doesn’t take hints.”

“You didn’t give him one,” she said quietly. “And you didn’t pack up after measure forty-one.”

“The math was right there, Celeste. The room was ringing.”

“I know what was ringing, David.”

David stared into his coffee, watching steam rise in slow spirals. “I can’t tell if I’ve broken through or come apart. There’s no edge.”

“I know,” she said.

They sat in the cafe’s warm drift of steam and clattering porcelain, the silence between them comfortable, worn smooth by familiarity.

Celeste set her hand on the table between them, palm up. David looked at the string calluses along her fingertips, the faint tremor left by the morning’s rehearsal. He reached out and rested his fingers in hers.

Her touch was warm, steady. Her thumb traced the edge of his palm, skimming the faint cerulean stain that still tinted the skin, then rested against his pulse.

“The tuning,” David said finally. “It’s not just an idea. There’s structure underneath. Something that holds.”

“I felt it.” Her fingers tightened slightly. “We all did. Even Linn.”

“Linn was holding me up.”

“Linn was playing the music. You gave him something worth holding.” She paused. “There’s a difference.”

David shifted, his ribs catching with a dry click of breath. Celeste watched him adjust, her eyes steady, waiting until his shoulders relaxed.

“I should probably go home,” he said. “Sleep. Actually sleep.”

“Probably.” She didn’t release his hand. “But you don’t have to go alone.”

David looked across at her. She had chosen the booth with the shadows to spare his swollen eye, her posture patient and unhurried.

“Okay,” he said.

She stood first, gathering her bag. David rose more slowly, hitching his leather shoulder bag over his uninjured shoulder, the cracked ribs reminding him of their presence with each breath. She waited without hovering, then fell into step beside him as they moved toward the door.

12.3 Walk D - Part 1 | For Celeste and David

Outside, the late afternoon slanted toward early dusk, casting long copper bands across the asphalt. The walk wasn’t long—less than ten minutes on a good day, fifteen at the pace David’s ribs allowed. Celeste matched his stride without comment, keeping close enough that her shoulder occasionally brushed his coat.

They walked in silence, their footsteps finding an unintentional rhythm, a slow 3/4 that neither rushed nor dragged. Students passed them, absorbed in their own trajectories. The physics building rose to their left, its windows catching the low sun. David didn’t look up at his office.

“Every day, you know,” Celeste said, as if picking up a conversation already in progress. She stared straight ahead toward the crest. Towards the bench.

David frowned, hands burrowed in the pockets of his jacket. “What?”

“Every day, I think of it.”

He squinted at her, waiting for the punchline, but she only watched the ground ahead.

“Celeste, I don’t know what you mean.”

She pointed with her chin. “It’s right there. I pass it every day. So do you. And every time, another flash of what might have been.”

He stopped, awkwardly, as if the sidewalk had ended without warning. He did not want to burden her with the buzz and the menace of it. He wouldn’t know how to explain it, even then.

“Well, it’s silly, I know,” she said, as if trying to float it away.

“It is silly.” His lips pressed into a line, sharp as a string under tension. “You shouldn’t.”

“You mean you don’t ever dream of—”

David exhaled hard through his nose. “No. I don’t. Ever!”

A pause. Then, quieter:

“I’ve bled that day out long ago. Drained it. Whatever that thing is in the ground, it doesn’t belong to me anymore. It’s not my problem.”

The dull ache beneath his ribs spiked, sharp as a needle, giving the lie to every word.

He gestured toward the crest. “Those two up there? They’re squatting on ghosts, not dreams.”

High on the hill, two of their own students sat on the bench, oblivious to the coming encounter. Their voices drifted down through the sharp air, lazy and amused.

12.4 The Bench

“I’m just saying,” Roman said, smoke leaking from both corners of his mouth, “if the multiverse is real, then somewhere I’m already sober. Perfectly sober. That’s just quantum balance, babe.”

Greene, flat on her back beneath the skeletal sycamore, didn’t open her eyes. “You’re failing quantum mechanics in this universe.”

“Maybe in this one,” Roman said. “But in another, I’m a tenured professor with a full head of hair.”

“You have a full head of hair now, idiot.”

“Yeah, but it’s scared. It knows what’s coming.”

Greene groaned, laughing. “This is what tenure does to people. They go all cursed prophet. You’ve been breathing too much library dust.”

Roman passed the joint with exaggerated precision. “I breathe ideas. My body is a finely tuned antenna for cosmic nonsense.”

“That’s why you failed the midterm.”

“No, I failed because I challenged the grading rubric. With passion.”

Greene snorted, then froze. Her gaze flicked toward the path.

“Oh. Shit.”

Roman turned. “What—?”

“Lang. And Armitage. Like, right there.”

Roman sat bolt upright, his boot catching on Greene’s tote bag. The joint flew from his fingers. He made a strangled squeak, scrabbling in the dirt to grind out the roach.

Twenty feet down the path, David stepped onto the lip of the exposed Devonian shale shelf where the bench was anchored above the gorge. The moment his boot took the stone, a low-frequency shear wave traveled through the bedrock, rippling up through the soles of his shoes and locking directly into his cracked ribs.

The breath tore out of his chest. His knees gave way, the ground pitching violently as if the fault line had slipped.

“David!” Celeste lunged, catching him by the arm. For a moment he hung there, suspended by her grip, breath coming in short, sharp bursts. He couldn’t breathe. He couldn’t… He couldn’t actually breathe.

The students leapt to their feet, music major first. “Professor Lang! Are you okay?” Greene hollered.

“Greene? Roman? You two. Help me. Help me get him to the bench.”

They scrambled to his side, one under each shoulder, and together they half-dragged, half-carried him to the bench. David collapsed onto the slats, head bowed, eyes wild and unfocused. Roman hovered nearby, hands fluttering helplessly.

Celeste knelt in front of him, her voice low. “Breathe. In and out. Just listen to me.”

The sound came again, clearer this time. A dissonant hum, like a cello string bowed underwater.

Only David seemed to hear it.

He pressed his palms to his temples, riding the wave of sensation until it passed.

“Are you all right, Professor?” Greene asked, her voice trembling.

David straightened slowly. Color returning to his face in increments. He nodded. “Yes. Yes, I think so.”

Celeste stood, brushing off her knees. “Thank you, both of you.” The students looked unsure. Should they linger or flee?

“No problem, Professor Armitage,” Greene offered. “Hope you feel better soon, Professor Lang.”

Celeste nodded, “See you in seminar next week, Greene.”

The two students mumbled their apologies and backed away down the trail, disappearing into the dusk.

As David straightened, he called after his quantum mechanics student, “Mr. Roman, don’t get any ideas about this improving your abysmal marks on the midterm. But, between us, you’ve got a better shot at a retake than you did five minutes ago.”

“Yes, sir. Thank you, sir! Feel better, David. I mean…PROFESSOR.” And with that, he turned and scampered off.

David stared down at the exposed shale beneath the bench, where the crushed roach still sent a thin thread of smoke from the dirt. “I’m okay. It was just… my ribs caught.”

“If you’re waiting for me to interrogate you, keep waiting. Who you get in fistfights with and why is no longer any of my business, thank God.”

She gave him room to speak, waiting for the defense she was certain would follow. But he said nothing.

Together, they sat for a long moment, listening to the silence that had settled impossibly over the quad.

Then something stirred in her, sharp and sudden. Celeste stood and walked toward the lip of the ridge, looking out across the valley. The wind caught her hair; below, the distant Finger Lake held the last light in a bruised, slate blue. David rose slowly, keeping his ribs guarded, and stepped up beside her. He kept his hands in his pockets, waiting.

A chill gust swept down through the bare sycamores, carrying the cold iron scent of the gorge. She drew in the sharp air, holding it before she spoke.

“I miss being a scientist, David,” she said, voice clean and simple, as if announcing the weather.

The admission seemed to surprise her less than the force with which it had arrived, for somewhere beneath the accumulation of years, the career she had built and the considerable satisfaction she had taken in it, there remained the extraordinary intellectual appetite that had once carried her through entire nights of calculation without any consciousness of their passing. Standing above a landscape whose apparent permanence concealed hundreds of millions of years of upheaval, she found herself wanting to ask questions of it, to measure something, to discover an error in a respectable explanation and pursue the consequences.

For a moment, she remembered what it had been like to have someone with whom to attempt it all.

He let the silence hold. A crow called from the pines behind them, its cry flat against the cold. Celeste’s eyes were bright, holding back tears she refused to wipe away.

“I know you do, Cele,” David said. He shifted his weight, boots grinding in the gravel. “I shut you out. I told myself I was keeping you safe from it, but I was just... I was shutting the study door.”

She looked at him directly then, searching his face beneath the bruises.

“Yes,” she said quietly. “You were.”

David met her gaze, offering no defense. He wanted absolution, but settled for endurance.

A draft rose from the gorge below, smelling of wet shale, fallen pine needles, and coming snow.

“I’m sorry, Cele.”

She heard the apology, and she believed it. It did not erase the years, nor knit the fracture whole, but it altered the space between them.

“I know you are,” she said.

The wind caught the hem of her wool coat. She stepped to the rim of the rock, taking a slow, deep breath that steadied her shoulders. Behind them, the sun dipped beneath the cloud shelf, the water below darkening from bruised blue to indigo.

“Come on,” she said, the faintest smile touching the corner of her mouth. “We can’t stand here all night without it turning into a requiem.”

12.5 Walk D - part 2 | For Celeste, David, bridge, and porch light

They descended from the ridge in silence as the amber light faded to violet, shadows pooling beneath the bare branches along the walk. David’s breathing settled into the careful rhythm his injured ribs allowed; when the dark mass of the physics building rose beside them again, he kept his eyes on the path ahead, refusing the backward glance the way someone passes a former home whose interior they still know by heart.

They crossed the canyon bridge and closed the final few blocks of the trip to the house on the hill.

The falls had grown quieter with the coming cold, though there was still enough open water to fill the gorge with a steady rush that reached Celeste's house on the western hillside. By January, ice would begin enclosing the descending water, leaving much of its movement hidden beneath the frozen surface. For now, the sound remained substantial, rising past the dark shelves of Devonian shale and reaching the porch through the bare branches of the trees.

When they reached the porch steps, Celeste climbed the first two, then turned to look back and study him. From this angle, their eyes were level. She studied his bruised face in the yellow glow of the carriage lamp, her mouth twitching with dry amusement as if weighing whether he could actually be trusted inside the house.

He couldn’t read her. She knew he couldn’t read her. When she turned to go inside, David found she had not released his hand. The slight tremor from hours of maintained tension disappeared.

She let out a quick sniff and a sly grin as she turned to climb the rest of the steps. It was a clear enough indication that he was welcome inside, but David lingered a moment longer, turning his head to take in the deep rustled breath of the gorge and the western campus beyond. This place he loved so deeply, this refuge he'd found so long ago, still leaned into the hill with the same aged wisdom as the day he first arrived. The wave of sound that had followed him through lecture halls and corridors, into sleep and out again, was no longer distinguishable beneath the great wash of water rising over the cliff sides. He stood listening to the falls, to a car passing somewhere behind the trees, to Celeste finding her keys in the pocket of her coat. There was nothing remarkable in any of it, and the ordinary sounds of an ordinary evening seemed, for reasons he could not yet explain, an almost extravagant kindness—a grace he hadn’t realized he’d missed so desperately.

"You coming?" Celeste said, holding the heavy glass-inlaid door open a crack. "Hell of a lot warmer in here, Lang."

"Yeah," he said, his battered ribs beginning to throb again in the evening chill. "One hell of a view."

"Me or the falls?"

"Yup," he said, and crossed the threshold.

12.6 The House on the Hill (return)

Warm light spilled out into the dusk. The smell of old wood and lavender. Inside, the hallway was lined with framed concert programs and photographs of ensemble performances spanning decades—the accumulated record of a life in music, each frame a stratum of devotion laid down over years the way the house itself had been laid down, plank by plank, by hands that meant to stay.

“You want tea?” she asked, already moving toward the kitchen. “Or something stronger?”

“Tea’s fine. I like what you did with all the programs. God, so much musical history in these,” he said out loud. So much of their history, he only thought.

David stood, taking in the place that had been his lost cause project for so many years.

“Hello, old friend.” Then he saw the broken banister finial sitting on the table. He picked it up.

“Hey, Cele, this finial, it looks stripped. Want me to—”

She was already running back to the foyer to stop him.

“No,” she said, taking the wooden globe from his hands and setting it firmly back on the table. “That’s my problem now. Not yours. Hands off.” She gave him a sharp, knowing look, then turned toward the kitchen. “In fact, resist the urge to fix anything this evening. You’ve got enough repairs on your hands as it is.”

She was right, of course. So David set his leather shoulder bag on the hall bench, letting the house’s acoustics settle around him—the familiar give of the floor joists beneath his boots, the quiet settling of the hallway timbers in the evening chill.

Celeste returned with two mugs, steam rising in delicate spirals. She handed him one, then gestured toward the stairs.

“Come on,” she said. “You can’t work on the draft standing in my hallway.”

He followed her up to the second floor, past the bathroom that had taken him seven months to gut and rebuild—well, nearly rebuild. Past the guest room that had served as his staging area for drywall and power tools. Then into the bedroom. Streetlight reflected off the old wavy glass of the double-hung windows. The desk, the dresser, the lamps, the bed—everything occupied the same coordinates. He couldn’t decide whether the permanence was an anchor or an ache.

She set her mug down on the desk, turned to face him.

“This isn’t—” David started.

“I know what it isn’t,” she said. “And I know what it is. You need rest. You need to not be alone. We can figure out the rest as we go.”

The simplicity of her statement, the lack of drama or complication, disarmed him completely.

“Okay,” he said. Then silence between them, before, “Celeste, you are—”

“A goddess, a saint, a genius? Yes, these things I know.”

She smiled then, genuine and unguarded. “Now sit down before you fall down. You’re pale. You look horrible.”

David lowered himself onto the edge of the bed. The mattress was firmer than his own, the wool blanket smoothed over the quilt. Celeste moved to draw the curtains, then paused.

“Do you want them open or closed?”

He thought about it. About the evening light still clinging to the rooflines across the street. About the fact that he’d spent too many hours in deliberate darkness lately.

“Open,” he said.

She left the curtains open, turning back to him. She crossed the distance, sat beside him, and her hand found his.

“Thank you,” David said quietly.

“For what?”

“For not asking questions I can’t answer yet.”

“They’ll keep,” she said. “The questions always keep.”

Outside, dusk deepened into night. Inside, the bedside lamp cast a ring of warm amber across the floorboards. Neither spoke; they simply sat together in the quiet room.

After a moment, Celeste stood. “I’ll be right back.”

She left him there, returning minutes later with the tea refreshed and a blanket from somewhere deeper in the house. David had removed his jacket and his shoes. The tuning fork sat on the nightstand, catching the lamplight.

Celeste looked at the fork on the nightstand, then leaned back against the headboard beside him, pulling the quilt across his legs.

He chose closer.

12.7 The Draft

David woke to the disorientation of an unfamiliar ceiling, then remembered. The house. The bed. The tuning fork was a cool weight against his sternum, tucked beneath his shirt.

She wasn’t beside him. He turned his head carefully, finding her silhouette at the desk by the window. She’d pulled on an oversized shirt and was writing something, her posture suggesting absorption—the particular hunch of a mind that has left its body to its own devices and gone somewhere else entirely.

“What time is it?” His voice was rough.

She turned. “Just past nine. You slept three hours.” She stood, crossing to the bed. “How do you feel?”

“Like I got mugged and woke up in your bed without the fun part in between.” He paused. “In that order.”

The corner of her mouth lifted. “Accurate.” She sat on the edge of the bed. “I made tea. And Linn texted. Twice.”

David reached for his phone on the nightstand. Two messages waited. Linn: Dropped your cello inside your loft with the spare key. Office until 6 if you need anything. A second one, twenty minutes later: Heading home. Call if you need me to come back.

Celeste watched him read them. “I texted him back. Told him you were sleeping, that I’d make sure you got home okay.”

“You didn’t have to—”

“I know.” She handed him his glasses. “But he was going to keep checking otherwise, and you needed the sleep more than he needed immediate confirmation.”

David pulled himself to sitting, a process that required strategic breathing. He shifted cautiously, wincing as his injured ribs resisted the movement. “I should go. I have work—”

“You have work you can do here, if you need to.” She gestured toward the desk. “Or you can go home. But you shouldn’t be alone if you’re going to push yourself.”

He looked at her, at the matter-of-fact way she’d rearranged her evening to accommodate his presence. “The draft,” he said. “The paper. I need to finish the section on harmonic decomposition.”

“So finish it.”

She stood, clearing space along the desk. Her own sketches went into a folder. She pulled over a second chair, angling it so the desk lamp’s glare wouldn't aggravate his swollen eye.

“Tea’s in the kitchen,” she said. “I’ll bring it.”

David made his way to the desk slowly. By the time he eased into the chair, she had returned with two mugs and his shoulder bag from the hallway bench. He took his field notebook from his inner jacket pocket, set it beside her lamp, and opened his laptop from the bag. The draft document appeared on screen, pages of equations and prose that had seemed impossibly tangled yesterday now resolving into focus.

The title remained at the top of the document, unchanged through eight weeks of rejection and several increasingly determined attempts at revision: Harmonic Convergence in Non-Linear Quantum Fields. The argument beneath it had altered considerably. Whether the distinction would be apparent to anyone who had already decided what the paper was worth remained an open question.

Celeste returned to her own chair, pulling out a score and a pencil. She worked in parallel silence while David traced the relationships between harmonic decomposition and quantum superposition.

Night settled in gradual increments. The lamp by the desk cast a small perimeter of clarity. David sat without perfect posture. He couldn’t manage it with the ribs, but the desk’s edge shaped itself around his forearms, providing support.

He traced the mathematical contours directly. He worked slowly, decomposing the non-linear coupling tensor into the discrete harmonic eigenvectors of the boundary condition. The morning’s rehearsal had supplied the missing physical constraint: an injection lock that stabilized only when the driving frequency satisfied the exact integer ratios of the room mode. The blue stain on his palm had faded to a faint shadow, but he felt the residual vibration humming along the ulnar path as he derived the phase-locking boundary, pinning the proof to the page.

Once, from the attic rafters, a faint thump sounded, as if something had shifted in the empty space above. Celeste glanced up, met his eyes, returned to her score without comment. The house held its memories close.

The equations balanced. The cadence reached its quiet resolution, and the draft held together—a clean, unyielding structure sustained without collapse, the architecture of a thought that could bear its own weight.

David closed the laptop. Celeste looked up.

“Done?” she asked.

“For now.”

She set down her pencil. “There’s leftover pasta. Or I can walk you home.”

He considered. His apartment would be cold, empty, still holding the morning’s violence in its walls. Here, there was warmth and quiet companionship. But he needed to sleep in his own bed, to test the silence of the loft on his own terms.

“I should go home,” he said. “But thank you. For the offer.”

She nodded, understanding. “Let me walk you at least.”

They gathered his things. At the door, she helped him with his jacket. His hand went to his breast pocket, checking for the tuning fork. It was there, cool and certain.

“Thank you,” he said again.

“For what?”

“For texting Linn. For the tea. For—” He gestured at the desk, the bed, the space she’d made without fanfare. “All of it.”

“You would have done the same.”

Would he? A year ago, probably not. He’d have retreated, isolated, insisted on handling everything alone—the particular stubbornness of a man who mistakes solitude for strength and calls the confusion of the two a principle. But she was right about what he’d become, or hoped he was becoming. Someone who let Linn find him. Who came to rehearsal injured. Who accepted help and company.

“Yeah,” he said. “I would.”

She smiled, just a small, genuine gesture, and opened the door.

12.8 Walk E | for David, Celeste, notebook, and boundaries

The walk to his apartment was quiet. The night was clear, the air sharp. At his building, she stopped.

“You’ll call if you need anything?” she asked.

“I will.”

David leaned in to kiss her lips, but Celeste offered her cheek instead, the gesture quiet and firm—cheek, not mouth.

“Goodnight, David.”

He stepped back, accepting the boundary without defense.

“Goodnight, Celeste.”

And with that, she turned and walked back toward the hill, her figure growing smaller against the pallid haze of the streetlights.

David unlocked his door, pushed it open, and stepped halfway inside before turning to look back. For the next one minute and forty-six seconds, he stood watching her walk away down the sidewalk, waiting until her coat disappeared past the second streetlight into the cold night. He drew a slow breath against his ribs, stepped inside, and locked the deadbolt. He climbed the spiral staircase to the loft, where familiar silence filled the room. The blackboard triptych stood closed. Across the room, his cello case leaned securely against the sofa where Linn had left it.

He sat at his desk, the tuning fork cool in his hand. He didn’t strike it. He simply held it, feeling its latent frequency humming against his skin, a companion to his own pulse.

Outside, night settled over Ithaca. Inside, the desk lamp held its clean circle of light.

He opened his notebook to a fresh page and began to write.

12.9 Ten Minutes, Maybe Twelve

Celeste was not, under any circumstances, going to give him the satisfaction of looking back as she walked away.

He was watching her go. Of course, he was. He’d been watching her go for twenty years. A man of habit, if nothing else, he wasn’t going to stop tonight.

Or at least, she hoped he wasn’t going to stop… not tonight.

She shook her head and smiled to herself.

If the day hadn’t exhausted her entirely, she might have paused to examine that stubborn, familiar habit of his—the way his attention settled on her at the moment of departure, as though the act of leaving was the only thing that made him certain she was real. Instead, she walked on autopilot, startled when her boot hit the first porch riser.

It was as if someone had picked up her house and dropped it right in front of her while she’d been too busy checking both ways to cross the street. It was jarring. But the whole day had been.

Celeste reached the porch, pulling her wool scarf tighter against her chin. As she pushed the front door open, the floorboards greeted her with their customary creaks and groans—complaining, she felt sure, that its favorite handyman had not returned with her. The old timbers seemed to sag with house-proud disappointment.

Celeste stepped inside and kicked the door shut with her heel. “Whatever, house! You’ll see who loves you best.”

Jesus. I’m taunting an empty house.

She looked up at the balcony rail where, that morning, she had teetered on the brink of something unforgivably stupid. She laughed, but only halfway.

She walked through to the kitchen, flipped the switch on the electric kettle, then turned back with a decision. In the hallway closet, she pulled out her toolkit. Not his. Hers—organized, clean, every driver in its fitted slot. She crossed to the side table, picked up the loose finial, and sat on the third stair with the toolbox between her knees.

The screw was stripped. Of course it was. She had pointed that out two years ago. She braced the newel post with her left hand, set the bit, felt it slip, and adjusted her grip. She muttered a curse, leaned her shoulder into the driver, and turned slowly until the threads finally bit into the old oak. Three full, hard turns until the screw drove flush and tight. Done.

Ten minutes. Maybe twelve.

“Who’s your favorite now, huh?”

Celeste stood. She tested it once with her hand. Then, because no one was watching, she leaned her full weight into the banister and felt it hold.

She put the tools away, returning the box to its shelf, and paused in the hallway. Nothing. The hundred-year-old frame had gone completely quiet. No creaking joists, no water pipes sighing in the walls. It was uncanny. She walked toward the kitchen, treading lightly as if not to wake the place, then turned toward the stairs. Enjoy the peace, she thought. Trust it while—

From the kitchen counter, the electric kettle clicked off.
