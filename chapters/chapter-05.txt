--- CHAPTER TEXT BEGINS ---

Outside the music school, David’s boots found Celeste’s tracks in the snow. For a time the two sets of prints ran together beneath the trees; then hers turned toward the footbridge while he descended into the dark fold of Lower East Hill.

At the fork he watched her tracks continue three paces without him before he made himself look downhill.

The walk home took ten minutes. Eighteen months of living alone had worn it from his awareness. There had once been conversation in those blocks, or silence that held meaning. Tonight, after everything the day had taken from sequence, even that familiar absence unsettled him.

The tall frosted-green door shut behind him with a hollow cough. Beyond the entry hall, the two-story room waited as he had left it: dark brick rising the full height of the far wall, the fireplace cold beneath its mirror, the spiral staircase winding toward the loft and grated bridge. Across from the kitchen, three counterweighted chalkboard panels remained folded into the wall, presenting a blank surface where an hour’s damage could be concealed in seconds.

In the kitchen, David opened the refrigerator, found nothing recognizable as food, and turned to the grease-glossed pizza box on the stovetop. Inside, two desiccated slices of pepperoni waited. He slid them directly onto the revolving glass of the microwave, pressed thirty seconds, and surrendered the matter to physics.

As he sat on the sofa, his satchel drooped open. The leather binder slid onto the cushion beside him.

He knew what it was supposed to contain: the prepared spine of the morning lecture, drafted at his childhood desk in Massachusetts while grief kept him from sleep.

He pulled the binder to his knee. Thumb against leather, fingers under the corner.

The first page flipped. No lecture outline materialized.

Only a spiral occupied the center of the sheet, drawn in black ink with sufficient pressure to emboss the paper beneath. The line wound inward, narrowing into its own dark center.

He turned the page to find another spiral, this one opening outward. On the third sheet, two spirals had been drawn over one another, their curves intersecting so densely that the paper had worn nearly through.

He flipped frantically through the rest. Blank. Blank. Blank.

Then his fingers met a page that resisted them, heavy with ink.

A sketch sat in the center of the sheet, crude and unmistakably clear: a large square surrounded by another square half an inch wider, and within the frame, an iron tuning fork rising from a darkened baseline.

David stood before he knew he had moved. The binder slammed to the floorboards, landing open. The fork stared up at him.

He turned away, sickened by the unreliability of his own hands. In the kitchen, he took a tumbler from the cupboard and the bottle of scotch Arthur had given him three years ago—liquor that had always tasted more of medicinal bandages than malt.

He poured two fingers and swallowed them hard. The burn hit the back of his throat and spread through his chest. He poured a second measure, drank it down, and set the heavy glass on the marble counter with a sharp, echoing crack.

David turned toward the living room. Two steps in, the glass shattered behind him.

He spun back.

Crystal fragments lay scattered across the marble, shards tumbling over the edge onto the floor. A thin pool of amber liquor dripped steadily onto the floorboards.

The glass had been whole when he released it. He had heard the base strike stone. He had walked away. Only then had it burst.

His hand was shaking as he reached into his coat for his phone. Before intention could stop him, he pressed the favorite.

Arthur's photograph filled the screen: standing beneath a maple at David's doctoral hooding, hand raised against the sun.

The phone rang once. Twice. The recording engaged.

His father's voice entered the apartment with its old, precise courtesy.

“Hello, this is Arthur Lang. I can't come to the phone at present. Please leave a message after the tone, and I'll return your call at the earliest interval.”

The tone sounded.

David kept the receiver pressed to his ear. The silence on the line gathered the apartment into its depth: the brick, the rafters, the dripping scotch on the kitchen floor.

“Dad?” David whispered.

From within the line's empty grain, a faint carrier hum emerged. It held steady, pressing closer until it locked onto an exact frequency in the dark: 68.48 Hz.

The apartment seemed to gather inside that vibration, trembling straight through the phone casing into the bones of David's fingers.

“Dad?” he said again.

The line clicked dead. Silence rushed back, sealing every surface.

The screen went dark. For a long moment David held it there, thumb resting over the black glass.

To call again would be to turn grief into an addiction. He set the phone facedown on the mantel and looked into the mirror above.

At first he saw only his own reflection: collar open, chalk dusting his sleeve, dark hair fallen across his forehead. Then Arthur's features declared themselves one by one: the severe brow, the deep bracket beside the mouth, the slight narrowing of the left eye.

David leaned closer. As a child, he had believed that face belonged entirely to his father. He had not understood that the dead wait in the blood, entering by degrees: a gesture of the hand, the angle of the head when listening, the silence used in place of forgiveness.

He placed two fingers against the glass. The surface was cold. For a second, his reflection seemed to divide, Arthur's face beneath his own, and David felt the touch answer against his cheek.

Then the light shifted, and there was only David, pressing cold glass.

He crossed to the wall of blackboards and pulled down the first counterweighted panel. The cable groaned above him.

“You live inside a machine now, son,” Arthur had remarked upon his first visit.

“It's a counterweight, Dad. Just physics.”

“That's what a machine would want me to think.”

David had laughed. Arthur had paused, pleased with himself, before asking if the spiral staircase would pick up his coat if he left it on the floor.

The memory arrived so cleanly that David looked back toward the empty entry rack.

He picked up a piece of chalk. At first, he wrote only what he could defend: 68.48 Hz, and beneath it, 3:2.

A perfect fifth: two frequencies entering relation, holding their nature while each was altered by the presence of the other.

Below it he wrote:

12 minutes, 13 seconds.

3 minutes, 47 seconds.

He left a space beneath the final line.

Twenty-nine years belonged there: twenty-nine years since the afternoon Arthur had stood at the edge of the conservatory stage after David's first solo recital, holding a cup of bad coffee and saying nothing until they reached the car.

“The second movement,” Arthur had said at last.

David, seventeen and raw with wanting, had waited.

“You hurried the return, that’s all.”

“You hated it.”

“That is not what I said, David.”

“You didn't have to say it.”

Arthur had set his coffee on the snowy roof of the car, looking at him with an expression that softened just enough to wound.

“Son, if I had hated it, I would have discussed the parking.”

Now the chalk snapped between David's fingers. The smaller piece bounced across the floor and rolled beneath the coffee table.

He looked back at the slate. He did not write twenty-nine years. He drew lines between the numbers, erased them with his palm, and replaced time with frequency, frequency with distance. At the corner of the board he began the concentric squares from the notebook, then scrubbed them out before the tuning fork could take shape.

White dust coated his hand. He wiped it against his slacks, leaving the pale imprint of fingers there.

Every attempt to balance the interval slid out of tune: music stripped of humanity, mathematics made unreliable by longing. His best work had always lived in that narrow shimmer between tremolo and theorem. Arthur had distrusted that border while making David believe it was the only thing worth defending.

Once, the phone shifted a millimeter against the wood of the mantel. David looked at it, waiting for the screen to light up with Arthur's name, for the laws of matter to be briefly suspended by love.

The screen remained black.

*   *   *

By 2:45 a.m., the room had resolved into a triangle of potentials: stove, chalkboard wall, cello case. Each a vertex. None resolved.

The refrigerator compressor shuddered into motion; beneath it, the bathroom faucet dripped its slow measure. At the foot of the mantel, shards of crystal held fractured slices of the room: brick, window, the white edge of slate.

Chalk dust drifted through the winter draft from the high windows, rising and falling too slowly for gravity.

David watched as a fleck drifted through the space his father's voice had occupied. It floated down until a single pale particle settled on the dark face of the phone, and then was still.
