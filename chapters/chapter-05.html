Outside the music school, David’s steps found Celeste’s in the snow and kept to them across the quad. For a time the two sets of prints ran together beneath the trees, though she was already gone; then hers turned toward the bridge while he descended into the dark fold of Lower East Hill.

At the fork he watched her tracks continue three or four paces without him. Then he made himself look downhill.

The walk home took no more than ten minutes. Eighteen months had worn it almost entirely from his awareness. There had once been conversation in those blocks, or silence that held meaning. Now he left rehearsal and found himself at the apartment door with no clear sense of what had occupied the interval. Tonight, after everything the day had already taken from sequence, even that familiar absence unsettled him.

The tall frosted-green door shut behind him with a hollow cough. Beyond the narrow entry hall, the two-story room waited as he had left it: brick rising the full height of the far wall, the fireplace dark beneath its mirror, the spiral staircase winding toward the loft and the grated bridge above. Across from the kitchen, three counterweighted chalkboard panels remained folded into the wall, presenting a blank white surface where an hour’s damage could be concealed in seconds.

The apartment was not warm, but neither was it clinical. The jagged brick, steel, glass, the chalk dust worked permanently into the baseboards, all unfinished intentions that had always suited him. Other people remarked that the place detested company. With David it seemed to close around him, asking nothing.

In the kitchen, he stood for a moment, trying to determine whether the hollowness beneath his ribs was hunger or something less cooperative.

He opened the refrigerator, found nothing he was willing to recognize as food, and turned instead to the grease-glossed pizza box abandoned on the stovetop. Inside, three desiccated slices of convenience-store pepperoni waited for him.

David tried first to jam the entire box into the microwave. When it refused to fit, he considered the problem for several seconds, then simply tilted the box and let the congealed slices slide directly onto the revolving glass.

He shut the door, pressed thirty seconds, and surrendered the matter to physics

As he sat down, the flap of his satchel drooped open. The leather folder slid out onto the couch beside him. David stared at it as he chewed through the last bite of pepperoni. He had the distinct feeling it was staring right back.

For a moment there was nothing, a silence so dense it threatened to suffocate. When it became clear that neither of them was going to back down, David pulled the binder to him.

He knew what it contained. The prepared spine of the morning lecture: causality, the sum over histories, the pivot he had never reached. He had prepared the week's lectures while sitting at his childhood writing desk, the weight of circumstance and the history of the room he had grown up in too immediate and punishing to allow for sleep.

Now those lessons rested inside the binder on his knee. He watched his left one move toward the cover. Thumb against leather. Fingers beneath the corner. A simple action, completed slowly, correctly.

The first page flipped, but no lectures outline materialized. Only a spiral occupied the center of the paper, drawn in black ink with sufficient pressure to emboss the sheet beneath it. The line wound inward and narrowed into its own dark center.

David turned the page to find yet another spiral, this one opening outward. On the next page, two had been drawn over one another, their curves intersecting so densely that the center had been rubbed nearly through.

He turned another page, then another. There were no equations, no numbered sequence, no prepared transitions anywhere. Not even… David’s breath caught hard and he looked away to regather himself. Confirmation began to set in, unyielding and dark. Nothing of what he remembered preparing seemed to exist.

The refrigerator motor had fallen silent. Somewhere in the bathroom, the faucet released a single drop.

David moved with panicked speed now, frantically flipping through page after page. Blank. The next blank as well. Blank and blank and, yes, blank once again.

Then his fingers reached a page that resisted them, as though the ink on its surface had altered the weight of the paper. When the page flipped over, the entire room exhaled and froze. On the page sat a sketch of sorts, crude in its construction, but painfully clear in its identity. A large square, surrounded by another square perhaps half an inch wider. Inside the frame stood a tuning fork, its prongs rising from a darkened line at the bottom.

David jumped up, standing before he even knew he had moved. The binder slammed to the floor, but landed with the sketch still facing upward. The fork stared at David, daring him to deny what his own eyes could so plainly see.

David turned from it, disgusted by the sheer unreliability of his mind's hold on the day's events. He stormed into the kitchen, opened one cupboard, and removed a rocks glass. From another he took the bottle of scotch Arthur had given him three years ago, which David had never admitted tasted to him more of bandages than of fine spirit.

He poured two fingers and swallowed them back hard. The alcohol struck the back of his throat with a wild sting, then changed to a strange warmth as it pressed through his chest and into his belly. A sharp cringe overtook his face. Disgusting, but he poured again.

The second measure stayed in the glass for several seconds before he drank it. Another burn, easier this time, and as he pressed himself to swallow, he slammed the tumbler on the counter hard enough to make the bottle tremble beside it.

The sound shot through the loft with a hard echo, spreading into every crease and corner and up into the raftered beams.

David turned toward the living room and had begun to walk away when, behind him, the glass shattered.

He flinched and spun back at the sound. Fragments of crystal scattered across marble, the counter, shards tumbling over the edge and onto the floor. A thin pool of scotch rolled from fragment to fragment, then started to drip over the counter's edge.

He stared at the empty space where the glass had stood. It was whole when his hand left it. He was certain of that. He had heard the base strike the counter. He had turned away. He had taken two steps. Only then had it broken.

By the time he had the phone in his hand, the tremor had worked its way down into his fingers, persistent enough to make the screen quiver against the room behind it. His dad would have found that funny. Of all the miraculous properties ever attributed to scotch, this was apparently the one nobody bothered to print on the label: May be harmful to fine glassware and perceptual stability when consumed after the person who gave it to you has left the planet. Arthur Lang. The phone delivered the name with complete confidence, a clean mechanical statement carrying the old promise intact even after biology had revoked every practical means of fulfillment. David watched the letters hold themselves there. The system believed Arthur could still be reached. Somewhere inside its architecture, one long number remained assigned to another; one device would ask the network to find its counterpart, the request would climb toward the nearest tower, be handed forward through whatever invisible chain of frequencies and switches knew how to turn wanting into location. That logic had worked yesterday. It had worked for years. Maybe the destination had simply changed.

The thought widened under the scotch. A routing problem. Fine. Routing problems had solutions. Find the signal, boost the request, push it farther than the old map required. The first long number was still here; the second had merely acquired coordinates the network had never been designed to admit. New vector, then. New derivation. Once, the destination had been trivial: latitude, longitude, tower, handset, Arthur. Now one term had gone missing beyond the edge of the available mathematics, somewhere outside the comfortable little jurisdiction in which physics liked to pretend it had accounted for everything. David could almost hear his father objecting to the looseness of the proposition, picking apart the assumptions one by one, but the phone itself remained wonderfully free of such intellectual vanity. It had Arthur’s number. It had a picture. It had a button labeled call. The machine was offering to try.

So let it try.

His thumb touched the screen, and Arthur’s photograph rose from the dark with the quiet cruelty of software carrying out an impossible instruction perfectly. Somewhere, frequencies were already being gathered and shaped around the request. Somewhere, the first number had begun looking for the second. Up the hill to the nearest tower, then onward switch to switch, node to node, farther if necessary. Keep routing. Keep asking. If the old coordinates failed, derive new ones. If the distance had changed, account for the distance. The universe had spent enough centuries humiliating human certainty to earn at least one technical concession now. Maybe death was simply the point at which the current map ran out.

For the first two rings, David lived entirely inside that possibility. The sound itself seemed to sustain it, each pulse sent outward and returned as proof that the request was still moving through some system somewhere, still searching for a destination. Anticipation had already found its old place in his face before he noticed it there. Then the third ring stretched thin, and impossible began reasserting its jurisdiction. The familiar mechanical hitch arrived, followed by Arthur’s voice with all its precise, maddening courtesy. “Hello, this is Arthur Lang. I can’t come to the phone at present. Please leave a message after the tone, and I’ll return your call at the earliest interval.”

The wording went through him with surgical neatness. At present. Earliest interval. Arthur had always spoken as though time itself ought to be properly scheduled if everyone would only stop being sloppy about it. The recording preserved that faith with obscene fidelity, holding open the old arrangement in which his father had merely stepped away from a desk, entered another room, become briefly unavailable. Somewhere in the sentence still lived the promise of return, and David found himself listening for the interval to end.

The tone sounded, and he kept the phone against his ear while the silence opened around him. At first it belonged to the recording, a blank space waiting to receive whatever he chose to leave behind, but the longer he said nothing, the more it seemed to draw the apartment into its circuitry: the dark fireplace beneath the brick, the wall climbing toward the rafters, the broken glass scattered across the kitchen floor and holding narrow strips of light along its edges. The whole room seemed to lean toward the line with him, caught inside the same absurd experiment, waiting to discover whether enough distance could eventually become a kind of answer. “Dad?” The word left him softly and entered the network. Somewhere beyond the phone, beyond the walls, beyond every tower and cable and mapped coordinate the living had bothered to name, the request went looking for somewhere to arrive.

The recording continued. From somewhere within its silence, a faint buzz emerged. It held steady beneath the electrical grain of the line, so slight at first that he might have mistaken it for the pressure of his own blood against his ear.

Then it pressed closer. The call had tuned itself to an exact frequency in the dark: 68.48 Hz.

Through the bleak darkness of a silent line, the entire apartment gathered inside its vibration and trembled into the bones of David's hand. Brick, glass, wire, blood; everything held within the same invisible measure.

“Dad?” he said again.

The line went dead. The frequency vanished as silence seized the apartment all at once, sealing every room and surface, leaving David with the phone against his ear and the air emptied around him.

The screen went dark in his hand. For several seconds, David held it there, his thumb resting over the place where Arthur's face had been. He pressed the button again.

Arthur's photograph returned: taken at David's doctoral hooding, though the frame excluded the robes, the university seal, all evidence of the occasion. Arthur stood beneath a tree with one hand lifted against the sun. His expression was not quite a smile. He had always regarded photographs as minor ambushes.

David remembered taking the picture. He remembered Arthur asking whether his tie was crooked. It had been, yes, but David said otherwise. It was silly, inconsequential even, but there were so few lies between them that this one came back intact.

His thumb moved toward the call icon and stopped.

To hear the message twice would be to turn grief into a habit. Such habits began here, he understood: with a voice still available in the world, asking only to be summoned.

He set the phone facedown on the mantel and looked into the mirror above. At first he saw only himself: collar open at the throat, chalk along one sleeve, hair fallen across his forehead. Then Arthur's features began to declare themselves one by one. The severe brow. The long division beside the mouth. The slight narrowing of the left eye, as if every visible thing had arrived with a proposition attached.

David leaned closer. As a child, he had believed that face belonged entirely to his father. He had not understood that the dead are patient. They wait in the blood. They enter by degrees: a gesture of the hand, the angle of the head when listening, the particular silence used in place of forgiveness.

He raised two fingers and placed them against the glass. The mirror was cold, and for one instant, his reflection divided: Arthur's face beneath, his own laid over it, and David felt the touch answer against his cheek.

Then the light from the kitchen trembled and there was only David, his fingers pressing a surface that returned nothing.

He lowered his hand to the mantel, where the phone remained facedown. Arthur's absence was sealed inside it with all the other preserved things: missed calls, ordinary instructions, a photograph beneath a tree, the promise to return a call at the earliest interval. Language composed for a temporary absence, now forced to bear the full weight of death.

David turned away before he could listen again and crossed to the wall of blackboards: his wall of truth.

He pulled down the first panel. The counterweight moved somewhere above him, followed by the low complaint of cable and track.

The sound had once irritated Arthur upon his first visit after the breakup with Celeste.

“You live inside a machine now, son. You know that?”

“It's a counterweight, dad. Jesus, it’s just physics.”

“Language, David. Please. Anyway, that is what a machine would want me to think, isn't it.”

David had laughed. Arthur had looked pleased with himself for less than a second, then asked where he was supposed to hang his coat. “... or will the spiral staircase come and pick it up for me if I just leave it here on the floor?”

The memory arrived so cleanly that David turned toward the entry hall and the coat rack where no coat hung, and no father waited beside it.

David returned to the boards. Comfort and clarity were expected from them now.

At first, he wrote only what he could defend: 68.48 Hz, and beneath it, 3:2.

After the ratio, he stopped to listen. The interval opened soundlessly inside him. A perfect fifth: two frequencies entering relation, both holding their nature while each was altered by the presence of the other.

Below it he wrote: Fifteen minutes, thirteen seconds. Then, five minutes, perhaps more.

Three minutes, forty-seven seconds. He left a space beneath the final line.

Twenty-nine years belonged there. The chalk hovered. Twenty-nine years since the afternoon Arthur had stood at the edge of the conservatory stage after David's first solo recital, holding a paper cup of bad coffee and saying nothing about the performance until they had reached the car.

“The second movement,” he had said at last.

David, seventeen and raw with wanting, had waited.

“You hurried the return, that’s all.”

“No I… Dad, no I didn't.”

“You did, son. There’s no use pretending otherwise. It happens to…”

“You hated it.”

“That is not what I said, David. Not at all what I said. Look, this happens to everyone…”

“You didn't have to say it.” David’s voice trailed off as he turned away.

“What? What David? Look, to everyone. It happens to every… Why, I can recall literally hundreds of…” He stopped, David was no longer listening. Arthur placed the coffee on the roof of the car, snow and the weight of scattered intention gathering on his shoulders.

“Son, if I had hated it,” he said, regrouping, “I would have discussed the parking.”

At seventeen, David had slouched into the front seat before his father could see how completely the sentence had undone him.

Now the chalk broke between his fingers. Half of it struck the floor and rolled beneath the coffee table.

David watched until it disappeared, then looked back at the board. He did not write twenty-nine years. The numbers refused to cohere. He drew lines between them, erased the lines, replaced time with frequency, frequency with distance, distance with probability. At the edge of the board, he reproduced the paired squares from the binder, then scrubbed them out with the heel of his hand before the tuning fork could rise between them.

White dust coated his palm. He wiped it against his trousers and left the shape of a hand there.

On the board, every attempt to hold the interval slid out of tune: music stripped of the thing that made it human, mathematics made unreliable by longing. And it seemed to David that perhaps his best work had always lived in the narrow shimmer between tremolo and theorem. Arthur had distrusted that border while somehow making David believe it worth defending.

Once, the phone shifted against the mantel. Only a fraction of an inch: the minute surrender of an object poorly balanced. David looked at it and imagined the screen illuminating, Arthur's name entering the room, the laws of matter briefly corrected by love or madness.

He waited until the screen remained dark long enough to become only a screen again.

* * *

By 2:45 a.m., the room had become a triangle of potentials. David stood equidistant from the cold stove, the chalkboard wall, and the cello case. Each a vertex. None resolved. The refrigerator compressor began again. Beneath it, the bathroom faucet kept its slower measure.

At the foot of the mantel, the broken glass held pieces of the room: a fragment of brick, a strip of window, the white edge of the board. In the mirror above them, David's reflection had been divided among the fragments.

Chalk dust drifted through the light from the vertical window, rising and falling too slowly to belong to gravity.

David watched as a fleck passed through the room his father's voice had just occupied. He watched until one pale particle settled on the darkened surface of the phone, and then was gone.
