It was, perhaps, an unfortunate hour to be speaking of coherence.

David Lang had not slept. Three inadvisable energy drinks and four hundred miles of Massachusetts interstate had driven his nervous system past exhaustion and into that particular dizziness that blooms from the gut when sleep has become a spot you stare at on the ceiling.

Twenty minutes earlier, David had decided to abandon the endeavor altogether, making his way downstairs to the kitchen, where he was attempting to soothe his sour stomach with a pint of milk. Though he managed the first gulp well enough, the second missed rather badly, sending a pale and now spreading sheet of white across the marble countertop. David stared at the roll of paper towels on the far side of the room, then back to the open bag of mini donuts directly beside him. Without a second thought, he reached inside, pulled out the first donut, and pressed it into service as a sort of squeegee-dam hybrid.

It was, admittedly, disgusting, the sort of thing polite society might call crass, or even evidence of a minor defect in character. But this morning, David seemed less concerned with such things. The old, long-forgotten privileges of bachelorhood returned to him. After all, the donut was already there. The milk: already there. To introduce a superfluous third party at this late stage seemed inefficient, even wasteful. And so the conservation of resources took precedence: he popped the soggy donut into his mouth.

Finding a great deal of liquid remaining, he reached back into the bag for another donut, and another, and yet another until the bag was empty and the mess was gone. As he chewed, his constitution began to steady, a slow return to baseline that let him break from the kitchen and drift toward the closet.

David had long since refined the act of Suiting into a sequence of near-automatic movements, an applied science resilient even now: socks, slacks, arms into sleeves, the final constriction of collar and tie. The procedure lived so deeply in muscle memory that palette, pattern, and belt seemed to arrange themselves without requiring a single deliberate thought.

At five-eleven, David had kept the lean, square carriage of a man who had never quite learned what to do with being handsome. His face was strongly made: high, rigid cheekbones; a brutally straight nose that seemed drawn from brow to tip with a ruler; a broad, intelligent mouth more naturally disposed toward skepticism than charm. Thick brown hair, still improbably abundant at forty-seven, swept back from his forehead with the sort of careless fullness that suggested less maintenance than it actually required. There was something dashing about the whole arrangement, unfortunately, and David had been aware of this for most of his adult life, an awareness he carried with the same discomfort he reserved for compliments and ceremonial introductions.

As he worked the second cuff through his jacket sleeve, Arthur’s watch slipped into view.

David glanced at the time with a smirk.

7:24:47.

Amazing how easy it was to be early when you got home so ridiculously late.

He had his overcoat in hand, his wallet and phone in pockets, and was turning toward the front door when he remembered the damn book.

It was a tattered, sorry little thing David had rescued from the front parlor of his parents’ house: an eighteenth-century volume on tuning methods, its cover warped by damp windowsills, its pages yellowed by a hundred New England summers. He had found it crammed between a collection of Wagner vocal scores and a Bartók-Meets-the-BSO LP. One look made two facts clear: no one in their right mind would ever read this thing of their own accord, and Celeste was going to absolutely love it.

All that remained was to give it to her before something occurred to transform the gift into an apology.

But the book was all the way upstairs, buried in his suitcase with everything else too delicate to survive travel unprotected. For one indefensible moment, David considered whether remembering the gift might just be enough. The thought had barely formed before standards returned, and some more reliable part of him was already turning toward the spiral staircase to retrieve it.

Upstairs, he undressed the book from its sweater, tucked it into his left hand, and was turning toward the stairs when a sound from outside stopped him at the rail.

Laughter.

Of course, voices from the town below reached the apartment all day, rising with traffic and the hourly migrations of students, but this sound refused to settle among them. It lingered, turning David’s attention toward the balcony, so that before he quite realized why, he was crossing the grated bridge, his right hand tracing the railing, catching each vertical support until there were no more and his fingers found the balcony doorknob instead.

Cold met him at once, sharp and clean, carrying the scent of shale and frost-bitten pine up from the gorges. The morning had broken open beneath a pale winter sky as the sun crested East Hill, casting long indigo shadows over the roofs below.

David stepped onto the deck, wind tugging at his shirt as he moved to the far railing. From there he could trace the narrow streets north toward Cornell, the dark scrollwork of the southern gate visible just beyond the first rise. He closed his right hand around the iron and leaned into the cold, the book tucked against his other palm.

Another sharp burst of laughter flew past him on the wind. In the distance, near one of the old block houses, David spotted a family making their way out into the new day. He watched as a young mother pulled one of her gloves on with her teeth while a little girl attempted to step inside the frosted bootprints left by someone much larger. Without looking down, the mother shortened her stride to match.

A bit farther on, a lanky, awkward boy in a screaming red hat scrambled across the crusted grass with the reckless conviction of someone to whom the earth had not yet proved dangerous. His father pursued at the distance the game required: close enough to catch him, but far enough to preserve the boy’s belief in his own escape.

As the boy dipped briefly out of sight, something robin’s-egg blue flew up from the bushes. David mistook it for a bird at first, but the father lunged, bobbling the thing from one hand to the other before finally trapping the pair of puffy mittens to his chest. He raised both arms, pumping his fists above his head as though he had just saved the kingdom.

Celebration turned to laughter and when it reached David again, he laughed right along with them. The sound left him so easily that he did not at first recognize it as his own. For two weeks, every sound he made had been trimmed for solemn decorum and consoling company. This one wasn’t. He felt the smile settle across his face, unguarded and foolish.

He took a deep breath of the cold morning and turned to go inside.

In his left hand, the pages of the tuning book rustled in the wind. David stopped and opened it, letting his thumb fall somewhere near the middle while several swollen pages lifted and settled against one another.

As he tipped the book toward the light, his cuff drew back and the glass face of Arthur’s watch caught the sun, throwing it directly into his eyes.

David flinched against the sudden white glare.

The watch had belonged to Elliot first, then Arthur, its narrow band carrying the years from grandfather to father, and now to son. Two weeks earlier, beneath the white lights of the morgue, an attendant had unfastened it from Arthur’s wrist and placed it in David’s hand.

David had watched the pale circle emerge beneath where the skin had been protected from age and sun. Without intention, on that impossible night, he had put the watch on for safety, signed the required papers, and driven his mother home to a newly empty house. The watch had not left his wrist since.

For a moment the whole lineage stood before him: three wrists occupying the same small circumference of metal.

As his vision cleared, David looked down at the watch.

8:05.

Wait.

8:05?

The book, the stairs, the family below—none of it could possibly have taken more than two, maybe three minutes at the most.

David had gone from thirty-five minutes early to five minutes late in two minutes flat.

He hurried back to the forward rail.

The pavement below the houses was empty. His eyes followed the family’s route toward the path entering the trees, and at first there was nothing; then, at the farthest reach of what the branches allowed, a fleck of red appeared between two trunks.

The boy’s hat.

It bobbed once, much farther on than it had any right to be, and vanished.

When David looked back to the north, the hills of Cornell were filled with students, and there, just beyond the southern gate, he saw them: Professor Sweeney and the rest of the morning cohort already making their way up the first rise.

This was not an old-watch problem anymore.

No, this was real.

He was late.

David Lang was never late. Never. For forty-six years, this fact had served to guarantee the rest of him.

He dashed inside and practically slid down the spiral stairs like a firepole. On the ground floor he snatched his overcoat from the stool, forcing his arms into the sleeves as he crossed the long entryway. The mirror beside the front door presented an image that could admittedly use some work, but the circumstances called for triage rather than grooming.

David grabbed the leather folder containing the day’s lecture notes, shoved it into his satchel with Celeste’s book, and within seconds the green glass door slammed behind him as he rushed into the Ivy-Leagued madness of an Ithaca morning.

* * *

The light blinded him instantly. As his eyes adjusted, he glanced in all directions to ensure that no one was particularly looking his way, then broke into an all-out, middle-aged sprint toward the southern gate.

Cold needled at his eyes as he darted through the narrow streets, his coat pulling at his shoulders while each block clipped his breath shorter. The town thinned along with his breath, porches slick with frost as modest houses gave way to the steepening order of East Hill. As he reached the south gate, he reduced his speed by degrees to something that might, from a charitable distance, resemble purposeful, respectable walking.

Usually the climb arranged him. Arguments settled into sequence, lectures found their opening moves, and whatever residue life had left upon him yielded to the old disciplinary pressure of the hill.

This morning, Arthur came with him.

I do not know how you stomach these collegiate conversations.

The words rose in his father's exact cadence, thinned by age and carrying emphasis on stomach, as if his profession had been something David had mistakenly chosen to ingest.

He lengthened his stride.

Always one up on the next man. Always something, son.

There was no memory strong enough for David to argue with, only the voice carrying the old exasperation uphill beside him.

Across the lower quad, departments drifted uphill in conversational orbits, yesterday's arguments resuming beneath fresh complaints about whichever dean had most recently mistaken obstruction for leadership.

At the first trail turn onto the main pathway, he spotted his gaggle. Near the center of the procession, Professor Henry Sweeney appeared to have inherited command.

Two weeks of David's absence had left a vacuum, and Sweeney, like all soft men with tenure, had expanded obligingly to fill it.

This walk had belonged to David for years in ways no departmental handbook could adequately describe. With a series of short, undignified bursts, determined that no mere bereavement leave was going to alter that gravity, he caught up to the group halfway up the slope.

Sweeney was mid-anecdote.

“No, I'm telling you, he said cross-disciplinary intimacy to my face. In a budget meeting. To my actual face.”

“Christ,” David said. “Intimacy and face in the same sentence? Hank, my friend, you should probably stop right there before one of these grad students peels off to notify Human Resources.”

Sweeney turned. “Lang. My goodness. And here I thought I had another day. Welcome back, sir.”

“Don't.”

“What? I've barely said two words.”

“Fourteen words, actually, and already I can hear the withering pangs of kindness gathering momentum.”

“You've just buried your father, David.”

“Yes. I have.”

Sweeney let that answer stand while, just behind him, Naomi Feld walked with both hands wrapped around a paper coffee cup, watching David over the lid. From History, Barton offered the cautious nod people reserved for funerals and department votes, while a pair of David’s graduate students slowed discreetly in their wake, maintaining the practiced neutrality of those whose funding depended on finding everyone equally amusing.

Finally, David sighed. “Alright, fine. One humane observation. But only one, Sweeney, so… you know, at least try to make it memorable.”

Sweeney considered the assignment. “Hmm. Oh, I’ve got it. You look almost refreshed… all things considered.”

“Well, it's true what they say. Burying a parent can be remarkably restorative. Takes ten years off the face.”

For a beat nobody spoke, until David added, “And five off your lifespan.”

Sweeney looked him over. “Ah, I see. So that’s why you look eighty again.”

Naomi's laugh escaped into her coffee.

“There it is. Oh, and Sweeney, for the record—cross-disciplinary intimacy? This can only land two ways: Title IX or tenure.”

“Good to have you back, Lang,” Sweeney said, roughening the words enough for both of them.

The usual chatter resumed, though half the gossip prepared for the morning had plainly concerned David; he could feel it withdrawing through softened faces and little rearrangements of posture. He gave them nothing. He had returned, and the world could resume its proper cruelties.

* * *

At the lower crossing, where the older paving stones met the darker run laid nearer the quad, a deep, menacing sound stopped David so abruptly that Naomi nearly walked into him.

But it was not laughter this time.

“Lang?”

It felt at first like constriction, a pressure beneath the sternum followed by a low vibration that reached his jaw before he was certain what the sound even was. He held still long enough to separate the sensation from the ordinary complaints of exhaustion, and as the pressure strengthened he stepped backward almost instinctively, only to feel it ease with enough immediacy that his attention dropped to the pavement beneath his feet.

The change had occurred almost exactly at the seam between the two sections of stone, as if the utilities buried deep beneath had mistaken his rib cage for pipe. He glanced at the others. Clearly he was the only thing strange going on.

“Did you all hear that?”

Sweeney glanced across the quad. “Heard what, Lang?”

“That tone.”

“Oh, we heard your tone, Professor,” Naomi said, drawing a nervous laugh from the group while David, hardly listening, stepped forward across the seam again.

The pressure shot back, but when he stepped back again, it diminished. For several seconds he simply regarded the darker paving stones, then crossed the seam once more, slowly enough this time to be certain of the sequence: pressure gathering beneath the sternum, vibration rising toward the jaw; one step backward, and both receding.

Sweeney had moved closer. “You okay?”

“Yeah. I’m…” David swallowed. “I’m good.”

“It’s just… you’re sweating like it’s eighty degrees out here.”

“Am I?” David wiped his forehead with the back of one hand and looked at it. “Just a few long days, is all.”

“Of course.” Sweeney's voice softened. “Really, though. Glad you're back, David. Your dad was a great man. Be proud of a big life, yeah?”

A big life, as though life could be measured in acreage.

“Yeah,” David said. “Thanks, Hank.”

“Oh, and don't let those quantum undergrads keep you down.”

“They'd need a quorum first.”

Sweeney took David’s persistence of humor as proof enough to let the moment pass. With a final pat on the shoulder, he allowed himself to be absorbed into the advancing group, taking Barton and the graduate students with him.

Only Naomi remained.

The two of them stood in silence and watched the others fade away toward campus.

“You're not fine.”

“No.” David looked beyond her toward the quad. “But I am late.”

Her gaze dropped to the seam. “What happened?”

“I don't know.”

“Do you want me to call someone? Celeste, maybe?”

“No. God, no.”

“Then I'll walk in with you.”

He adjusted the satchel, trying not to call attention to the fact that his hands had begun to shake. “I'd rather preserve the illusion of competence.”

“David.”

“I know.”

Naomi watched him another moment before nodding toward campus. “Okay. I'll be nearby.”

“How ominous.”

“Good.”

She continued up the path, leaving David beside the crossing until she disappeared around the corner. Only then did he move again, giving the darker section of pavement a wider berth than he could presently justify.

* * *

Feynman Hall received him with its familiar administrative quiet, a few faces lifting from computer screens long enough to register David before lowering again without particular interest. Good. Apparently he still looked like someone arriving exactly when he meant to, though his stomach knew better.

By the time the classroom doors came into view, even that pretense had begun to fray, and the remaining thirty feet between David and his students acquired an unpleasant and entirely unnecessary precision.

So many proofs, son.

He stopped at the sound of Arthur’s voice, not a memory exactly, or at least not one he could place, but the cadence was unmistakable.

So much proving for approval’s sake, don’t you think, Davey?

David tried to breathe around it as something beat beneath the band of his watch, once and then again, until there was no longer much point pretending he hadn't noticed.

He looked down.

Eighteen minutes and fifty-three seconds late.

Shit.

The students beyond the classroom doors were waiting, and whatever else the morning had become, it had not relieved him of the obligation to teach them. David tightened his grip on the leather folder, opened the door, and entered with every intention of behaving as though he had arrived precisely when he meant to.

“Good morning. My apologies for the late start.”
