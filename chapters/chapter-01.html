Scene 1 Analysis & Outline

Narrative Summary

David Lang, a physics professor, is running late for his lecture at Cornell after discovering that fifteen minutes of his morning have vanished inexplicably. He joins his colleagues Sweeney, Naomi, and Bardon on the walk up East Hill, recovering his normal banter, but experiences a strange physical pressure and low-frequency hum near a seam in the paving stones.

Beat Outline

  • David Lang wakes up late and realizes 15 minutes have vanished from his watch.
  • David scrambles to catch up with his colleagues walking up the slope to Feynman Hall.
  • David engages in academic banter with Sweeney, Naomi, and Bardon, masking his disorientation.
  • David experiences a sudden, violent chest pressure and low-frequency vibration at a paving seam.
  • Naomi notices David is sweating and not okay, but David insists on continuing to his lecture.

Thematic & Motif Analysis

This opening scene establishes David's obsession with perfect timing and control, contrasted immediately by a breakdown in physical time (the missing 15 minutes). The low-frequency hum (68.48 Hz) introduces the central motif of resonance as a disruptive force that behaves like a physical weight or structural vibration. The seam in the paving stones serves as a physical threshold between ordinary reality and the strange aperture of Project ARGO.

It was, perhaps, an unfortunate hour to be speaking of coherence.

David Lang had not slept. Three inadvisable energy drinks and four hundred miles of Massachusetts interstate had driven his nervous system past exhaustion and into that particular dizziness that blooms from the gut when sleep has become a spot you stare at on the ceiling.

Twenty minutes earlier, David had decided to abandon the endeavor altogether, making his way downstairs to the kitchen, where he was attempting to soothe his sour stomach with a pint of milk. Though he managed the first gulp well enough, the second missed rather badly, sending a pale and now spreading sheet of white across the charcoal-marble countertop. David stared at the roll of paper towels on the far side of the room, then back to the open bag of mini donuts directly beside him. Without a second thought, he reached inside, pulled out the first donut, and pressed it into service as a sort of squeegee-dam hybrid.

It was, admittedly, disgusting, the sort of thing polite society might call crass, or even evidence of a minor defect in character. But this morning, David seemed less concerned with such things. The old, long-forgotten privileges of bachelorhood returned to him. After all, the donut was already there. Indeed, the milk: already there. To introduce a superfluous third party at this late stage seemed inefficient, even wasteful. And so the conservation of resources took precedence: he popped the milk-heavy donut into his mouth.

Finding a great deal of liquid remaining on the countertop, he reached back into the bag for another donut, and another, and yet another until he grabbed the very last one, swiped up the remaining slag, and tossed it into his mouth. As he chewed, his constitution began to steady, a slow return to baseline that let him break from the kitchen and drift toward the closet.

David had long since refined the act of Suiting into a sequence of near-automatic movements, an applied science resilient even now: socks, slacks, arms into sleeves, the final constriction of collar and tie. The procedure lived so deeply in muscle memory that palette, pattern, and belt seemed to arrange themselves without requiring a single deliberate thought.

Against the momentum of the morning, he had laid out his overcoat across the entryway stool, filled its pockets with the accoutrements of modern life, and was turning toward the front door with ten minutes and thirteen seconds to spare, when he remembered the damn book.

It was a tattered, sorry little thing David had rescued from the front parlor of his parents’ house: an eighteenth-century volume on tuning methods, its cover warped by damp windowsills, its pages yellowed by a hundred New England summers. He had found it crammed between a collection of Wagner vocal scores and a Bartók-Meets-the-BSO LP, as though the book had spent two centuries hiding from the world. One look made two facts clear: no one in their right mind would ever read this thing of their own accord, and Celeste was going to absolutely love it. All that remained was to actually manage to give it to her before something occurred to transform the gift into an apology.

But the book was all the way upstairs, buried in his suitcase with all the other gestures too delicate to survive travel. For one indefensible moment, David considered whether remembering the gift might just be enough. The thought had barely formed before standards returned, and some more reliable part of him was already turning toward the spiral staircase to retrieve it.

Upstairs, he had undressed the book from its sweater, tucked it into his left hand, and was turning toward the stairs when a sound from outside stopped him at the rail.

Laughter.

Of course, voices from the town below reached the apartment all day, rising with traffic and the hourly migrations of students, but this sound refused to settle among them. It lingered, turning David’s attention towards the balcony, so that before David quite realized why, he was crossing the grated bridge, his right hand tracing the railing, catching each vertical support until there were no more and his fingers found the balcony doorknob instead.

Cold met him at once, sharp and clean, carrying the whispered scent of shale and frost-bitten pine up from the gorges. The morning had broken open beneath a pale, luminous winter sky as the sun crested East Hill, casting long indigo shadows over the roofs below.

David stepped onto the deck, wind tugging at his shirt as he moved to the far railing. From there he could trace the narrow streets north toward Cornell, the dark scrollwork of the southern gate visible just beyond the first rise. He closed his right hand around the iron and leaned into the cold, the book tucked against his other palm.

Another sharp burst of laughter flew past him on the wind, drawing his focus toward the reason for this strange detour. In the distance, near one of the old block houses, David spotted a family making their way out into the new day. He watched as a young mother pulled one of her gloves on with her teeth while a little girl attempted to step inside the frosted bootprints left by someone much larger. Without looking down, the mother shortened her stride to match.

A bit further on, a lanky, awkward boy in a screaming red hat scrambled across the crusted grass with the reckless conviction of someone to whom the earth had not yet proved dangerous. His father pursued at the distance the game required: close enough to catch him, but far enough to preserve the boy’s belief in his own escape.

As the boy dipped briefly out of sight, something robin's-egg blue flew up from the bushes. David mistook it for a bird at first, but the father lunged, bobbling the thing from one wrist to the other before finally trapping the pair of puffy mittens to his chest. He raised both arms, pumping his fists above his head, as though he had just saved the kingdom.

Celebration turned to another round of laughter and when it reached David again, he laughed right along with them. The sound left him so easily that for an instant he did not recognize it as his own. For two weeks, every sound he made had been trimmed for solemn decorum and consoling company. This one wasn’t. He felt the smile settling across his face, unguarded and foolish, and something inside him yielded to the strange pleasure of having been caught there.

David took a deep breath of the crisp morning, preparing to turn back inside, when he heard pages rustled at his feet. David looked down. The book lay open on the balcony deck, its cover curling in the damp. Wind worried at the swollen pages, lifting them in loose handfuls and slapping them back against the boards.

But David could still feel its weight in his left hand. Wait. No. No, he couldn’t. It was gone there too. Worse still, when he moved to pick it up, nothing answered. The intention occurred. He could follow it from the thought of bending through the expected machinery of shoulder, elbow, wrist, fingers. But somewhere between command and consequence, the arrangement ceased to apply. His arm hung visibly from his shoulder, but its position belonged to sight alone. Internally, it remained elsewhere, holding what it had not held for some unknown length of time.

His right hand should have pulled him upright. Instead, the fingers remained locked around the frozen railing. For one terrible interval David stood bent above the fallen book, attached to the balcony by one hand he could not release and another that no longer occupied the same version of events.

Was this a stroke? Would a stroke permit him the diagnostic privilege of asking?

Before he could finish the question, the left arm came rushing back.

Sensation broke through the shoulder and flooded toward his fingers, fierce enough to empty his lungs. Pins of heat drove beneath the nails. His elbow contracted hard against his ribs, and then his hand slammed back into place.

At nearly the same instant, the fingers of his right hand loosened enough for him to tear them from the iron. Skin dragged before releasing. His palm had gone white, the fingers curled inward as though preserving the shape of the railing after the railing was gone.

That degree of cold made no sense. He had been outside for less than a minute.

The book waited at his feet, pages still flapping in the morning breeze. This time, with deliberate attention, David reached down and picked it up. His fingers closed around the damaged cover and, after a slight delay he could not be certain had occurred, its familiar weight answered, and he had the damn thing in hands once more.

As he straightened, his cuff slipped back and light flashed across the face of Arthur’s watch.

It had belonged to Elliot first, then Arthur, the narrow band carrying years from his grandfather’s wrist to his father’s and now to his own. Two weeks earlier, beneath the white lights of the morgue, an attendant had unfastened it from Arthur’s wrist and placed it in David’s hand.

David had watched the pale circle emerge beneath where the skin had been protected from age and sun. Without intention, on that impossible night, he had put the watch on for safety, signed the required papers, and driven his mother home to a newly empty house. The watch had not left his wrist since.

For a moment the whole lineage stood before him: three wrists occupying the same small circumference of metal. He stared into its face as though expecting both men to look back through the glass.

Great. Broken.

David brought the watch nearer, checking its mechanics. No, not broken. Still ticking, in fact. The second hand swept past twelve in perfect working order. But when he looked at the minute hand, something splintered. He had noted the time on his way up the stairs: seven forty-nine and forty-seven seconds. The detour upstairs, the book, the balcony, the boy in the red hat: none of it could have occupied more than two minutes. Yet the watch stared back unflinchingly: it was five minutes past eight o'clock.

David had gone from ten minutes early to five minutes late in two minutes flat.

What the Fuck.

When he turned back, the pavement outside the house was empty. His eyes followed the family’s route toward the path entering the trees. At first: nothing. Then suddenly, at the farthest reach of what the branches allowed, a fleck of red appeared between two trunks.

The boy’s hat. He saw it bobbed once, much farther on than it had any right to be, then vanished.

Then he heard it: Professor Sweeney and the rest of the morning cohort. He glanced back to the north to find they had already passed through the southern entrance and were climbing the first rise without him.

There was no longer any other way to parse this moment.

He was late.

David Lang was never late. Never.

For forty-six years, this fact had served to guarantee the rest of him.

He dashed inside. On the ground floor he snatched his overcoat from the stool and forced his arms into the sleeves as he crossed down the long entryway. The mirror beside the front door presented an image that could admittedly use some work, but the circumstances called for triage rather than grooming. He looked away with a huff.

David grabbed the leather folder containing the day’s lecture notes, shoved it into his satchel with Celeste’s book, and within seconds the green glass door slammed behind him as he rushed into the Ivy-Leagued madness of an Ithaca morning.

* * *

The light blinded him instantly. As his eyes adjusted, he glanced in all directions to ensure that no one was particularly looking his way, then broke into an all-out, middle-aged sprint toward the southern gate.

Cold needled at his eyes as he darted through the narrow streets, his coat pulling at his shoulders while each block clipped his breath shorter. The town thinned along with his breath, porches slick with frost as modest houses gave way to the steepening order of East Hill. He sustained the sprint until he reached the gate, then reduced it by degrees to something that might, from a charitable distance, resemble purposeful, respectable walking.

Usually the climb arranged him. Arguments settled into sequence, lectures found their opening moves, and whatever residue life had left upon him yielded to the old disciplinary pressure of the hill.

This morning, Arthur came with him.

I do not know how you stomach these collegiate conversations.

The words rose in his father's exact cadence, thinned by age and carrying emphasis on stomach, as if his profession had been something David had mistakenly chosen to ingest.

He lengthened his stride.

Always one up on the next man. Always something, son.

Arthur returned now as pressure without occasion, a syllable struck harder than the sentence required. No memory accompanied the words strongly enough for David to argue with it. There was only the voice, carrying the old exasperation uphill beside him.

Across the lower quad, departments drifted uphill in conversational orbits, yesterday's arguments resuming beneath fresh complaints about whichever dean had most recently mistaken obstruction for leadership.

At the first trail turn onto the main pathway, he spotted his gaggle. Near the center of the procession, Professor Henry Sweeney appeared to have inherited command.

Two weeks of David's absence had left a vacuum, and Sweeney, like all soft men with tenure, had expanded obligingly to fill it.

This walk had belonged to him for years in ways no departmental handbook could adequately describe. The old instinct sharpened in David. With a series of short, undignified bursts, determined that no mere bereavement leave was going to alter that gravity, he caught up to the group halfway up the slope.

Sweeney was mid-anecdote.

“No, I'm telling you, he said cross-disciplinary intimacy to my face. In a budget meeting. To my actual face.”

“Christ,” David said, sarcasm dripping. “Intimacy and face in the same sentence? Hank, my friend, you should probably stop right there before one of these grad students peels off to notify Human Resources.”

The laugh arrived exactly where he expected it.

Sweeney turned. “Lang. My goodness. And here I thought I had another day. Welcome back, sir.”

“Don't.”

“What? I've barely said two words.”

“Fourteen words, actually, and already I can hear the withering pangs of kindness gathering momentum.”

“You've just buried your father, David.”

“Yes. I have.”

Sweeney waited. David let the silence pull the group firmly back to his control.

Just behind him, Naomi Feld walked with both hands wrapped around a paper coffee cup, carefully watching David over the lid. From History, Barton offered the cautious nod people reserved for funerals and department votes, while a pair of his department's graduate students slowed discreetly in their wake, maintaining the practiced neutrality of those whose funding depended on finding everyone equally amusing.

Finally, David sighed. “Alright, fine. One humane observation. But only one, Sweeney, so… you know, at least try to make it memorable.”

Sweeney considered the assignment. “Hmm,” he feigned, “oh, I’ve got it. You look almost refreshed… all things considered.”

“Well, it's true what they say. Burying a parent can be remarkably restorative. Takes ten years off the face.”

Nobody spoke.

“And five off your lifespan.”

Sweeney looked him over. “Ah, I see. So that’s why you look eighty again.”

Naomi's laugh escaped into her coffee.

“There it is. Oh, and Sweeney, for the record,” he continued, “disciplinary intimacy? This can only land two ways: Title IX or tenure.”

That broke them properly. David's unprotected eyes had begun watering in the cold, however, which was intolerable and, worse, visible.

Barton noticed, but as he moved to volley his own line of attack, David turned directly at him with a glare that said only one thing: Don’t even try.

Barton translated the look instantly and gently fell back into the pack, pretending to stumble on something along the way.

“Good to have you back, Lang,” Sweeney said, roughening the words until neither of them had to acknowledge the sincerity beneath them.

The walk uphill had been salvaged for the day and the usual chatter of what came next began. Half the gossip prepared for the morning had plainly concerned David. He could feel it withdrawing through softened faces and little rearrangements of posture.

He gave them nothing. He had returned; the world could resume its proper cruelties.

* * *

At the lower crossing, where older paving stones met the darker run laid nearer the quad, his body stopped beneath him.

The stillness traveled backward through the group. Naomi checked herself before walking into him, while Sweeney's hand rose automatically to steady her coffee.

“Lang?”

At first, the sensation carried so little urgency that David nearly dismissed it: pressure beneath the sternum followed by a sour inward lurch, the sort of complaint one might attribute to black coffee and a body whose grievances had been accumulating without representation.

Then the sound arrived.

It began below hearing, a grating under pressure pitched low enough to feel structural, as though one of the buried utilities beneath campus had mistaken his rib cage for pipework. The vibration passed through his chest before reaching his ears and stopped his breath midway in.

For one flashing instant, the skin of his face drew tight against the bone. In the next, it loosened into the grey slackness of an age that had not happened yet.

Then, the present closed back around him, cold air entering his lungs.

David looked back to Sweeney and the others. “You heard that.”

Sweeney glanced across the quad. “Heard what, Lang?”

“That tone.”

“Oh, we heard your tone, Professor,” Naomi said.

A nervous laugh passed through the group and died before anyone could pretend it had helped.

David stepped backward. Almost immediately, the pressure beneath his sternum eased. Its retreat drew his attention to the seam in the paving stones, where the darker run began, as though the campus had drawn a line through itself and left it waiting.

Sweeney pressed in toward him, now genuinely concerned and therefore harder to tolerate. “You okay?” he asked under his breath. “You're sweating like it's eighty degrees out here.”

“Yeah, I’m… I’m good. Just a long few days.”

“Of course.” Sweeney's voice softened further. “Really, though. Glad you're back, David. Your dad was a great man. Be proud of a big life, yeah?”

A big life, as though life could be measured in acreage.

“Yeah,” David said. “Thanks, Hank.”

“Oh, and don't let those quantum undergrads keep you down,” Sweeney said, clapping him on the shoulder.

“They'd need a quorum first.”

Sweeney allowed himself to be absorbed into the advancing clot, taking Barton and the graduate students with him as the procession repaired itself and continued uphill.

Naomi remained behind.

“You're not fine,” she said, knowingly.

“No.” David looked beyond her toward the quad, which was thinning into silence as students vanished into classrooms. “But I am late.”

Her gaze moved to the paving seam. “What happened?”

“I don't know.”

The honesty escaped before he could revise it into something more survivable.

“Do you want me to call someone? Celeste, maybe?”

“No. God, no.”

“Then I'll walk in with you.”

He adjusted the satchel. His hands were shaking, an indignity he found more offensive than the nausea. “I'd rather preserve the illusion of competence.”

“David.”

He stood breathing until the tremor beneath his sternum receded into something manageable.

“I know,” he said.

Naomi nodded toward campus. “Okay then. I'll be nearby.”

“How ominous.”

“Good.”

And with that, she turned to go, leaving him beside the seam and the last 50-odd feet of path leading to Feynman Hall.

David let her disappear around the corner before moving, the darker paving stones left conspicuously untouched beneath his feet. Whatever rule governed that particular avoidance could remain unnamed.

Feynman Hall swallowed him into its familiar administrative quiet. A few faces lifted from their screens, registered David, then lowered again without interest. Good. Apparently he still looked like someone arriving exactly when he meant to.

His stomach knew better.

By the time the classroom doors came into view, even the pretense had begun to fray. Thirty feet remained between him and them.

So many proofs, son.

The distance suddenly seemed very exact.

He tried to breathe around the voice.

So much proving for approval’s sake, don’t you think, Davey?

Something beat beneath the band of his watch. Once. Again.

There was no longer much point pretending he hadn’t noticed.

Eight minutes and fifty-three seconds late.

Shit.

* * *

Deep in Sub-level Two, inside a concrete corridor omitted from university floor plans sometime during the Nixon administration, a copper bypass valve finally failed.

Trapped air escaped through the seam in a single thin breath.

The ventilation shaft carried it upward through the buried architecture of East Hill: past biology laboratories and steam conduits, through abandoned storage rooms, behind electrical chases sealed by renovations no one fully remembered. Along the way, machine oil mingled with the mineral dust of old equipment.

By the time the air reached Feynman Hall, radiator heat had worked those buried traces into the scent that used to cling to Elliot Lang's sweaters after his nights in the physics building.

The hallway cooled around David's ankles. The corridor was empty.

Still, for several seconds, he could not rid himself of the certainty that someone had been standing close behind him.

The students beyond the classroom doors were waiting.

David entered the lecture hall with his notes held too tightly in one hand and the intention, unchanged by lateness or nausea, of winning them over.

“Good morning. My apologies for the late start.”
