It was, perhaps, an unfortunate hour to be speaking of coherence.

David Lang had not slept. Three inadvisable energy drinks and four hundred miles of Massachusetts interstate had driven his nervous system past exhaustion and into that particular dizziness that blooms from the gut when sleep has become a spot you stare at on the ceiling.

Twenty minutes earlier, David had abandoned the endeavor altogether, descending to the kitchen to soothe his sour stomach with a pint of milk. The second gulp missed rather badly, sending a pale sheet across the charcoal-marble countertop. David stared at the roll of paper towels on the far side of the room, then at the open bag of mini donuts beside him. Without hesitation, he reached inside, pulled out the first donut, and pressed it into service as a squeegee-dam hybrid.

It was crass, perhaps evidence of a minor defect in character, but the old privileges of bachelorhood returned: the donut was already there, the milk was already there, and introducing a third party seemed inefficient. Conservation of resources took precedence. He popped the milk-heavy donut into his mouth, swiped the remaining spill with three more until the bag was empty, and chewed his way back to baseline.

David had long since refined the act of Suiting into a sequence of near-automatic movements, an applied science resilient even now: socks, slacks, arms into sleeves, the final constriction of collar and tie. The procedure lived so deeply in muscle memory that palette, pattern, and belt arranged themselves without requiring a single deliberate thought.

He had laid out his overcoat across the entryway stool, filled its pockets with the accoutrements of modern life, and was turning toward the front door with ten minutes and thirteen seconds to spare, when he remembered the damn book.

It was a tattered, sorry little thing David had rescued from the front parlor of his parents’ house: an eighteenth-century volume on tuning methods, its cover warped by damp windowsills, its pages yellowed by a hundred New England summers. He had found it crammed between a collection of Wagner vocal scores and a Bartók-Meets-the-BSO LP, as though the book had spent two centuries hiding from the world. One look made two facts clear: no one in their right mind would ever read this thing of their own accord, and Celeste was going to absolutely love it. All that remained was to actually manage to give it to her before something occurred to transform the gift into an apology.

The book was upstairs, buried in his suitcase with all the other gestures too delicate to survive travel. David turned toward the spiral staircase to retrieve it.

Upstairs, he undressed the book from its sweater, tucked it into his left hand, and was turning back toward the stairs when a sound stopped him at the rail.

Laughter.

Voices from the town below reached the apartment all day, rising with traffic and the hourly migrations of students, but this sound refused to settle among them. It lingered, drawing David across the grated bridge to the balcony door.

Cold met him at once, sharp and clean, carrying the whispered scent of shale and frost-bitten pine up from the gorges. The morning had broken open beneath a pale, luminous winter sky as the sun crested East Hill, casting long indigo shadows over the roofs below.

David stepped onto the deck, wind tugging at his shirt as he moved to the far railing. From there he could trace the narrow streets north toward Cornell, the dark scrollwork of the southern gate visible just beyond the first rise. He closed his right hand around the iron and leaned into the cold, the book tucked against his other palm.

Another burst of laughter flew past on the wind. Near one of the old block houses, David spotted a family making their way into the day. A young mother pulled one of her gloves on with her teeth while a little girl attempted to step inside frosted bootprints left by someone much larger. Without looking down, the mother shortened her stride to match.

A bit further on, an awkward boy in a screaming red hat scrambled across crusted grass with the reckless conviction of someone to whom the earth had not yet proved dangerous. His father pursued at the distance the game required: close enough to catch him, far enough to preserve the boy’s belief in his own escape.

As the boy dipped out of sight, something robin's-egg blue flew from the bushes. David mistook it for a bird at first, but the father lunged, bobbling the thing from wrist to wrist before trapping the pair of puffy mittens to his chest. He pumped both fists above his head as though he had just saved the kingdom.

The family laughed, and when the sound reached David again, he laughed right along with them. The sound left him so easily that for an instant he did not recognize it as his own. For two weeks, every sound he made had been trimmed for solemn decorum and consoling company. This one wasn’t. He felt the smile settling across his face, unguarded and foolish, yielding to the strange pleasure of having been caught there.

David took a deep breath of the crisp air, preparing to turn inside, when pages rustled at his feet. He looked down. The book lay open on the balcony deck, its cover curling in the damp. Wind worried the swollen pages, lifting them in loose handfuls and slapping them against the boards.

Yet David could still feel its weight in his left hand.

No. It was gone there too.

Worse, when he moved to pick it up, nothing answered. The intention occurred. He could follow it from the thought of bending through the expected machinery of shoulder, elbow, wrist, fingers. But somewhere between command and consequence, the arrangement ceased to apply. His arm hung visibly from his shoulder, but its position belonged to sight alone. Internally, it remained elsewhere, holding what it had not held for some unknown length of time.

His right hand should have pulled him upright. Instead, the fingers remained locked around the frozen railing. For one terrible interval David stood bent above the fallen book, attached to the balcony by one hand he could not release and another that no longer occupied the same version of events.

Was this a stroke? Would a stroke permit him the diagnostic privilege of asking?

Before he could finish the question, the left arm came rushing back.

Sensation broke through the shoulder and flooded toward his fingers, fierce enough to empty his lungs. Pins of heat drove beneath the nails. His elbow contracted hard against his ribs, and then his hand slammed back into place.

At the same instant, the fingers of his right hand loosened enough for him to tear them from the iron. Skin dragged before releasing. His palm had gone white, the fingers curled inward as though preserving the shape of the railing after the railing was gone.

That degree of cold made no sense. He had been outside for less than a minute.

The book waited at his feet. With deliberate attention, David reached down and picked it up. His fingers closed around the damaged cover and, after a slight delay he could not be certain had occurred, its familiar weight answered.

As he straightened, his cuff slipped back and light flashed across the face of Arthur’s watch.

It had belonged to Elliot first, then Arthur, the narrow band carrying years from his grandfather’s wrist to his father’s and now to his own. Two weeks earlier, beneath the white lights of the morgue, an attendant had unfastened it from Arthur’s wrist and placed it in David’s hand. David had watched the pale circle emerge where the skin had been protected from age and sun. Without intention, on that impossible night, he had put the watch on for safety, signed the required papers, and driven his mother home to a newly empty house. The watch had not left his wrist since.

Three wrists occupying the same small circumference of metal. He stared into its face as though expecting both men to look back through the glass.

David brought the watch nearer. Still ticking. The second hand swept past twelve in perfect working order. But when he looked at the minute hand, something splintered. He had noted the time on his way up the stairs: seven forty-nine and forty-seven seconds. The detour upstairs, the book, the balcony, the boy in the red hat—none of it could have occupied two minutes. Yet the watch stared back unflinchingly: five minutes past eight o'clock.

David had gone from ten minutes early to five minutes late in two minutes flat.

What the fuck.

When he turned back, the pavement outside the house was empty. His eyes followed the family’s route toward the path entering the trees. At first, nothing. Then, at the farthest reach of the branches, a fleck of red appeared between two trunks.

The boy’s hat bobbed once, much farther on than it had any right to be, then vanished.

Then he heard them: Professor Sweeney and the morning cohort. Looking north, David saw they had already passed through the southern entrance and were climbing the first rise without him.

He was late.

David Lang was never late. Never. For forty-six years, this fact had served to guarantee the rest of him.

He dashed inside, snatched his overcoat from the entryway stool, and forced his arms into the sleeves. He grabbed the leather folder containing the day’s lecture notes, shoved it into his satchel with Celeste’s book, and the frosted-green glass door slammed behind him as he rushed into the Ithaca morning.

* * *

Cold needled his eyes as he broke into a sprint through the narrow streets, each block clipping his breath shorter. Porches slick with frost gave way to the steepening order of East Hill. At the southern gate he reduced his pace to something that might, from a distance, resemble purposeful walking.

Usually the climb arranged him. Arguments settled into sequence, lectures found their opening moves, and whatever residue life had left yielded to the old disciplinary pressure of the hill.

This morning, Arthur came with him.

I do not know how you stomach these collegiate conversations.

The words rose in his father's exact cadence, thinned by age and carrying emphasis on stomach, as if his profession had been something David had mistakenly chosen to ingest.

He lengthened his stride.

Always one up on the next man. Always something, son.

Arthur returned as pressure without occasion, a syllable struck harder than the sentence required. There was only the voice, carrying the old exasperation uphill beside him.

Across the lower quad, departments drifted uphill in conversational orbits. At the first trail turn onto the main pathway, he spotted his colleagues. Near the center of the procession, Professor Henry Sweeney had inherited command. Two weeks of David's absence had left a vacuum, and Sweeney, like all soft men with tenure, had expanded obligingly to fill it.

David caught up to the group halfway up the slope.

Sweeney was mid-anecdote: “—said cross-disciplinary intimacy to my face. In a budget meeting. To my actual face.”

“Hank,” David said, his voice dry, “stop before a graduate student notifies Human Resources.”

The laugh arrived where he expected it.

Sweeney turned. “Lang. And here I thought I had another day. Welcome back, sir.”

“Don't.”

“You've just buried your father, David.”

“Yes. I have.”

David let the silence pull the group back under his gravity. Just behind him, Naomi Feld walked with both hands wrapped around a paper coffee cup, watching him over the lid. From History, Barton offered the cautious nod people reserved for funerals and department votes.

“You look almost refreshed,” Sweeney ventured, “all things considered.”

“Burying a parent can be remarkably restorative,” David said. “Takes ten years off the face.”

Nobody spoke.

“And five off your lifespan.”

Sweeney looked him over. “Ah. That’s why you look eighty again.”

Naomi's laugh escaped into her coffee.

“Good to have you back, Lang,” Sweeney said, roughening the words until neither had to acknowledge the sincerity beneath them.

The walk resumed its ordinary momentum. Half the gossip prepared for the morning had plainly concerned David. He could feel it withdrawing through softened faces and small rearrangements of posture.

He gave them nothing. He had returned; the world could resume its proper cruelties.

* * *

At the lower crossing, where older paving stones met the darker run laid nearer the quad, his body stopped beneath him.

The stillness traveled backward through the group. Naomi checked herself before walking into him; Sweeney's hand rose automatically to steady her coffee.

“Lang?”

At first, the sensation carried so little urgency that David nearly dismissed it: pressure beneath the sternum followed by a sour inward lurch, the sort of complaint one might attribute to black coffee and an exhausted body.

Then the sound arrived.

It began below hearing, a grating under pressure pitched low enough to feel structural, as though one of the buried utilities beneath campus had mistaken his rib cage for pipework. The vibration passed through his chest before reaching his ears, stopping his breath midway in.

For one flashing instant, the skin of his face drew tight against the bone. In the next, it loosened into the grey slackness of an age that had not happened yet.

Then the present closed back around him, cold air entering his lungs.

David looked back to Sweeney and the others. “You heard that.”

Sweeney glanced across the quad. “Heard what, Lang?”

“That tone.”

“Oh, we heard your tone, Professor,” Naomi said.

A nervous laugh passed through the group and died.

David stepped backward. Almost immediately, the pressure beneath his sternum eased. Its retreat drew his attention to the seam in the paving stones, where the darker run began, as though the campus had drawn a line through itself and left it waiting.

Sweeney pressed in, lowering his voice. “You okay? You're sweating in twenty-degree air.”

“Just a long few days.”

“Of course. Glad you're back, David. Your dad had a big life.”

A big life, as though life could be measured in acreage.

“Thanks, Hank.”

Sweeney allowed himself to be absorbed into the group, taking Barton and the graduate students with him as the procession continued uphill.

Naomi remained behind.

“You're not fine,” she said.

“No.” David looked beyond her toward the quad, thinning into silence as students vanished into classrooms. “But I am late.”

Her gaze dropped to the paving seam. “What was that?”

“I don't know.” The admission slipped out before he could dress it in irony.

“Do you want me to call Celeste?”

“No. God, no.”

“Then I'll walk in with you.”

“I'd rather preserve the illusion of competence.”

“David.”

He breathed until the tremor beneath his sternum receded into something manageable. “I know,” he said.

Naomi nodded toward campus. “I'll be nearby.”

“How ominous.”

“Good.”

She turned to go, leaving him beside the seam and the last fifty feet of path leading to Feynman Hall.

David let her disappear around the corner before moving, the darker paving stones left untouched beneath his feet. Whatever rule governed that avoidance could remain unnamed.

Feynman Hall swallowed him into its administrative quiet. A few faces lifted from screens, registered David, then lowered again without interest. He still looked like someone arriving when he meant to.

His stomach knew better.

Thirty feet remained between him and the classroom doors.

So many proofs, son.

The distance seemed very exact.

So much proving for approval’s sake, don’t you think, Davey?

Something beat beneath the band of his watch. Once. Again.

Eight minutes and forty-seven seconds late.

Shit.

* * *

Deep in Sub-level Two, inside a concrete corridor omitted from university floor plans sometime during the Nixon administration, a copper bypass valve finally failed.

The rupture discharged along two separate routes.

Trapped air escaped through the seam in a single thin breath. The ventilation shaft carried it upward through the buried architecture of East Hill: past biology laboratories and steam conduits, through abandoned storage rooms, behind electrical chases sealed by renovations no one fully remembered. Along the way, machine oil mingled with the mineral dust of old equipment. By the time the air reached Feynman Hall, radiator heat had worked those buried traces into the scent that used to cling to Elliot Lang's sweaters after his nights in the physics building.

The second path moved faster, through solid matter. The valve seat gave way against eighty pounds of back-pressure, sending a faint mechanical shear wave into the iron casing. The shudder passed from the casing into the anchor bolts and surrounding concrete. Beyond them, the layered rock received what remained of the disturbance, carrying a diminishing tremor into the foundations above. A quiet vibration shivered through water pipes and steel beams long before the breath of air arrived.

The hallway cooled around David's ankles. A faint, prickling tension rose through the soles of his shoes, tightening the roots of his molars before settling into the base of his skull. The corridor was empty.

Still, for several seconds, he could not rid himself of the certainty that someone had been standing close behind him.

The students beyond the classroom doors were waiting.

David entered the lecture hall with his notes held tightly in one hand and the intention, unchanged by lateness or nausea, of winning them over.

“Good morning. My apologies for the late start.”
