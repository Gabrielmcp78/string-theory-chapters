Something had changed in the night.

Celeste couldn’t name it. A misalignment, perhaps, as if the air had shifted half a degree north. Nothing visible announced itself, and like most things in her life, it would remain untrustworthy until caffeine had been applied.

In the shower, she stood beneath the spray, suds tracing slow paths down her temples. With her eyes squeezed shut against the warm water, she found she couldn’t remember if she had actually put the kettle on, or if the memory was just another intention left at the bedside like a shoe dropped at the door—proof of arrival without evidence of follow-through.

Somewhere in the kitchen below her, the kettle began to whistle.

“Shit.”

The sequence mattered. It always did. Now the water was sitting there boiling away on the front burner.

It’s surprisingly difficult to remember to make coffee before you’ve had coffee.

The thought lingered as the water pressure dropped without warning. A sudden rattle in the wall, then freezing water as the shower cycled: lukewarm, cold, briefly hot again.

“Please. No.”

The flow sputtered into a thin, rusty trickle, then cut out entirely.

“Perfect.”

She fumbled for the towel, blinking suds from her lashes. Cold ran a precise line down her spine. Downstairs, the kettle reached a sharp, ear-splitting scream.

“Oh, for God's sake.”

She stumbled out of the claw-foot tub, half-blind, reaching for the towel just as it slipped from the rack and hit the floor.

“Great. Come here, you little shit.”

She snatched it up, wiped her face dry, knotted the fabric, and pulled the wet hair from her neck as she hurried to answer the kettle.

“I’m coming!”

As she barreled out of the bathroom, the loose tile in the last row before the door shifted under her heel. It was just enough to force the slight overcorrection that sent her careening toward the overlook. She caught herself hard on the banister, vertigo flickering, then settled. She took a deep breath. Another half-inch and she would have taken the stairs headfirst.

From the landing, the foyer opened below her in clean rectangles of morning light.

She took the stairs fast, skipping steps, and grabbed the bottom finial to swing around the turn, only for the carved wooden ball to come clean off in her hand.

She stopped in the center of the foyer and stared at it.

“Goddamnit, David!”

The plumbing. The tile. The banister. His hand in everything that broke.

She set the finial on the table. It rolled slightly, then settled.

“It’ll be done next weekend,” she said aloud, the words automatic, mocking.

David had promised that. About the tile. About the pipe under the sink. About the bedroom ceiling fan he had accidentally rewired so that every setting ran in reverse. The first few months had been a dream: weekends full of tools and confidence, the appeal of a scientist turning his mind loose on a house—the same rigor that made his equations elegant, applied now to joist and joinery, as though the structure were a proof waiting to be completed.

Then next weekend became next month. Then next summer. Then, quietly, the following year.

The house had settled into its own habits—the chronic record of a place touched, begun, and then left.

Since David left, the tone of that presence had changed. Anger had softened to grief, but lately, to Celeste’s dry delight, plain irritation had taken up residence. A click in the floorboards where there hadn’t been one before. A loose sash vibrating in the north frame when the wind came up from the gorge.

She read this house better than she read most people. This morning, something was off.

She paused at the corner of the kitchen. The morning light fell at an unfamiliar angle, cold and slightly skewed across the floorboards.

She didn’t say his name. But the thought arrived intact.

What on earth has David done this time?

She stood there a beat longer than necessary. Listening.

No. On to things you actually control.

On to coffee.

She pulled the kettle off the flame just as the water threatened to boil dry. Steam rose hot against her wrist.

Then, beans to grind, grinds to press, boiling water on top. She waited.

Three minutes waiting for the press to brew gave her ample time to catalog everything she had already botched before eight in the morning.

She poured the dark brew into a chipped mug and carried it to her desk.

She pushed a drift of score sheets aside with her forearm and sat. Took a sip.

Scalding. And then the loose grounds hit her teeth. She spat a mouthful of gritty sludge back into the mug with a low, disgusted hiss.

Burnt. Same as yesterday. Gritty. Same as last Saturday. Same as every morning since David moved out to the apartment in town. He couldn’t seal a simple drain, or hit more nails than he missed. But damn, the man made one hell of a good cup of coffee.

“Linn?” called the counter kid, shouting over the shriek of the milk wand. “Two large coffees with room, for Linn?”

Professor Roger Linn wove past the tangle of undergrads at the pickup counter, the strap of his violin case hitched over his shoulder, snagging the cardboard tray with an easy, one-handed swoop. “Thank you very much. Oh—and the scone?”

The barista, a grad student with hair the color of printer toner, blinked. “Oh—sorry about that. Let me just… hey, look at that—last one. Lady luck’s on your side today.”

Linn grinned, tucking the waxed-paper bag under his arm beside his music folder. “We’ll see about that. Thank you.”

He nudged the door open with his elbow, violin case tucked against his ribs and coffees steady in the tray, when his phone vibrated in his coat pocket. He craned his neck, bracing the cardboard, and saw instantly it was a non-negotiable pickup. He managed to thumb the screen.

“Oh, Mother,” he muttered to himself as he wedged his foot to the base of the door, holding it for a shivering postdoc juggling a laptop and a banana. “Hey, Ma.”

“Roger. I read in the journal just last week that potato chips and green salsa make your poop runny. Does that happen to you, son?”

Linn blushed as a nearby undergrad glanced over. “Mom—who’s the doctor here?”

“Neither of us anymore. Don’t make me come out there.”

The signal cut. He stared at the phone, half-relieved, half-guilty. It buzzed again before he’d taken three steps.

“Sorry, Ma. Signal dropped.”

“Roger. Are you eating?” Her Boston accent was thick as chowder, and so was her suspicion.

“Just picking up coffee. And a scone.” He hesitated, irritation and guilt crossing paths.

She snorted. “Scone. It’s all butter and sugar. You still seeing that nutritionist? You tell her about the sodium in that bagel?”

“It’s a scone. Not a bagel.”

“You think your arteries know the difference?”

Linn navigated the icy sidewalk, phone pressed to his ear, violin case bumping against his hip, tray balanced on muscle memory. “I’m walking to rehearsal. That counts as exercise.”

“Not if you slip and crack your head open. You know I worked trauma at General, right?”

He sighed. “You mention it every Christmas.”

“Just checking. Don’t bring me any more data on your ‘theoretical metabolism’ until you’ve had a real salad. Love you, Roger.”

He could hear her smile through the line, and hated how quickly his annoyance dissolved. “Love you too, Ma.”

He hung up, pocketed the phone, and turned the corner toward David’s building with the coffees balanced in the tray.

The door stood ajar.

David’s left eye had swollen nearly shut in the night, blurring whatever crossed his field of vision into grey doubles. Light filtered in unevenly: sharp through the gap in the blinds, diffused across the table where an empty whiskey glass sat in a sticky ring. Dull pain throbbed across his cheekbone, answered below by the hot, shallow ache of cracked ribs. Breath, blink, twitch: his body kept its own ragged tempo.

He was on the floor, half-propped against the sofa, his temple crusted with dried blood where his head rested against the neck of his cello case wedged beside him. His hand crept to his breast pocket, fingers finding the hard, cool lines of the tuning fork. He drew it out. In the grey light, the cerulean residue on its prongs caught the pale air with a faint, metallic sheen. The same blue stain marked his left palm, dry and stubborn against the skin.

Page 117-B clung to his skin. He peeled it away slowly, leaving behind a faint blue echo on the paper. The blackboard triptych on the far wall stood closed, dormant. The entire loft hummed with a new density, the violence of the night having tuned the space to a different fundamental.

Footsteps in the hallway. A pause. The door, already ajar, swung inward with the slow pressure of uncertainty.

“David?”

Linn’s voice, carrying the question carefully, tested the space before he entered. When no response came, he stepped inside, closed the door behind him with the heel of his shoe, and moved down the hallway, balancing the cardboard coffee tray in one hand and hitching his violin case securely against his hip until he reached the opening into the main room.

David turned his head. The movement cost him as ribs protested, vision blurring at the edges. Linn stood to the side of the spiral staircase, the cardboard tray in hand, his expression cycling through recognition: the split lip, the swollen eye, the way David held his left side, the door that hadn’t been locked.

The coffee tray and scone went to the desk without comment. Linn unslung his violin case and canvas tote, setting them beside David’s papers. He crossed the room with quiet purpose, his gaze already taking inventory of the damage.

“Sit up for me,” Linn said.

Linn’s fingers went to David’s face first, tilting his chin toward the window’s light. He traced the orbital bone with quiet concentration, checking for fracture, then leaned in to inspect the split skin above David’s left eyebrow. The blood had dried into a stiff, jagged crust.

“Hold still,” Linn murmured.

He reached into his canvas bag for the first-aid kit—an old tin kept in music department studios for blistered fingertips and chin-rest sores, now pressed into emergency service. He tore open an antiseptic wipe, cleaned the gash with firm, stinging strokes while David hissed through his teeth, and pulled three butterfly strips from the paper sleeve, drawing the edges of the split eyebrow together until the skin held.

“Your ribs?”

“Left side. Two, maybe three.”

Linn nodded, laying out gauze and elastic wrap on the coffee table.

“Shirt off,” Linn said.

David complied slowly, groaning as the flannel dragged over his shoulder. The bruising spread across his left flank in mottled shades of plum and sickly yellow. Linn winced, then set his jaw. He wound the elastic wrap around David’s torso—firm, overlapping passes anchored taut with surgical tape. It was an improvised splint, tight enough to support the thoracic wall when David moved, though Linn knew how little a wrap solved for cracked bone.

When the binding was secure, Linn handed David a clean shirt from the back of a chair and helped him work his arms into the sleeves. Then he popped the lid off one of the paper cups and placed the warm coffee in David’s hand.

“The door,” Linn said finally. “It was open.”

“I know. That was me.”

“And all this?” Linn gestured toward David’s blackened eye and wrapped flank.

“On the street. I don’t know.”

“You should see a doctor, Dave.”

Linn waited, letting the silence settle. When David offered nothing more, Linn didn’t press.

David took a swallow of the coffee. Bitter, lukewarm, gritty. Almost as terrible as Celeste’s French press. A half-smile caught at the corner of his mouth, but when he tried to laugh, his ribs seized in a sharp, breath-stealing spasm. He swallowed hard, letting the ache subside into a dull, manageable throb.

Linn tried to forge a path. “We have rehearsal,” he said, not looking away from the window. “Obviously, you don’t have to play. You can sit. Listen.” A pause. “Or we can cancel.”

“No.” The word came out rougher than intended. David cleared his throat, tried again. “No. I’ll come.”

Linn turned from the window, studying David’s face with the same careful assessment he’d used for the injuries. Whatever he found there seemed to satisfy him. He picked up his own coffee.

“We have forty minutes,” Linn said. “Take your time.”

David stood slowly, testing his balance within the wrap’s constraint. The pain had boundaries now—sharp, corrugated fences along his left flank, but boundaries nonetheless. With gingerly choreographed patience, he worked his arms into the heavy wool coat, moving like a marionette strung with cheap twine. Linn gathered the discarded wrappers, the blood-speckled gauze, and the little yellow tear-packets of antiseptic wipes, packing them into his canvas kit with the practiced efficiency of a man who had spent decades cleaning up after rehearsals.

They were bundled, ready to leave. David looped the strap of his battered leather shoulder bag—laptop and notebook securely tucked inside—over his good right shoulder.

David turned toward the back of the sofa, where his cello case leaned upright against the cushions—bulky, battered, and forty pounds of molded flight-casing that resembled an upright Victorian wardrobe. He took two steps toward it, squared his shoulders, and reached out his right hand.

His body simply refused.

The arm froze an inch above the nylon handle. The arithmetic of levering that massive dead weight across three cracked ribs announced itself with brutal clarity before his fingers even closed.

David didn't move. He stood staring at the handle.

Linn stopped buttoning his coat. He looked at David’s locked stance, looked at the monolith on the sofa, then looked back at David. Not a syllable passed between them; the sheer absurdity of the proposition hung in the quiet loft.

A slow dread crept across Linn’s features.

“No,” Linn whispered.

“Rog,” David said softly.

“David, absolutely not.”

“Look at me, Roger. If I lift that handle, my lung will puncture my spleen.”

Linn closed his eyes, his mouth tightening into a thin line of collegiate martyrdom. Without another word, he unshouldered his own violin case—a narrow, featherweight dart of black composite. He stepped forward and shoved the handle into David’s midsection.

David caught it. It weighed nothing. An empty cigar box.

Linn moved past him to the sofa, bent his knees, took the cello case by the central handle, and hoisted. A muffled grunt escaped his throat as the dead weight dragged his shoulder down six inches.

“Sweet mother of God,” Linn hissed.

“Lift with your legs, maestro,” David said.

“Shut up. Door.”

David turned the knob, and they stepped out directly into the teeth of the Ithaca morning.

Outside, the raw November air came off Cayuga Lake in sheets of atomized slush, sharp with the smell of road salt, wet slate, and diesel exhaust. East Hill rose before them, a sheer, unforgiving wall of grey incline that usually cleared David’s lungs, though today every upward foot was an audit of torn intercostals.

Except for one thing: his right side was floating.

David looked down at the slender case dangling from his fingers. He gave it an experimental, wrist-flicking bounce, as if testing the heft of an empty envelope.

“Jesus, Rog. Is there anything in this?”

“About four hundred grand,” Linn wheezed, wrestling the monolith past the curb.

“No, seriously.”

“I’m serious.”

David gave the case another jaunty little swing. It moved through the sub-zero air with the effortless buoyancy of a baguette. “You could carry this thing in your teeth.”

“Don’t.”

“I’ve been hauling a goddamn refrigerator around for forty years, and you’ve been walking to work with a lunchbox.”

“Careful with the Strad, Dave.”

“A Stradivarius. Of course. A four-hundred-thousand-dollar lunchbox.”

Behind him, Roger was already discovering that a cello case extended four inches wider than his natural stride. On his third step, he clipped the corner of the low retaining wall.

The carbon case gave off a hollow, resonant thwack.

Roger muttered an apology to the fiberglass, twisted his hips sideways to clear the wrought-iron gate, and nearly took off his own left kneecap on the rebound. A sharp crosswind gusting out of Fall Creek Gorge caught the broad side of the case like a spinnaker, pivoting him half a foot into the slush.

David stopped on the pavement, turned, and took in the spectacle.

“Oh, this is wonderful.”

“Shut up,” Roger muttered, hitching the strap as the case banged violently against his outer thigh.

“Honestly, Rog. You look like a man trying to smuggle a wardrobe up a mountain.”

Roger came to an abrupt halt on the sidewalk, panting, the cello case resting upright against his thigh like a shield.

“Want it back?”

“Absolutely not.”

They made another twenty yards before Roger caught the case flush against his shin. The sharp wooden knock rang out like an off-pitch rimshot. David winced in pure acoustic empathy, and then, helplessly, dissolved into laughter.

The laughter was an ambush. It seized beneath his fifth rib, twisted like a barbed wire loop, and drove all the air from his lungs in a sickening heave.

“Ah—fuck—”

David doubled over, catching himself against the damp stone cap of the terrace wall. His left hand locked onto the cold masonry; his right hand plunged instinctively into his heavy overcoat pocket, his fingers wrapping around the cool, rigid steel of the tuning fork.

Roger dropped the cello upright onto the sidewalk with a wet thud.

“Dave?”

“Hang on.” The words were a dry rattle. “Hang on... God...”

The cold steel bit into the flesh of his palm, and beneath the chill came that faint, persistent harmonic humming deep within the prongs—68.48 Hz, stubborn and quiet under the distant whine of traffic. He kept his eyes shut, his forehead inches from the wet stone, and rationed his breath: four beats in, four beats out, letting the interval establish itself until the spasm loosened its claw on his side.

Roger stood two feet away, watching David’s buried hand. He didn’t reach out. He didn’t offer a hand he knew would be batted away. He simply stood between David and the street, a buffer against the wind and passing students, his breath rising in steady grey bursts.

After four breaths, David straightened, testing the tight wrap around his torso.

“You okay?” Roger asked quietly.

“Yeah.”

“Sure?”

David nodded. Then he looked down at the massive off-white case standing sentinel between them on the concrete, reaching nearly to Roger’s chest.

“God, look at that thing. You look like you’re delivering a fucking coffin.”

Roger reached down, gripped the molded handle with a leather-gloved fist, and heaved the sarcophagus back off the pavement.

“Keep walking, asshole.”

And they did.

11.5 The Chapel Rehearsal

Beneath the floor of Sage Chapel lay four hundred million years of silence.

It was the silence of the Ithaca Formation: late Devonian mud, silt, and dead marine life settled in the stagnant basin of an ancient inland sea, compressed into sandstone and fissile black shale. In November, that mineral chill rose directly through the flagstones into the leather soles of your boots. Above it, High-Victorian Gothic hammer-beam trusses of butternut and quarter-sawn white oak met the cold with a century of radiator steam and waxy oak luster.

The open rehearsal was an institutional obligation—a public-relations tithe for the university charter. The congregation huddled in the carved pews was a study in campus demographics: nine hungover undergraduates in baseball caps chasing an extra-credit signature; two emeritus professors of comparative literature in butterscotch corduroy; and a local who had slipped through the north vestibule to escape the gorge wind.

Up near the altar steps, Catherine Rieger and Celeste Armitage were waiting.

Catherine, second violin, sat brisk and razor-edged, her Landolfi resting in her lap while she attacked her nails with an emery board. Beside her, Celeste tuned her viola with sharp, venomous twists of the pegs, glaring at the ambulatory door as if she intended to dismantle it.

The heavy oak door banged inward.

Roger Linn entered first, hauling David’s carbon-fiber cello case beneath his armpit like a rolled-up rug he had just shoplifted, eyes darting left and right.

Three paces behind him shuffled David Lang, his leather shoulder bag slung across his good shoulder, carrying Roger's narrow composite violin case in his right hand.

The Feynman Chair of Theoretical Physics looked less like an endowed luminary and more like a man ejected from a moving vehicle at highway speed. In the gloomy chapel, David wore scratched aviator sunglasses, walking with a lock-kneed waddle. His left brow was hitched together with three butterfly bandages against an eggplant-purple bruise, his heavy coat hanging open over the thick elastic wrap binding his torso.

Celeste’s bow halted dead on the string, the sheep gut choking with an ugly screech.

Her eyes tracked from his shoes to his taped brow, widening in sharp panic before her jaw set hard. A flush rose from the collar of her black sweater. Her knuckles went chalk-white against the viola neck. She looked ready to vault the music stands and shake him; then her chin lifted, and she turned her gaze fixedly toward the altar.

Catherine’s emery board froze in mid-air. “Oh, exquisite,” she murmured in a stage whisper. “Tell me, Roger, did the physics department finally attempt a coup?”

“Just play, Catherine,” Celeste snapped, low and flat.

David set the violin case onto Roger's music stand before easing toward the choir stalls. Roger dragged David’s rehearsal chair deep into the acoustic shadow, angled away from the nave, and unlatched the cello case with rapid clicks.

“Sit,” Roger barked softly. “Down. Don't talk. Don't bow to the public.”

David lowered himself with a whistling hiss of air through his teeth.

“Careful with the bout,” Roger muttered, handing the instrument over.

David drew the cello between his knees. Even through two shirts and the elastic wrap, the varnished maple pressed directly into the cracked ribs, flaring hot with every inhalation.

“Bach,” David croaked behind his aviators. “The quartet transcription of the c-minor Sarabande. Measure thirty-two, into the second reprise. In three.”

“Are you certain, David?” Catherine asked sweetly. “We could do a reading from the Book of Job instead.”

Roger picked up his Stradivarius from the stand, tucked his chin, and tapped the wood with the heel of his bow. “From thirty-two. No vibrato in the opening bars. Let the room speak.”

He raised his Stradivarius, tucked his chin, and suspended his bow.

A sarabande: triple meter, weight suspended upon the second beat—a slow, stately lift of the heel before the foot returns to earth.

Roger’s hand dropped.

Celeste’s viola entered with a dark, reedy C that laid the floorboards; Catherine layered the interior G above it; Roger traced the high, sorrowful melody like an architect drawing an elevation in black ink.

David brought his bow down on the low C string.

The physical sensation was sickening. Even rationing his stroke to the middle six inches of hair, every downbow pulled against the torn intercostals, while the upbow drove the maple bout straight back into the bruised ribs.

Then the building intervened.

High above their heads, suspended beneath the hammerbeams, ran the catwalks and wrought-iron gasolier brackets, coupled through the clerestory walls to the university’s underground steam tunnels and central mechanical plant. Somewhere beneath the campus, the broadband kinetic rumble of the ventilation plant had found and driven the structural resonance mode of the chapel itself: an invariant 68.48 Hz.

It came up through the endpin resting on the flagstones, climbed the steel rod, and bloomed inside the cello’s spruce soundboard.

David’s open C string was tuned to standard orchestral pitch: C2, exactly 65.41 Hz.

The building’s mode vibrated at 68.48 Hz.

The difference was 3.07 Hz.

Two acoustic frequencies separated by three cycles per second produce an audible interference flutter: whump... whump... whump. But coupled mechanically through the steel endpin seated against the bedrock, the standing wave wasn't merely audible; it was somatic. The constructive phase drove a low-frequency hammer directly through the instrument's spruce belly into David's bruised intercostal wall.

David grimaced, his knuckles white on the frog. On stopped notes, his left hand could micro-adjust, rolling his calloused fingertips sharp or flat to evade the room’s standing wave. But the architecture of David’s four-part transcription continually pivoted back to the tonic. In expanding Bach’s solo line into four contrapuntal voices, the reprise demanded the open C.

And an open string cannot be shaded. Its length is fixed between nut and bridge; its mass and tension are immutable. You cannot lean into an open string with your finger to save yourself.

Measure forty-one arrived. The cadence folded inward. Roger and Catherine suspended their voices on a minor seventh, Celeste sank to a low G, and the harmony opened a hollow space that belonged solely to the cello’s fundamental C.

David pulled the bow.

The open C2 roared out at 65.41 Hz. Instantly, the 68.48 Hz pulse from the catwalks slammed into it. The 3.07 Hz beating struck with brutal physical violence—whum... whum... whum—hammering inside David’s skull and vibrating his bruised eye socket until the butterfly bandages puckered.

His diaphragm locked in an involuntary spasm. The bow skittered across the string, bounced twice against the wood of the bridge with an ugly, choked scratch, and lifted into empty air.

The bass voice died.

In the pews, an undergrad blinked.

Before the silence could widen, Roger Linn stepped into the breach. With the quiet, unhesitating instinct of thirty years of chamber music, Roger leaned into his Stradivarius, executing a subtle upward portamento that gathered the melody into his own voice. Celeste read him instantly, dropping her dynamic to a dark pianissimo, digging into her lower string with a rich double-stop that laid a warm floor beneath the missing bass. Catherine answered on the second beat, supplying a deft, muted pizzicato pulse.

To the listeners in the pews, it sounded seamless—a measured, deliberate hush written straight into the score.

They carried the phrase to the double bar and let the final chord evaporate into the cold dust motes.

David sat with his head bowed, the cello trembling against his knees, his breath whistling through his nostrils in shallow, jagged gasps. Cold sweat stung the cut over his eyebrow.

Catherine snapped her violin from her shoulder, her voice dry as salt. “A stunning interpretive choice. We should play all of Bach without a bass line. It saves on heating.”

“David,” Celeste said softly. Her bow was down. She leaned across the gap between their stands, her eyes examining every millimeter of his battered face. “Put the cello down. We're done.”

“No,” David rasped.

He reached his right hand into his coat pocket. His fingers found the cold steel of the tuning fork.

He pulled it out.

The polished steel caught the violet light slanting through the stained-glass prophets above. On the tapered prongs, faint traces of cerulean oxidation still clung to the metal—the chemical ghost of the night before.

Catherine let out a long groan. “Oh, God in heaven. The fork. Roger, please tell him we have department evaluations on Tuesday and I am not spending my Saturday listening to him lecture on non-linear dynamics.”

“Catherine, shut up,” Celeste said softly.

Catherine turned on her. “Celeste, for heaven's sake—”

“Shut up.” Celeste’s eyes never left David’s face. She read the tremor in his split brow, the stubborn, desperate clarity behind his dark lenses. “What did you hear, David?”

David brought the stem of the tuning fork down against the hard maple of his music stand—a firm, decisive strike.

The fork rang. A sharp, brilliant reference pitch cut through the damp chapel air. David leaned forward with care and pressed the brass base of the stem directly onto the maple bridge of his cello.

The instrument resonated, driving the fork’s vibration down through the steel endpin into the stone flagstones.

Hummmmmmmmm.

The vibration went everywhere: through the floor grates, through the wooden pews, through the iron water pipes.

And instantly, the building's 68.48 Hz resonance rose up to meet it.

“Listen,” David whispered, his hoarse voice commanding the stands. “Listen to the beating. Three hertz. We're fighting the room. As long as we force equal temperament, the room is an acoustic sink that tears the chords apart.”

Roger was already leaning in, his Strad held at the ready, his eyes narrowed with clinical curiosity. “The structural mode?”

“The bedrock, the conduits, the iron trusses,” David said. “Sixty-eight point four eight hertz. That's an acoustic C-sharp. We were playing C-natural against it—a minor second, three beats a second tearing into the soundboard. We can't overpower it. We have to couple to it.”

He looked at Celeste. Behind the dark lenses, his eyes were bloodshot, but burning with absolute clarity.

“An F-sharp chord,” David said. “Just intonation. Roger, take the C-sharp in your register—the fourth harmonic on your G string. Celeste, the root on your C string. Catherine, octave above on the D. I'll take the low F-sharp. No open strings. Stop the notes and tune by ear to the room's standing wave. When the beating stops, we lock.”

Roger didn't argue. He raised his Stradivarius, his left hand settling onto the fingerboard. “Cathy. On the root.”

Catherine gave a sharp exhale through her nose, but her bow came up.

Celeste pressed her ear toward the upper bout of her viola. She set her fingers on the string, finding the stopped F-sharp, listening not to an abstract pitch, but to the interference pattern in the stone. Roger laid his bow across the G string, micro-adjusting his stopped C-sharp by a fraction of a millimeter.

The harsh flutter in the room slowed: three hertz... two hertz... one hertz...

Then the beating vanished. Roger's fourth harmonic locked onto the building’s 68.48 Hz fundamental like a key sliding into an oiled mortise lock. The timbers of the chapel seemed to exhale.

They were no longer playing against the room. They were coupled to the mechanical skeleton of the hill: to the shale strata, the steam pipes, the iron catwalks, and the cold gorge air.

David lifted his bow. The movement sent a hot stab through his ribs, but the elastic wrap held.

“Now,” David whispered. “Dead center of the string. No vibrato.”

Roger lifted his scroll. He breathed in through his nose, a sharp intake of cold air that they all took together.

Four bows touched four strings simultaneously.

They sounded the chord.

The chord was an F-sharp, built so its upper fifth locked directly onto the fourth harmonic of the 68.48 Hz building fundamental. It was tuned not as an equal-tempered compromise, but as a pure harmonic ratio against the ambient room tone.

The 3.07 Hz beating stopped dead.

The fluttering pulse of the steam conduits was drawn into immediate phase alignment with the instruments. The room’s rumble didn't vanish—it resolved, coupled directly into the soundboards of the quartet.

The sound seemed to rise from the walls. The stone piers of the chancel hummed against the skin. High in the hammer-beam vaults, the hidden iron tie-rods and bronze chandelier chains began to ring in sympathetic resonance—a pure, high, shimmering overtone series.

The quartet held the chord. Four instruments, four minds, locked onto a single standing wave. In the front row, an architecture student slowly reached out and pressed his bare palm flat against the cold stone of the chancel arch. The masonry was humming.

Roger reached the frog of his bow and lifted his wrist.

The sound cut.

The silence that followed was absolute, dense with the stored energy of the room.

In the pews, nobody moved. The two emeriti sat still, hands resting on their corduroy knees. The undergraduates stared at the chancel. Outside, the wind through Fall Creek Gorge seemed to have dropped away.

Two seconds passed. Three. Five.

From the back corner, one of the literature professors ventured an uncertain, solitary clap—thwack—realized he was alone, and let his hands fall still.

Roger Linn didn't wait for a review.

He stepped forward, caught David’s cello by the neck, and hoisted the case in his other hand.

“Rehearsal concluded,” Roger announced briskly to the pews. “Don't forget to validate your parking.”

David stood. It took him three separate, careful attempts to straighten his knees, but he managed it, his coat hanging straight over the elastic wrap, the aviators hiding his eyes.

Celeste walked over to him. She didn't look at Roger. She didn't look at the audience. She stopped in front of David, her viola case slung over one shoulder, her dark eyes fixed on his face. She smelled of cold rain, winter wool, and black tea.

Her gaze took in the butterfly bandages, the split brow, the exhausted pale sweat at his temples. Her hand moved slightly inside her glove, then settled at her side.

“Coffee?” she asked.

David looked back at her through the dark glass. His ribs were throbbing, but beneath the pain, the ringing in his ears had stopped.

“In an hour, say,” David said. “I have a meeting. We'll talk about the tuning.”

Celeste held his gaze for a long beat.

“One hour,” she said. “Don't be late. And take off those ridiculous glasses.”

She turned and marched toward the side exit, her boots clicking sharply against the encaustic tile. Roger fell in behind her, hauling the cello like a shepherd with a prized ram, leaving David to follow in their wake, one slow, stubborn step at a time.
