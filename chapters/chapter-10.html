The cafe’s acoustic tiles could not quite contain the Wednesday-evening murmur, but Celeste had chosen their corner booth precisely for its distance from the kitchen’s iron percussion. David arrived eight minutes late, a crescent of white chalk still pressed beneath the cuticle of his thumb despite three washings. He slid into the booth, his satchel falling onto the vinyl seat beside him with the heavy, leaden thud of unread monographs.

“Hmmm.” She cast a sideways glance toward her phone, then back to him. “I’m not entirely sure I recognize you coming from this side of the hour. Eight minutes late, huh?”

“I know, Celeste. I’m sorry, I—”

“No, no. I mean, hey, I’m a big fan of the casual arrival. Always have been, David.”

“Yes, I’m well aware, but I promise, it’s not—”

“Now wait. Before you toss it off, let’s take a look. Perhaps it suits you.”

She looked him over in earnest this time.

“I mean, yeah. It could work, if you decided to sleep once in a while. David. Have you slept?”

“Well, yes. Yes, of course I have.”

“When? On Sunday? David, you look like you haven’t slept since Sunday.”

“Monday, thank you very much,” he corrected, settling into the booth. “I slept Monday. For three hours.”

She ordered a carafe of the house pinot noir without asking. Ten years of Wednesday dinners had left them with few decisions to make. When the wine arrived, she swirled it with her right hand, her fingers curled in the light, automatic curve of a viola bow hold.

Though this dinner was no longer required under their dissolved romantic tethering, both had agreed, eighteen months ago, that ten years of Wednesday suppers was too stubborn a habit to be undone by the semantics of a breakup. For David, the ninety Wednesday minutes had long since become the still point of his week: a tide that pulled at him before he knew he was moving. Hours before, he would feel it: a quiet loosening, then a tightening in the chest, the body’s old, wordless rehearsal for joy. He did not decide to go. He simply found himself ready, waiting, turned toward the hour like a shore waiting for the moon.

Of course, he didn’t call it waiting. No, waiting implied hope, and hope was a claim he had long since renounced. What remained was something more respectable: a fixed appointment in the week’s arithmetic, a bracket around which the other hours arranged themselves without complaint. He went because the hour had come. Nothing more.

Certainly, it was not the charms of this particular cafe that drew him every week. The food had declined steadily over the years, to what now rarely rose above short-order plus, and the room, once quiet and intimate, had long since been overrun by undergraduate discovery. Neither mattered in the end. Not to David. A man may sit through a great deal of ordinary time if it is only the vessel for a single face, a single voice, a single possibility he refuses to name.

It was not love. That much was certain. Not out loud anyway. It was order. Plain. Simple. Order had reasons; love had none. That made sense. That was all…

That, and he was quite fond of the chicken piccata.

“You’ve done something with your hair, yes?” David wondered, unsure which version of her he was ever seeing.

“Mmm… no. No, I don’t think so.” But she was happy he noticed anything at all.

“You sure? Didn’t get it cut or—”

“No, David. I’d remember.” She smiled sheepishly.

“Well, you look… you look beautiful.”

She glanced up with a blink and a sideways smile as she tucked a wayward chunk of hair behind her right ear.

David watched her tuck the hair behind her ear, the same small gesture that had mesmerized him since the day they met. For an instant, the years between then and now seemed to give way. There she was, three feet across the table, as she had been through all those years of being his, of being them.

The distance was the same. Three feet. Yet she had never seemed farther away. Distance had become elastic and cruel.

And somewhere inside that impossible measure, between the man he was now and the two of them he could still remember being, he knew that if a Wednesday ever came when she stopped coming, it would end him.

Of course, he couldn’t tell her that. Not anymore. The world between them no longer seemed to answer to speech. Meaning had gone ahead of him into the dark and would not turn back.

He took a sip of water, then straightened the napkin, the menu, himself. Small rituals of order. Stones on a grave.

She watched the faint tremor in his wrist when he reached for the glass. She would not ask about the classroom, nor the conference over the weekend. She had decided that before her coat was off.

“Long day?” she asked.

He nodded without looking up. “They’re all long now.”

She wanted to touch his hand but settled for refolding her napkin instead. Candlelight made him look almost well; if she did not stare too hard, she could believe it.

Celeste would always come. Her friends told her she was weak for it, that she was indulging his pathology, normalizing his self-absorption. Eventually they stopped saying it, or stopped calling her at all. Still, she came every Wednesday. At intervals as random and unassuming as she could manage, while pretending to examine the entrées on offer, she glanced above the flimsy laminate protection of her menu to study the man she loved and lamented in equal measure. She hoped he didn’t notice. He always noticed.

She thought if he noticed, her friends would be vindicated; he would see her as weak. She needed him to believe that leaving had been an act of strength. She needed to believe it herself.

Christ, stop looking. Where’s the damn waiter?

“Should we order?” she said. “Let’s get the waiter.”

Why did she come? She knew why she came: to check in on him, his mind, his demeanor, the shake of his hands. And because she did so very much love the Linguine Caruso. She smiled faintly at the absurdity. David thought it smelled vaguely of dead rat. Perhaps that was why she loved it: because David did not.

“How’s your mom doing?” she asked.

After a delay, he said, “Hmm? What was that?”

“Your mom, David.”

“Shit.”

“David, tell me you’ve spoken to your mother since the funeral. She’s alone in that house for the first time in almost fifty years.”

“No. Yes, I mean, of course I have. It’s not that. The damn lawyers. I keep missing their calls during lectures, and I… I haven’t—” He stopped, his gaze listing out the window toward the quiet street and the glowing blur of lamplight along the wet street below.

“Hey.” Celeste reached for his cheek, coaxing him back without letting him turn the gesture into rescue. “It’s what you hired lawyers for. Right? So you can miss a few calls and everything your father wanted done will still get done.”

“Yeah.” He found a smile for her, nearly usable.

Something in him had to admit, as he sat staring across the table at the only woman he had ever truly needed, and now the only woman he had truly thrown away in spite of it, that the missed calls from the lawyers were purely tactical. If the estate had not yet been settled, the boy inside him could keep believing Dad was in the parlor, smoking a cigar and playing his violin all day. Just as he had been last month, last year, since the day his son David had become a reality in this world.

“I miss him too, David. Best father-in-law a girl never had.”

The waiter arrived. He was not permitted to deliver the specials or suggest a beverage upgrade before Celeste said:

“I’ll have the Caruso.”

“And the piccata for me.”

“Anything to drink with your meals? Another glass of—”

Together, in parallel, they said, “No, thanks. Water’s fine.” They waited while the major-undeclared freshman faded from earshot.

“Da-vid.” Celeste stretched the two syllables into a reprimand only ex-wives, girlfriends and mothers could achieve, a precise instrument of calibrated disappointment.

“Yes. You’re right. I’ll… I’ll keep up on… on all of that.”

“She needs you now more than she ever did. Don’t let your work do that thing you always let it do.”

“I know. I know. I’ll call her tomorrow. She’s fine. The old battle-ax is fine.”

“You and I both know that’s not true. The same way we both know you are not fine.” A pause. “And that’s… okay.”

“Yeah. I know.”

The conversation eased into the familiar grooves of their shared geography: department meetings, Ithaca gossip, the students they held in common. Big school, small town. Fine lines everywhere: invisible, permeable, at least until crossed and then: unflinching. Unforgivable.

Somewhere between the entrées cooling and the second refusal of dessert, David reached into the satchel. His fingers found the plastic bag and the book inside it. Twenty years of evidence suggested there was a fairly reliable interval between a pleasant evening with Celeste and David finding some new way to damage it. He had never successfully calculated the mean, but he knew enough to recognize when the clock was running. Suddenly, giving her the book seemed less sentimental than prudent.

“Hey, I brought you something.”

She looked at the torn grocery bag and smirked. “What, the other half of Monday’s turkey sandwich? What is this.” She laughed as she took the package, her fingers tearing gently at the plastic where the seam had already failed. Beneath, lay the narrow volume. The tuning manuscript. It was bound in dark leather gone gray at the hinges, its spine cracked, the gilt title reduced to a few stubborn islands of light. She read the spine and went entirely still.

“David.”

“I rescued it from the parlor,” he said. “It was getting choked by Wagner and Bartok and…”

“Whoa,” Celeste balked. “Tough neighbors.”

“Yeah. Well, I just… I knew it belonged to you.”

Her fingers moved over the warped cover with a reverence that, to David, looked like magic. Just as she moved to open the cover, something stopped her.

“There’s no way I’m opening two-hundred-year-old pages over marinara. This deserves a table with a lamp and some dignity of surroundings.”

“Well, that can be arranged.”

She held the book against her chest. Then, as though the two objects had been waiting for one another, she reached into her viola case and brought out several folded pages.

“So, I was going to ambush you after the piccata,” she said. “Pythagorean, with modern alterations.”

Her free hand shaped an interval in the air between them.

“Elegant in a purely musical sense. I thought we might do some stringed investigation with it later. You know, just music. Force quantum mechanics to sit in the corner like a good dog.”

David looked at the charts, then at the book against her chest. Something in the arithmetic of the evening resolved without his permission. Two people had walked into a mediocre restaurant carrying, from opposite directions, the same idea.

“Professor Armitage, what would you say to joining me for a little chapel break-in?”

“Why, Professor Lang,” Celeste said, “I thought you’d never ask.”

He signaled for the check and, for once in his adult life, did not calculate the tip with unnecessary precision.

At the door he held her viola case while she wound her scarf.

She let him. And while both of them noticed, neither said a word.

They stopped at the apartment for the cello, and Celeste waited in the car with the book on her lap, not opening it. She rested both hands upon the cover, as if contact alone might begin whatever permission the thing required.

“You understand this is technically a crime,” David said when she turned up East Hill.

“I think God will understand, don’t you?”

“Sure, but I very much doubt he’d post bail for us.”

“Yeah. What a cheapskate.”

“I dare you to say that once we’re inside.”

The side entrance accepted her key with a strange familiarity.

Celeste looked back, a grin of success across her face. “They never digitized this back door lock for some reason. Had this key since the day you dragged me to this frozen wasteland.”

Somewhere beyond the lower corridor, a practice room leaked four bars of trumpet, stopped, started again, and found a more interesting way to fail.

The chapel was nearly dark when she found the old rehearsal lamp near the front and switched it on. Its cone of light fell across the long table, the music weights, the torn grocery bag, and the book she still had not opened. The room held the final residue of the day: a flute line too high to have survived, a door closing somewhere in the lofts, the low animal patience of the timber overhead.

As David drew out the cello’s endpin, the small sound of metal meeting floor rose into the room and returned faintly from somewhere above them.

“Don’t you dare, Lang,” Celeste said.

“I have said nothing.”

“No, but you were about to become unbearably mathmaticious, and you know it.”

“I was about to admire the room, thank you.”

“Yes, with equations.”

“Maybe I was. So what? Some rooms invite such things, you know.”

“Well, this one has suffered enough, and so has the room.”

“Oh, that’s very funny, Ms. Armitage. Very funny. And for the record, mathmaticious is not a word.”

“It is if I say it is, Lang.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

She glanced back at him with a wink in her eye as she searched for supplies.

The book’s pages had swollen and contracted through enough seasons that the volume would no longer lie flat without persuasion. She retrieved two felt-covered music weights from the vestibule beyond the piano, settled them across the corners, and began examining the opening chapter.

The printed text began with monochord lengths, divisions of the octave, and an author untouched by the possibility that anyone might disagree. Someone had disagreed almost immediately.

“David, look. There’s writing in it.” She pulled him beside her by the sleeve.

A brown line of ink ran beside the second paragraph, ending in a cramped correction whose final word disappeared into the binding. Two pages on, a darker hand entered, sharper, reducing the author’s claim to three ratios and a question mark. Farther along, a pencil appeared only beside passages on beating, sympathetic vibration, and the behavior of low strings in closed rooms. Then, above an empty stretch of margin, five notes in blue-black ink, the last scratched out.

“I think… yes. I think there are four distinct hands here,” she said.

“Five.” He pointed to a faint gray notation curving around the oldest ink without touching it: slighter pressure, another slope, a hand with its own idea of how much space it was entitled to occupy.

Celeste followed it through the margin, then turned back two pages, forward three, checking something.

“No one writes over anyone else.”

“Seems unusually civilized.”

“Yeah, maybe. But I don’t think it’s manners.” Her finger rested where the gray hand bent itself around the brown. “They all seem to be leaving room for…”

“For what?”

“I don’t know yet.”

They read until the hands became almost social: one defending the pure fifth at any cost, one restoring the third and mourning what it displaced, the pencil counting in irregular clusters, patient as a man tapping a table while he listened for a thing he could not yet name.

Celeste spread her charts beside the open book. David asked what the interval had done to deserve the word cowardice circled above it. She said it had compromised. He was reaching for the pencil on the music desk before he had decided anything, and her hand closed over his around it.

“David Lang, what did I say about mathmaticianing?”

“That it too is not a word.”

“Don’t.”

“What? It’s not.”

“Just don’t.”

“All right. All right. I’m just checking a single ratio.”

“Oh, really. How about you’re just putting the dog back on the furniture.”

“It’s just a little arithmetic, Cele. There are no animals involved here.”

She smiled as he said her name. “That is what you say just before spacetime gets involved.” She took the pencil from him and tucked it, along with her hair, behind her right ear.

She looked back and caught David staring at her with a ridiculous grin. For a few moments, neither turned from the other’s eyes.

David’s phone broke the moment, its buzzing and screen light flooding the chapel. His mother’s name filled the display. It stopped, waited just long enough to allow the room to settle, and started again.

“She knows you can see her calling, David,” Celeste said.

“She also knows it’s nearly ten.”

“Oh, please. Your mother has never once regarded time as an enforceable structure. It’s one of her—”

“Worst qualities, I know.”

“I was going to say best, actually.”

David looked at her. “The two of you were always far too simpatico for my liking,” he said. “Maddening, really.”

Celeste’s face lit as she again moved the hair from her eyes. “That may be the nicest thing you have ever said to me, David Lang.”

When the phone rang a third time, she said, “I see. This is exactly what you do to me too, isn’t it? You turn anyone who actually wants to talk to you into a nuisance.”

“Oh, I do not.”

“Answer your poor mother.”

“Fine. Fine.” He swiped at the screen with mock effort and an actual smile. “Hello, Mother.”

Cassandra arrived in the chapel the way she arrived everywhere: mid-sentence, at concert volume, speakerphone utterly unnecessary.

“—and if she doesn’t like it, you are to bring it straight home. I have a list of people who would commit crimes for anything of Lenny’s. Well, crimes is unkind. Petty moral collapses, perhaps, but still. You cannot leave that sort of thing lying around among people with advanced degrees and no moral supervision.”

“Mother, you are in the middle of someone else’s conversation again.”

“Well, David, honey, whose fault is that? I called three times. If you had picked up the first time, like a good son, you would know exactly what I have been talking about for the last three minutes.”

Celeste’s laugh escaped before she could stifle it, and the chapel made the joy of it larger than either of them expected.

“What was that?” Cassandra asked.

“Nothing, Mom.”

“Is Celeste with you?”

“No, Mom.”

“Celeste, honey, are you there?”

“Hey, Cassy,” Celeste said. “I’m here.”

“Brave girl, spending time with my son at this hour.”

“Stupid is probably—”

“All right,” David said. “How about we not make this beat-up-on-David hour.”

“Forgive my boy, Celeste. He got his moodiness from his father, you know.”

A pause settled over the line. The knowledge of why belonged to all three without anyone having to touch it.

“Well,” Cassandra said, more quietly. “I guess I stepped into that one, didn’t I?”

“What can we do for you, Mother?”

“What can you do for me? You can tell me how it went. Did she like it? Did she love it? Celeste, honey, do you love it?”

“Love what, Mom?”

“The book, Davy. The old tuning thing from the parlor, unless you’ve started distributing Lang-family treasures to every beautiful woman you run into on the street. I think you know what book.”

Celeste looked at David. “Wait. She knows?”

“What? No,” David said.

“It was my idea, dear,” Cassandra said.

“Mother, it was not your idea.”

A small, satisfied silence crossed the line before David said, “You said, ‘Go in the parlor and see if there’s anything you want to take with you,’ then mentioned Wagner scores.”

“No. I said, ‘Wonderful Wagner scores.’”

“That does not make it your idea. Sorry.”

“Oh, really, son?”

“Yes, Mother. Really.”

“Honey, what did I say after that?”

David looked at the open book and felt the trap close with embarrassing elegance.

“You said there were some wonderful Wagner scores in the cabinet by the—”

“And where did you find the book?”

Celeste’s laugh escaped before she could stifle it. “Cassy, you are a genius,” she said.

“No,” Cassandra said. “I have simply spent forty-seven years trying to get this boy to do what he needs to do before he notices he is doing it. The idea came from me.”

Celeste lifted the volume as if Cassandra’s victory had altered its weight, and the old paper jacket slipped free and folded onto the floor. When she bent for it, she stopped.

“David.”

Beneath a faded dealer’s stamp, a small engraved plate caught the lamp:

FROM THE LIBRARY OF LEONARD BERNSTEIN

David reached for the jacket. “Mother,” he said carefully, “where did this book come from?”

“The parlor, silly.”

“Before the parlor, silly.”

“Oh. The Bernstein kids sent boxes after Lenny died. Scores, books, somebody’s hat. I never did find out whose. Your father alphabetized all of it and complained for a month, which is how I knew he was pleased.”

Celeste had gone quiet, reading the bookplate’s edges the way she read margins.

“I mean honestly, what on earth would I have done with a tuning book?” Cassandra continued. “My job was to sing the right note when Lenny wanted the right note and make the wrong note beautiful when he wanted the wrong one. He always seemed perfectly satisfied. No book required.” A pause, softer and more dangerous. “Besides. I knew who would eventually open it.”

David was still assembling a response when his mother said, in exactly the same tone:

“David, honey, did you tell her about the cello?”

The room went still around David’s single warning: “Mom.”

“What?”

“No.”

“No, you didn’t, or no, you won’t?”

“Cassandra,” Celeste said, and there was enough care in her voice to make the care frightening, “what about the cello?”

David reached for the phone. Celeste’s hand arrived first and lay flat over it, not pressing anything, simply present.

“If anybody on this earth would understand it,” Cassandra said, “it’s the woman standing next to you.”

Celeste kept her eyes on him. “Understand what?”

He set down the bow he had not realized he was holding. “There was a repair label inside it,” he said. “From Buffalo.”

“What kind of label?”

“A repair strip.” Then, because each fragment purchased a moment in which he did not have to produce the next: “Old. Eighty-five. It reads E.G. for E.L. BFL 11/85. Elliot Lang was my grandfather. I don’t know what was done.”

She absorbed this, her attention moving once toward the cello and back.

“How long have you known?”

“Four days.”

Her face stayed quiet, which was worse. “And you found the shop by accident.”

“I found the address by accident.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

He looked at her. “No,” he said. “It wasn’t.”

Cassandra let the silence hold just long enough to prove she had heard it.

“David,” she said, “either you tell her these things when they happen, or I will. You are not the gatekeeper between me and your ex—” A pause; the audible redrafting of an entire domestic constitution. “Better half, or whatever it is we are calling you tonight, dear.”

A classically Cassy giggle spilled across the line. “Well,” she said, “I think my work is done here. Good night, my darlings. And, Celeste, enjoy the book. I knew you’d give it a good home.”

The line clicked quiet before either of them could answer, which was, they both understood, the point. Celeste did not move toward him.

For a moment the chapel seemed to keep the call for itself: the open book under the lamp, the cello waiting on its side, the phone gone dark between them as if it had never been anything but an object.

“You weren’t going to tell me,” she said.

“Sure, I was.”

“Sure you were.”

“No, Celeste… Jesus. Yes, I was…”

“When?”

Every answer available to him became another form of delay, and to his credit he attempted none of them. Celeste held him there another moment. Then her attention shifted to the cello.

“Show me.”

“Wait, so you’re not pissed at me?”

“Oh, I’m pissed. But I’m a hell of a lot more interested in this than I am in you.”

He angled the treble f-hole beneath the rehearsal lamp and showed her: the faded strip deep against the inner rib, the ebony around the endpin fractionally newer than the wear surrounding it. Celeste examined both with her hands clasped behind her back, the way she had taught a generation of students to look at instruments that did not belong to them, and he understood that the posture was addressed to him.

When she straightened, she said, “The cello gets a vote.”

“In what?”

“The tuning.”

“That is not how physics works.”

“David Lang, do you really wanna be trying to tell me how physics works? Think carefully about that before you…”

“No. No, of course not, Cele…”

She tucked the viola beneath her chin, tested the open C, and let the note settle. The silence that entered afterward had weight.

“What?” he asked. “What is it?”

“Nothing,” she said, close to breaking.

“Celeste—”

A moment’s silence flattened out between them before Celeste jumped back in.

“The fact that your mother trusts me with what you hide from me. The fact that you hide it anyway. The fact that this instrument has had a secret longer than either of us has been alive.” She drew the C again, quieter. “And the fact that if we stand here discussing your talent for concealment, you will become unbearably precise and I will become mean.”

“I would never accuse you of imprecision.”

“That is because you value your life.”

He almost smiled, but the attempt did not survive.

She lifted her bow. “Open C.”

David sat down, brought the cello’s neck to his shoulder, and felt the familiar, heavy wood press against his chest. He turned the peg of his C-string. It resisted, cold wood sticking in the box, and he had to press the palm of his hand hard into the scroll to force it to hold. The peg clicked twice, vibrating up his forearm, his thumb aching from the counter-pressure.

When he drew the bow, the first note was rough, a raw friction that shook through his collarbone. Celeste entered an octave above him on her open C, then swept cleanly to the open G: a pure, resonant fifth suspended above his bass. Her viola’s tone was bright, almost clinical, cutting through the damp chill of the stone room.

Celeste listened with her whole body, head inclined, bow arm suspended, the line of her throat lengthening above the viola as though the room had asked a question only she could hear. David watched too long, then looked away too late.

“Again,” she said.

He gave her the note again. She entered beneath it, correcting by less than speech could have named, and his hand found the adjustment before his mind reached it.

The sound changed (closer rather than louder), and they adjusted the fifth between them, then adjusted it again, following her chart until their breathing began to find the same interval. She leaned into the upper edge of the pitch; he answered from below. When she moved, he knew where she was going a fraction before she arrived, and when he resisted, she smiled without looking at him, because resistance was also information and she had always known how to use it.

The two instruments began to occupy the room more completely, the floor accepting the sound before the walls did. Anger remained. So did the four days, the hidden mark, the phone call still cooling upon the long table. But beneath all of it their bodies remembered a fluency their words had lost: her bow asking, his hand answering; her shoulder opening the phrase, his bass gathering it; each correction a pressure passed between them and returned transformed.

For a few measures, they were perfect together, the danger inside the perfection part of its beauty.

Celeste lowered one string by less than he could reliably measure. “Again.”

They played, and the interval opened like a door neither of them had meant to touch. David felt it first in the endpin, a faint change in resistance where the cello met the floor, then in the cup of his left hand as the neck answered against his thumb. Celeste heard it before he could name it. He knew because her bow slowed without losing the line.

“There,” she said.

“I know.”

“No, you don’t. Listen.”

He did, following her through the marginal corrections one by one, accepting none as authority. A pure fifth. A narrowed third. The disputed comma. The five-note figure in blue-black ink, its ending scratched away by someone dead long enough to have become handwriting.

Celeste supplied an ending; David rejected it. She played it again quietly, with a change in the weight of her arm that made refusal feel suddenly ill-mannered. He found a bass beneath her answer, four measures that returned without quite repeating.

The bass returned until repetition became ground, each cycle altered by the pressure of what had preceded it. They passed the figure between them, widening and narrowing its interior space, until the room held the shape of a passacaglia neither had chosen and both recognized.

Near eleven she missed an entrance because he altered the bass beneath her.

“That is not what you played before.”

“Well, it's what I meant to play before, so...”

“So… You don’t get to revise retroactively.”

“Excuse me, I’m a physicist, of course I do. ”

“That is not the exoneration you think it is, mister.”

“Hey, I know it’s been a while since you put on a lab coat, but um… physics doesn’t really exist without retroactivity.”

“Infuriating man.” And the grin was no longer something she could stifle.

“That’s right. I am. Thank you.”

She struck the passage again, hard enough to make him chase her, and he did. The argument crossed three keys, lost all contact with Pythagoras, and acquired instead the dangerous fluency of two people who knew exactly how far the other could be pushed. Her bow found the edge of his timing and leaned there. His bass moved under her, refusing to give way until the refusal itself became support. She turned, he followed; he held, she answered; neither led for more than a breath before the other changed the terms.

They arrived together on an F neither had intended and could not hold for laughing. David lowered his bow, but something continued: a low response inside the cello, thinner than the note they had played, lasting perhaps two seconds before it slipped beneath hearing.

Celeste’s laughter faded. “Again,” she said.

They tried and got nothing. He reset the endpin, checked the bridge, touched the string lightly as though the instrument might be withholding out of offense.

“Again.”

Again, nothing, only the room, the ticking heat in the lower pipes, and the chapel holding its opinion.

Celeste looked toward the open book. He followed her gaze. Neither said anything, and both noticed the other not saying it.

They played until nearly midnight, pursuing the sound long after the cello had returned to the ordinary loyalties of wood, gut, and varnish. Each attempt clarified only the distance between repetition and return, and still they played.

What Celeste had called leaving room for another hand now appeared as a measurable property: each system status retained prior constraint while admitting later influence. The response did not erase what came before. It incorporated it. The apparent noise in the data was evidence of hidden path.

Then the cello pulsed from inside the car, entering the lot first as pressure: a low vibration moving through the rear seat, the door, the frozen air, the soles of their shoes.

Both men looked at the car. David, curled on his side between the vehicles, kept his eyes closed while blood ran from his nose to the pavement.

“What the fuck was that?” the second man said.

When the first man opened the rear door, David moved before he knew he was moving. He caught the man’s sleeve.

The second struck him behind the ear, and the world folded inward. When he came back, the watch was free.

The second man held it in one hand, strap broken, crystal catching the lot light. David lunged. His fingers closed on air. The man jerked back, and the startled watch flew from his grip in a bright, spinning arc over the curb, past the salt bin, and into the winter shrubs beside the loading dock.

“Motherfucker,” the second man said.

He took two steps after it, then stopped. Someone was coming. David heard only the storm and the blood inside his own head, but the men heard more, or felt more.

The first straightened, his face turned toward the covered dock. The second backed away from the shrubs, suddenly empty-handed, suddenly finished with the watch, the car, the cello, the whole afternoon.

A small, dark shape moved behind the veil of snow near the loading bay, unhurried.

“Leave it,” the first man said.

“What?”

“Leave all of it.”

The second looked once at the cello case, once toward the figure approaching through the storm, and dropped the keys near the driver’s door.

That was the insult of it. Their bodies carried no panic large enough to honor what they had done, only a quick animal revision of risk.

Cash, phone, wallet: enough. The watch could remain abandoned in the shrubs, the cello breathing in the car, and David on the ground with his ruined papers while the storm did its patient work.

He tried to crawl toward the bushes, but the body declined. He tried again because the watch was there, because Arthur was there, because the thieves had failed and failure required recovery before the universe changed its mind.

His palm dragged through slush and his ribs lit white. The shrubs were only a few yards away and might as well have been Buffalo.

“No,” he said.

The word fogged once in front of him and disappeared as boots stopped beside him.

VII. DEPOSITIO / SEPULTURA | You Will Freeze Out Here

“You will freeze out here if you stay like this.”

The voice was close, low, and practical, edged by an accent he could not place because pain had made geography unreliable.

David lifted his head to find a woman standing beside the open rear door, short, wrapped in dark wool, her face lined without softness, her pale eyes examining him as if injury were an instrument and he had been badly handled by amateurs.

In one gloved hand, she held Arthur’s watch, black with grime and meltwater, its crystal cracked and the strap hanging torn from the case.

“Missing something?”

“Where did you—”

“Find it? Does it matter, really? It’s here now, back where it belongs.”

She crouched and pressed it into his hand, closing his fingers around it before he could ask the first impossible question.

“Eyes.”

“What?”

“Yours.”

She took his chin and turned his face toward the lamplight. Her thumb lifted one eyelid, then the other. The examination was brisk, intimate, impersonal.

He might have been a violin opened upon a workbench. “Concussion?” he asked.

“Among other indignities.”

“Are you a doctor?”

“Yes.”

“Of what?”

The old woman smiled, wryly, as if the answer would be amusing to David in particular, and therefore something she was unwilling to supply.

She took his cut hand next. The edge of the watch crystal had opened his palm; blood followed the lines already there, turning his hand into a small red map.

She looked from the cut to the watch, then toward the cello case. As she did, she whispered something, and acknowledgement that David, in his battered state, did not hear.

Hello old friend.

“What? What was that?”

“The instrument is whole.”

“No. That’s not what you said.”

“Wasn’t it?”

“No. I mean… I don’t,” and then language began to catch up to David. “Wait, it’s whole? What do you mean? How do you know?”

“Because it is still arguing.”

The cello pulsed again. The vibration moved through the curb and into the soles of his shoes, too low for ordinary hearing, too organized to dismiss as aftershock. David felt it gather against his ribs, where the boot had landed, and his breath caught before pain could claim the movement.

The woman reached into her coat and withdrew a small tuning fork of dark metal, its prongs beveled to an impossible thinness. She held it between them without striking it, and the fork began to tremble in her grip.

David blinked against the snow and blood, attempting to locate one rational surface upon which the exchange could rest.

“What is that?”

“A question,” she said.

“Yes, of course it’s a question. What is that?”

“No,” she said, gesturing to the object, “This is a question.”

“Lady, I hate to break this to you, but that is a tuning fork.”

“Yes.”

“Tuning forks do not ask questions.”

“Are you so very certain of that?”

“Yes, of course–”

And a strange, pressing stare stopped David mid-declaration, forcing him somehow to recant the certainty.

“Well… I mean… fine. Christ. Fine. Your little tuning fork is a question. Whatever.”

“Good then. Most people only use them to demand answers.”

“Yeah, like what’s A 4.”

She scoffed, a dry little sound that made the snow and blood and cracked watch feel like insufficient evidence for his intelligence. “Is that all you can hear? I expected more.”

“We humans are such predictable fools,” she said. “Everything we do not yet understand falls at once into one bucket, one label of permitted stupidity. Magic. Everything you have ever studied was once called magic by someone too frightened or too tired to continue looking. Be smarter. See further. Hear more.”

The cello answered, a note forming inside the case, low and narrow, threading itself through the fork without touching it. In the meltwater beneath the car, color gathered where oil should have been: a faint magenta sheen, unstable as a bruise beneath glass.

David tried to sit higher and failed, so the woman put one hand against his chest and pressed him back to the car.

“Stay.”

“I am having an unusually poor day with instructions.”

“You are having an unusually poor day because you keep mistaking instruction for humiliation.”

“That’s a popular theory lately.”

“Mounting evidence all around.”

The fork trembled harder as the cut in his palm brightened where blood met metal.

David looked down at his hand. “What did you just do?”

“Reminded it.”

“Of what?”

“Of who you are. Of how it should listen.”

“Hands don’t listen.”

“No. Yours have a nasty habit of lecturing first.”

He laughed once, and the laugh became pain. The woman watched without pity or cruelty, a delineation David could not parse.

The cello gave three faint pulses (low, higher, lower again), the beginning of a figure he almost knew and could not catch.

She looked toward the case, then toward the storm beyond the faculty lot.

“You should leave before they return with braver friends.”

“Who were they?”

“Men who understand enough to be dangerous and too little to be useful.”

“That narrows nothing.”

“It narrows more than you think.”

She released his hand and stepped back. The tuning fork had gone still.

“Wait,” David said. “Who are you?”

She turned and began walking toward the service road with the same careful efficiency with which she had arrived, each step finding purchase before the next trusted the snow.

Behind him, the cello sounded once: a single low note, the first interval of the figure, followed by silence. David looked toward the case.

“By the way,” the woman called.

He turned back to find her stopped beneath the edge of the loading dock, one hand resting lightly upon the yellow salt bin, her face half taken by snow.

“You should really have that watch cleaned,” she said. “So filthy you cannot even read the inscription.”

He looked down at the watch in his hand. The metal back was blackened with years of skin, polish, and whatever weather the parking lot had added to the inheritance.

“How did you know there was an inscrip—”

The service road stood empty. David stared at the place where she had been while snow moved through the loading-dock light.

The paper cup upon the salt bin had tipped over and rolled softly against the plastic lid.

He looked back to the watch. The grime resisted at first. He rubbed with his thumb, then with the edge of his sleeve, then harder, ignoring the sting in his palm as the old film broke and lifted.

Letters appeared by degrees, shallow and precise, engraved into the back where the watch had rested against three generations of pulse.

In Time; E.G.

VIII. RELIQUIAE | Up From the Depths

The cello answered once more from inside the case, only the first interval, but enough to make David turn.

Nothing remained of the magenta thread. The men were gone. So was the woman. Snow was already softening their footprints.

That troubled him more than if she had vanished cleanly.

Arthur’s watch remained, cracked and cold against his wrist, its strap torn where the buckle had fought them. He remembered a hand at his arm, another at the clasp, the heel coming down near his ribs; remembered the stupid, animal twist of his body around the case, as if Arthur had been there in the snow with him and the only instruction left between them was to hold the instrument, hold the line.

His phone and wallet were gone. The keys lay beside the open car door, already dimming beneath white. He picked them up because leaving them there was a stupidity he could still identify.

He still had the cello, but the thought did not become relief; it became pressure.

David lifted the case from the seat.

Pain drove through his side so completely that the lot emptied of everything else. He bent over the instrument, unable to lower it without allowing the weight to pull through his ribs. For several seconds he remained half standing, half folded, breath coming in ugly pieces.

When the spasm released, he brought the strap over his good shoulder and gathered what remained of the satchel from the floor behind the driver’s seat: pens, receipts, a faculty parking permit, and the marked working set of the manifold paper, wet and trampled.

Rune’s red circles had widened in the meltwater until each objection became a pale wound in the page.

One sheet had folded beneath a boot print. David lifted it by the corner.

The rejected geometry occupied the center: the attractor Rune had dismantled. Two narrow trails crossed the page; one followed the curve, while the other left it.

The second path was not ink. It had no written edge, no graphite or toner to explain it. The paper had simply dried differently along that line, its fibers compressed into a shallow channel running from the false attractor toward the blank margin.

He looked at it until the cold got through his knees, then put the page away.

Driving was out of the question. The thought arrived not as prudence but as insult. He could see the driver’s seat, the smeared glass, the keys cold inside his hand. He could get home, clean up, call no one, explain nothing.

He took one step toward the front of the car and nearly went down; the cello struck his thigh, and the breath left him.

For an instant he saw the steering wheel not as an option but as an instrument of public death: his blood upon the airbag, his car folded around a lamppost, some undergraduate finding him because he had preferred dignity to gravity.

“All right,” he said, the words sounding childish inside the empty lot.

The tuning fork lay across his palm, narrow and cold, the stem darkened where his blood had dried along it. Where it had rested against the crescent cut, a blue line had appeared.

It was not ink, not exactly a bruise: cerulean at the edges, darkening toward the center as though the color had been pressed into him from beneath the skin.

Excellent. New symptom. Efficient evening.

He should have put it away.

Instead he held it against the cello case, metal to cracked vinyl, cold to cold.

No sound came at first. Then the case answered with a pressure so low it seemed less like vibration than fit. It entered through his palm, crossed the wrist, and found the bruise beneath his ribs.

David shut his eyes. When he opened them, the lot tilted.

“Home,” he said.

The cello answered, not loudly or helpfully, but with one interval held inside the case, and beneath it the fork gave off a thread of resonance too high for hearing. David felt it in his teeth, in the hinge of his jaw, in the bruised place behind the sternum.

He began to walk.

The first stretch was ridiculous. He had no better word for it. A tenured professor, beaten in a parking lot, carrying a cello that should have been stolen and was instead answering from inside its case, with a paper half dissolved in his bag and a tuning fork biting cold into his palm.

He almost laughed, but the attempt broke against his lip, and then the pain came back hard enough to take the joke with it.

He stopped beside the service road and bent over the case.

This is insane.

The cello became weight again, dead wood and string, thirty pounds of inheritance dragging at his shoulder while the hill below campus fell away through snow and sodium light.

David put one hand upon the case.

“No,” he said.

It was unclear whether he meant the instrument, his body, or the universe at large. The distinction did not seem useful. He tightened his grip around the fork until the blue line in his palm darkened.

“You get home.”

The fork answered first, not with sound, but alignment.

The pain did not vanish. Nothing as generous as that. It narrowed. His breath, which had been arriving in panicked fragments, found a count without becoming comfortable. The cello case moved against his leg at the end of each measure, not lighter, never lighter, but less opposed to him.

He descended as snow took the hill from him in sections. The faculty lot disappeared first, then the service road, then the line of buildings whose windows had already returned to institutional sleep.

Behind him, the tire ruts softened. The men’s prints blurred and joined the old tracks of maintenance carts and students and whatever ordinary traffic would be blamed for them by morning.

The place where the woman had stood vanished with everything else.

He kept walking.

At State Street, the pharmacy window gave him back a face bloodied from hairline to jaw, one eye narrowed behind the swelling, coat torn where the pavement had taken him.

A sane man would go inside, ask for help, surrender the evening to fluorescent light, insurance forms, police language, careful hands, concussion protocol.

Had he lost consciousness? Yes. Was he confused? Yes. Had he experienced visual disturbance? Yes. Auditory disturbance? Don’t be provincial. Had he seen anyone unusual after the assault? No physician would ask it that way.

That was the trouble with sane institutions. They arrived at the unsayable by degrees.

Rune’s voice entered with clinical patience: Have you considered that your certainty may be the symptom?

David looked at the reflection.

“Have you considered,” he said to the glass, “that your timing is poor?”

The automatic doors opened for a customer leaving with cough syrup and a lottery ticket. Warm pharmacy air reached him, smelling of rubber mats and artificial orange.

For one second he nearly went in.

The cello pressed against his leg: not a command, but a reminder.

Home.

He moved on.

After that the city narrowed to surfaces: curb, snowbank, black ice beneath fresh powder, brick wall, parking meter, the blue grocery awning beating itself against its frame.

He learned not to think beyond the next object. When he did, the distance opened and the pain rushed in to fill it; when he held only the next step, the fork and cello and whatever was left of him found one another again.

Three steps, then three more.

Every time the rhythm held, something in him spent itself to maintain it. Heat left him, and his fingers lost their finer instructions.

Twice the cello struck a post or the corner of a wall because his body understood distance half a second too late.

By the final hill, defiance had become too heavy for language. No wit or theory remained, only refusal.

He reached his building with the cello case dragging against his thigh and the tuning fork sealed inside his fist. His door sat upon the first floor, recessed just far enough from the street for snow to gather along the threshold.

He got the case through first, badly. The lower bout knocked against the jamb and sent the contact through his shoulder so sharply that the room flashed white.

His fingers missed the lock, then missed again. The key scraped uselessly beside the plate before finding the slot.

Warm air met him inside and made him weaker.

The apartment retained the morning’s disorder: coffee darkening in the press, loose sheets from the morning’s breakthrough, the chair pulled away from the slate wall.

Nothing had moved while he was gone.

David meant to close the door and got it mostly shut, but the latch did not catch, leaving a thin seam of winter air at his back.

The cello case slid down his leg. He tried to lower it carefully and failed.

It struck the floor with a dull wooden complaint.

David went down with it, not gracefully, not even completely, folding across the case where it lay beside the couch, one arm trapped beneath him, the other still closed around the fork. The broken watch pressed between wrist and rib. Snowmelt ran from his coat into the floorboards.

The door remained open several inches.

The last thing he heard was snow moving at the threshold.

Then everything went under.
