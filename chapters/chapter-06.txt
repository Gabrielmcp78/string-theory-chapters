6.1 SUBJECT | Buffalo, NY Present Day

The shuttle bus from Buffalo International Airport had no suspension, or perhaps simply chose not to use it. Each pothole translated directly into spine, the jolts accumulating like small grievances filed but never addressed. Upright in the seat ahead, the cello case stood braced between armrest and window, settled into place with the unremarkable patience of ordinary luggage.

At the wheel, the driver maintained a careless, ritualistic solemnity. His eyes remained fixed on the road, refusing even the rearview mirror, as though a glance might cost him something dear.

Above the windshield, a laminated sign read:

WELCOME TO THE NORTHEASTERN

SCIENTIFIC EDUCATORS ANNUAL CONSORTIUM.

NO FOOD. NO DRINK. NO QUESTIONS.

Through the vents ran a persistent buzz. Whenever the road supplied a sympathetic frequency, the cello found the narrow interval between that pitch and one of its own strings, trembling faintly inside the case. David pressed his knee against it. The hum retreated, returned, then retreated again, like a conversation he was not part of.

According to the onboard display, the forty-six-minute ride lasted twelve minutes; according to David’s watch, forty-seven. He found no reason to trust either.

Across the aisle sat a young postdoc with her laptop open to a half-completed grant proposal, its progress bar fixed at fourteen percent for the duration of the ride, though no upload appeared to be in progress.

At the hotel, the shuttle drew smoothly to the curb, as though arriving exactly on time for something important that had already happened without them. No one moved as they disembarked.

Only then did the driver glance into the rearview mirror. The upright cello case filled the frame where a passenger should have been.

“Professor?” he called.

David remained turned toward the rear window, staring at the road they had already traveled.

“Professor?”

Nothing, like he was watching something no one else could hear.


6.2 ANSWER | MIT - Boston, MA April 11, 2005

“You’re not listening,” David said. “I’m not saying music is mathematics. I’m saying they’re dialects. Same language. Different mouths.”

Marcus sprawled across the single chair he’d commandeered, legs draped over one arm, beer balanced on his chest with the casual entitlement of someone who’d never doubted his place in a room.

“Dialects.” He let the word hang, flat and unimpressed. “And what’s the native tongue, then? What’s the ur-language they’re both bastardizing?”

“Consciousness.”

Marcus snorted. “Jesus Christ.”

“I’m serious.”

“Oh, I know you’re serious. That’s the problem.” Marcus swung his legs down, suddenly alert. “You’re always serious about the most dangerous possible thing you could be serious about. It’s like watching someone juggle grenades and congratulate themselves for their hand-eye coordination.”

Celeste sat on the floor opposite David, her back against the radiator that clanked arrhythmically. It was a percussive commentary she’d long since stopped noticing. She held her beer with both hands, the way someone cradles a warm cup in winter.

Watching.

Always watching first.

“What’s dangerous about it?” she asked.

“What’s dangerous?” Marcus leaned forward. “He’s proposing that the entire edifice of physics, which, by the way, has done pretty well with things like not dying in childbirth and satellites, is just one translation of a consciousness-language we haven’t decoded yet. You know what they call people who think like that?”

“Visionaries?” Celeste offered.

“Unemployed.”

David’s jaw tightened. “You’re reducing it.”

“I’m not reducing anything. You’re inflating. There’s a difference.” Marcus jabbed a finger toward him. “You want to know what kills careers, David? Not being wrong. Being right about something nobody’s ready to hear. And you don’t have the patience for that. You want the revelation now. You want to skip the thirty years of proving yourself and jump straight to the Nobel speech.”

“That’s not—”

“It is. It absolutely is. I’ve known you for two years. You’re the smartest person I’ve ever met with absolutely no survival instinct for academia.”

“Maybe academia should work differently.”

“Maybe.” Marcus stood, pacing now, the small room forcing him into a tight loop. “Maybe it should. But it doesn’t. And you know what happens to people who try to change it from the outside? Nothing. They become footnotes. Crackpots. That guy who could have done something if he’d just played the game. Put in the time. Earned the right to break the rules.”

Celeste watched David’s face: the way his eyes went unfocused when he was running internal calculations, the micro-twitch at the corner of his mouth that meant he was suppressing something.

She’d learned to read him in three days.

It had taken Marcus two years.

“You think I want to play games,” David said quietly.

“Christ, David. You really don’t get it, do you? These aren’t games. They’re gates. You can’t get to the interesting work without passing through them first.”

“And if the gates are designed to filter out exactly the kind of thinking that might actually—”

“Then you beat them.” Marcus was in David’s space now, looming. “You beat them at their own game, and then you flip the table. That’s how revolutions work. Not from some grad student’s dorm room with a half-baked theory about consciousness and music and, what? Harmonics?”

“Resonance.”

“Resonance.” Marcus laughed, but it wasn’t kind. “You want to publish a paper on resonance and expect anyone to take you seriously?”

David was on his feet before Celeste could react. “You don’t get to decide what’s serious.”

“Someone has to! Because you won’t!”

They were chest to chest now, or close enough. Marcus had three inches on him, a fact he’d never once made David feel until this moment. David’s hands were balled. Marcus’s chin was lifted, daring.

“Hey.”

Celeste’s voice cut through like a tuning fork.

Sharp. Singular.

They both turned.

She hadn’t moved from the floor, but something in her posture had shifted: a stillness that demanded attention precisely because it refused to compete for it.

“Sit down.” Not a request. “Both of you.”

Marcus opened his mouth.

“Sit.”

He sat.

David remained standing for three more seconds, then lowered himself back to the floor, shoulder blades finding the bed frame again.

Celeste let the silence build. Outside, a bus groaned through its gears, Doppler-shifting from minor to major as it passed.

“You’re both right,” she said finally. “That’s the problem.”

She looked at David.

“You’re right that there’s something there. Some connection the rest of us haven’t learned to see yet.”

David exhaled.

“But Marcus is right that it doesn’t matter. Not yet. At least, not the way you want it to matter. You can’t storm the gates with a manifesto. You have to earn the right to be taken seriously. It takes time, David. And compromise. And I know compromise feels like betrayal when you think you can see something nobody else can see.”

“I can see something nobody else—”

“I know.” Her voice was gentle. “I believe you. We both do. But belief isn’t proof. And proof takes time. And you don’t have to solve everything tonight.”

The radiator clanked.

Marcus reached for another beer, cracked it open, and took a long drink. When he spoke again, the fight had drained from his voice.

“I’m not trying to kill your idea, Dave. I’m trying to keep you alive long enough to prove it.”

David stared at the floor. His fingers moved against his thigh. It was an unconscious compulsion: playing a passage only he could hear.

“What if it takes thirty years?” he said. “What if I prove it and nobody’s left who remembers I was crazy for thinking it?”

“Then you’ll be vindicated.”

“That’s not the same as being heard.”

The three of them sat in the silence that confession opened.

Celeste reached across the space between them and put her hand on David’s knee, a gesture so simple it embarrassed no one.

“We’ll remember,” she said. “Marcus and I. We’ll know what you were trying to say before you could say it.”

Marcus raised his beer, almost a toast.

“To the lunatics.”

David looked at him.

Finally, his shoulders dropped.

“To the lunatics.”


6.3 SUBJECT | Buffalo, NY Present Day

The registration table was arranged with military precision, except for the line marked:

LAST NAMES BEGINNING WITH

UNCERTAINTY PRINCIPLE.

David hovered before deciding he belonged in the H–R line, though the volunteer behind the table glanced at his badge and said, “Oh. You’re one of those,” in a tone suggesting the category was well known but rarely defined.

The exhibitor hall buzzed with intellectual self-importance. Posters leaned on collapsible frames, each promising incremental advances in fields that seemed to repel one another. A theorist lectured animatedly to a graduate student who nodded while staring into the middle distance. Someone dropped a stack of pamphlets advertising a paid workshop on interdisciplinary synergy. No one offered to help.

A senior physicist explained topological insulators to his winter coat.

The coat seemed unwilling to change its mind.

David kept hearing the shuttle’s hum whisper through the projector fans, carrying the same unresolved interval. He listened to the opening plenary with the attention one reserves for a fire alarm test: dutiful, doubtful, already bracing for the second round.

His own talk was scheduled for late afternoon. Location: The Morning Room.

Could he really be the only one hearing it?

The frequency seemed to flutter out of the objects he had blamed, gathering overtones until he could no longer tell which tone was the source and which the harmonic.

He grabbed a Local Tourist Info brochure from the front desk and began sketching the harmonic relationships on the rear panel. He needed to map what he was hearing. If anyone else was to confirm his sanity, he would need data.

The moment the concierge handed him a pen, another source, ambient and unknown, joined the chorus at precisely 136.96 Hz.

An exact doubling of the root.

One octave above 68.48.

And then he saw him.

Thirty feet away, near the poster session entrance. His beard owned twenty years of grey now, thicker through the middle, reading glasses perched on his nose as he squinted at a conference program.

Dr. Julian Kline, Astrophysics Chair at N.Y.U.

The hum surged in David’s chest with a density that sounded like recognition, as though the frequency had found something to resonate against and was doubling back on itself.

Kline looked up.

Their eyes met.

For exactly 2.47 seconds, neither moved.

David watched his own name flood Kline’s face, the recognition cascading from confusion to clarity and then to something that looked remarkably like dread.

Kline turned sharply, unwilling to risk a casual, even knowing second glance. He folded the conference program, tucked it beneath one arm, and started toward the opposite corridor with the stride of a man who had just remembered an urgent appointment that never was.

The phone at the registration desk rang out.

Two octaves above the root frequency.

David heard it before it rang. Or perhaps it had been ringing all along and only now had risen into audibility, doubling through one octave and then the next.

As the ring climbed, Kline telescoped forward as though someone had yanked a wire running through his spine.

He caught himself against the doorframe, one hand shooting out. The program fluttered to the floor.

He froze there for half a breath.

Then, slowly and intentionally, careful to give no one reason to notice, he straightened. He picked up the program without looking at it and disappeared down the opposite corridor.

David looked down at his own brochure.

His hands were shaking.

His left, gripping the paper, shook violently.

The concierge had seen all of it. With Kline gone, he looked at David.

“Sir? Are you all right?”

David flashed him a slick smile of assurance, then looked down.

The brochure was covered in notation: frequencies, ratios, harmonic intervals spiraling out from a central axis.

The work was unmistakably written in his hand.

He had no memory of executing a single line of it.


6.4 ANSWER | MIT - Boston, MA May 24, 2005

Celeste’s voice hit the line like a rupture.

“You did what?”

“Exactly what I just told you.” David shot back, barreling through the NYU student union, phone nearly slapped from his hand as he pushed into the chaos of Gramercy.

“I did what had to be done,” he snapped. “I showed him.”

“Showed who?”

“Dr. Kline.”

A pause. Then her breath hitched. A soft, involuntary intake, as if the floor beneath her had shifted.

“David… you didn’t. Not the data?”

“I did. I showed him everything,” David said, voice thinning now. “The plots. The resonance curves. I thought… God, I thought he could take it.”

“You thought?”

In the background, a rustle of footsteps on tile, a muffled exhale. Then Marcus’s voice tore through the line:

“What the fuck, David?”

“Marcus, just hold on now. The moment Kline saw the data, he froze. Went white.”

“David, man. We talked about this. We agreed. No acoustic harmonic data. Not at NYU.”

“You weren’t there. It was a judgment call. I told you, I had to know. Had to see if he… I thought I had him.”

“Fuck.” Marcus’s scream detonated into the phone. Then, to Celeste: confirmation by panic.

“I’m telling you, Marcus, something hit him. Hit him deep. He staggered like the numbers had gut-punched him or something.”

“You are unbelievable. Do you have any idea what this means?”

David knew exactly what it meant. The stupidity, so constant, so thick it made him dizzy. He stepped off the curb and almost vanished beneath a blur of screaming cabs slicing through the roundabout.

“Julian Kline? And you gave him resonance?” Marcus was past fury now. His voice curdled into mockery. “Congratulations, genius. It’s not theory anymore. You just took it live. You opened the goddamn door.”

“It’ll hold,” David said. “It’s not—”

“You don’t know that! Jesus, David, you’re so naïve. The whole damn world is going to come flooding in! You’ve fucked us. All of us!”

David could hear him pacing, the voice jerking sideways as Marcus turned on Celeste.

“Your boy just dragged us off a cliff.”

“Marcus, listen to me. It’s going to be okay. You have to trust me.”

“Trust you?” Marcus snapped. “After this? No. No more. I’m not doing this over the phone. Get your ass home. Now. Before you wipe out another wing of science and take our careers with you.”

“Come on, Marcus. You know I would never—”

“No, David. The only thing I know is that you’re an idiot.”

Celeste took the phone before he even felt her move.

“Idiot?” David was spiraling now. “Who’s the idiot here, huh? Me, or—”

She didn’t yell. She never did. Not when her father vanished. Not when David cracked the ceiling. Not even now.

“David,” she said, soft. Precisely.

And that was all it took. He froze. Exhaled. Silence pressed in like gravity.

“I’m on my way.”
