11.1 THE HOUSE ON THE HILL

Something had changed in the night.

Celeste couldn’t name it. A misalignment, perhaps, as if the air had shifted half a degree north. Nothing visible announced itself, and like most things in her life, it would remain untrustworthy until caffeine had been applied.

In the shower, she stood beneath the spray, suds tracing slow paths down her temples. With her eyes squeezed shut against the warm water, she found she couldn’t remember if she had actually put the kettle on, or if the memory was just another intention left at the bedside like a shoe dropped at the door—proof of arrival without evidence of follow-through.

Somewhere in the kitchen below her, the kettle began to whistle.

“Shit.”

The sequence mattered. It always did. Now the water was sitting there boiling away on the front burner.

It’s surprisingly difficult to remember to make coffee before you’ve had coffee.

The thought lingered as the water pressure dropped without warning. A sudden rattle in the wall, then freezing water as the shower cycled: lukewarm, cold, briefly hot again.

“Please. No.”

The flow sputtered into a thin, rusty trickle, then cut out entirely.

“Perfect.”

She fumbled for the towel, blinking suds from her lashes. Cold ran a precise line down her spine. Downstairs, the kettle reached a sharp, ear-splitting scream.

“Oh, for God's sake.”

She stumbled out of the claw-foot tub, half-blind, reaching for the towel just as it slipped from the rack and hit the floor.

“Great. Come here, you little shit.”

She snatched it up, wiped her face dry, knotted the fabric, and pulled the wet hair from her neck as she hurried to answer the kettle.

“I’m coming!”

As she barreled out of the bathroom, the loose tile in the last row before the door shifted under her heel. It was just enough to force the slight overcorrection that sent her careening toward the overlook. She caught herself hard on the banister, vertigo flickering, then settled. She took a deep breath. Another half-inch and she would have taken the stairs headfirst.

From the landing, the foyer opened below her in clean rectangles of morning light.

She took the stairs fast, skipping steps, and grabbed the bottom finial to swing around the turn, only for the carved wooden ball to come clean off in her hand.

She stopped in the center of the foyer and stared at it.

“Goddamnit, David!”

The plumbing. The tile. The banister. His hand in everything that broke.

She set the finial on the table. It rolled slightly, then settled.

“It’ll be done next weekend,” she said aloud, the words automatic, mocking.

David had promised that. About the tile. About the pipe under the sink. About the bedroom ceiling fan he had accidentally rewired so that every setting ran in reverse. The first few months had been a dream: weekends full of tools and confidence, the appeal of a scientist turning his mind loose on a house—the same rigor that made his equations elegant, applied now to joist and joinery, as though the structure were a proof waiting to be completed.

Then next weekend became next month. Then next summer. Then, quietly, the following year.

The house had settled into its own habits—the chronic record of a place touched, begun, and then left.

Since David left, the tone of that presence had changed. Anger had softened to grief, but lately, to Celeste’s dry delight, plain irritation had taken up residence. A click in the floorboards where there hadn’t been one before. A loose sash vibrating in the north frame when the wind came up from the gorge.

She read this house better than she read most people. This morning, something was off.

She paused at the corner of the kitchen. The morning light fell at an unfamiliar angle, cold and slightly skewed across the floorboards.

She didn’t say his name. But the thought arrived intact.

What on earth has David done this time?

She stood there a beat longer than necessary. Listening.

No. On to things you actually control.

On to coffee.

She pulled the kettle off the flame just as the water threatened to boil dry. Steam rose hot against her wrist.

Then, beans to grind, grinds to press, boiling water on top. She waited.

Three minutes waiting for the press to brew gave her ample time to catalog everything she had already botched before eight in the morning.

She poured the dark brew into a chipped mug and carried it to her desk.

She pushed a drift of score sheets aside with her forearm and sat. Took a sip.

Scalding. And then the loose grounds hit her teeth. She spat a mouthful of gritty sludge back into the mug with a low, disgusted hiss.

Burnt. Same as yesterday. Gritty. Same as last Saturday. Same as every morning since David moved out to the apartment in town. He couldn’t seal a simple drain, or hit more nails than he missed. But damn, the man made one hell of a good cup of coffee.
