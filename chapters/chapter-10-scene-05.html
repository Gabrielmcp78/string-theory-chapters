10.5 Into the Storm

VIA DOLOROSA

He took the corner past Sage Hall too fast, the heavy fiberglass flight case knocking a hollow rhythm against his hip, pulling him down into the slick, freezing mud on the hillside. He couldn’t slow down, though. He had no right to walk after that horror show. No, this was a flat-out escape. He half expected to look back and see Rune chasing after him with paperwork and a pack of dogs. The catastrophic realization of his own professional suicide began to flash behind his eyes.

The boundary conditions. He hadn’t seen the boundary conditions. Eleven variables wasn’t over-parameterization if the system was open; it was a necessary degree of freedom—

His right foot slipped as he turned the corner past the old lab buildings. A huge gust wrapped up from the falls, the cold hitting like sheet glass. He stumbled across the plowed concrete, the case banging against his thigh. Near the retaining wall, his heel found a patch of black ice, and before any part of him knew that the ground was out from under him, he was down. His ribs struck the concrete curb as he fell backward, the impact launching the cello case from his grip as he slid off the pavement into a two-foot bank of plowed, gray slush.

He lay there, wind roaring in his ears, freezing wet seeping through his coat.

Now the whole day swirled into focus. A circular proof. And he hadn’t seen it. A first-year grad student would have caught that error.

He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes, his jaw clenching against the sharp ache in his side. He was losing his mind. There was no other explanation. Slowly, David curled onto his side, drawing his knees up to his chest in the freeze, his boots clawing at the slush for something solid.

Deep inside his coat, muffled by wet wool and wind, his phone began to ring. He didn’t move. He stared up at the fractured white sky, breath pluming in short, terrified bursts.

The ringing stopped. Three seconds passed, then it started again.

He dug a trembling hand into his pocket. The screen had cracked, but the name glowed through it: CELESTE. He answered. Whatever he had been holding down broke loose.

“I didn’t see it,” he said.

“David? Where are you?”

“I couldn’t see it. I built the model. I set the boundary conditions and I… Celeste. Eleven adjustable parameters. A custom manifold. I couldn’t even see I was doing it.”

“David, slow down. Are you outside? I can barely hear you over the wind.”

“It’s circular. I needed it to be real, so I made it real.”

“David, stop. Listen to me—”

“I sat in his office, Celeste. I sat across from him and handed him—Christ, a sophomore wouldn’t have allowed that methodological bias. He looked at me. He looked at me with pity.”

“Where are you?”

He ignored the question. “I’m losing it. I can’t trust the math. If I can’t trust the math, what do I have? I’m looking at the numbers, and they’re doing—”

He stopped cold. He had to be certain of what he was about to say.

“They’re doing what I want them to do, not what they’re supposed to do. It’s like I’m corrupting the data just by looking at it.”

“David, stop talking and listen to me.”

He planted his free hand in the slush and forced himself upright, ignoring the tearing pain in his ribs. He retrieved the cello case from the drift. “Twenty years in the ground,” he spat. “Jesus. The semetry. Twenty years. He told me not to do it, told me I couldn’t—”

“David, stop this. There is more to you than a damned failed theory.”

“Failed theory. I don’t know Cele. Maybe thats’s all that’s left of me.”

“ You’re not well. We’re all concerned about you. Rune is. I am.”

He started toward the outdoor stairs, the case dragging at his shoulder, boots slipping on the path.

He stopped.

“Wait. Rune said you called him.”

“David—”

“After the seminar. Monday night.”

“Yes.”

“Before the chapel.”

“Yes.”

The cold seemed to cut deeper.

“You sat beside me last night. In that room. You heard every note, and all the time you’d already called him behind my back.”

“I called him because I was frightened for you. I had no idea what would happen in that chapel.”

“What were you afraid of, Celeste? That I’d drag you down with me? Is being associated with me suddenly too dangerous for your career?”

“That is not true, and I don’t believe you think it is.”

“I don’t think you’re thinking at all!”

He stumbled along the path, righted himself, and kept moving.

“Look at you. Always managing the board. Always making sure the collateral damage is filed neatly under professional concern. You couldn’t just let it be our work. Had to get your clearance slip signed early.”

“You are completely outside yourself right now.”

“Am I? I spent the day alone, turning what happened into something else, pushing the math until my hands shook. And you’d already made your report.”

“Stop calling it a report.”

“That’s what it was. Roger briefs you, you brief Rune, and Rune tries to take his red pen to my fucking career. Very clean. Very efficient.”

“You think I needed Roger to tell me something was wrong? I was there. I was the one sitting beside you when you couldn’t remember how long we’d been playing.”

“Then you could have said something to me.”

“I have been saying something to you, David. You hear the parts you can fit into whatever you’re working on and the rest just—disappears.”

He tried to answer and found that he couldn’t remember what she’d just said. The discovery made him angrier than the accusation.

“Is it inaccurate? Did you call him or didn’t you?”

“I called because I was frightened you were going to destroy yourself. And I was right to be frightened. But listen to me, David. I heard it too.”

He stopped beside the top of the outdoor stairs.

“What?”

“The cello. In the chapel. After we stopped. I’ve been at the piano all day, going over the intervals. I can’t explain it. I heard it, and it’s been driving me mad.”

“Don’t.”

“Don’t what?”

“Don’t do that for me. I don’t need you to agree because you’re afraid I can’t survive being wrong.”

“I’m telling you what happened.”

“No. You’re telling me what you think will help.”

“You impossible bastard. You think I need your permission to hear something? I’ve spent hours going over what happened. I don’t know whether it has anything to do with your model. I don’t even know whether there is a model. But there was a sound after we stopped, and neither of us was playing it.”

He could still feel that low answer through the floor of the chapel. He wanted, with an almost physical hunger, to ask her to say it again. Instead he tightened his grip on the case.

“You don’t get to use that,” he said, his voice stripped of its fury. “You don’t get to weaponize our history now.”

“That’s what you think I’m doing, is it?”

He didn’t answer. He was moving again, down one step and another, the case catching his knee whenever the wind swung it forward.

“David, you accused me of calling Rune to save my career. Do you have any idea what it costs me to hear that from you?”

“Well, you can certainly afford it.”

Silence came down the line. He knew, the moment he said it, that he had gone farther than he intended. But he could not summon an apology without surrendering every other word.

“I’m not weaponizing it.”

“My father didn’t spend his life in that office, Celeste. He spent his life in Symphony Hall. He knew what it meant to play a room that wouldn’t forgive you. You don’t know the first thing about what he left me.”

“I’m trying to keep you from crashing into a wall you built yourself.”

“I don’t need you to keep me safe. I need you to stand with me.”

“I have stood with you. For twenty years. And every time I try to tell you what I see, you treat me like somebody standing in your way.”

He wanted to protest that she was wrong about him. More than that, he wanted her to be wrong. But he could remember a hundred unfinished conversations, her face across the table, the peculiar impatience he felt whenever she asked a question whose answer seemed obvious to him.

“That’s not what I mean,” he said, and could hear himself making it worse.

“What do you mean?”

He had no way to explain it quickly enough. He never did.

“Look, you’ve made your choice, okay? You’ve always made the safe choices. You’re not in the room. You’re up on the hill, in our perfect little house across the bridge, away from all this.”

“David—”

“Don’t start pretending you want to be part of it again. Don’t play me. Don’t fuck with the heart you say I don’t have. There it is. I’m not your problem anymore.”

The pain beneath his ribs caught as he descended. He held the railing and forced the words out through his teeth.

“Just leave me as I am. Okay? Leave it alone. Leave me alone. We both know that’s what it’s come to. You already made the choice. It was a good one. Congratulations. Have the guts to stick with it.”

For several seconds there was only the wind.

“You answered, David. You could have let it ring.”

He kept his eyes on the stairs.

“After everything you’ve just said about me, you still answered.”

He opened his mouth. He could have told her he’d answered out of habit, that he’d only wanted to tell her about Rune, that it meant nothing. But he had seen her name on the cracked screen before he answered. None of it would survive her hearing him speak.

His throat worked. He had no answer she wouldn’t hear straight through.

“Goodbye, Celeste.”

He ended the call, killing her voice in a single, violent swipe of his thumb.

As he pocketed the phone, his left shoe found the ice-glazed lip of the final concrete stair into the faculty lot. His foot shot out. He threw his right hand against the brick retaining wall, catching half his own weight. The cello case struck his ribs, nearly pitching him forward into the slush.

He stayed there, gasping, one hand pressed to the brick until the pain loosened enough to let him straighten.

A man was standing three steps above him.

For a ridiculous instant David wondered whether the fellow had heard the call, had witnessed the humiliation of a grown man losing his temper over a telephone. The thought embarrassed him before the more immediate oddity registered: the man had been there long enough to have snow gathering on the shoulders of his coat.

Dark coat. Hands in his pockets. Silent and close enough that David wondered how long he’d been there.

“Excuse me,” David muttered, shifting into the slush to let him pass.

The man didn’t move.

David continued into the lot. The storm had blurred the painted lines and made the cars into low white shapes. His own stood beneath the sycamore at the far edge.

Someone was waiting beside it.

A man in a Carhartt jacket, hood drawn low, snow collecting on his shoulders. He watched David approach without moving toward a vehicle of his own.

David glanced back. The first man had followed him down the steps.

David pressed the remote. His car chirped. He kept the keys in his hand. No response came from the man beside it.

He opened the rear door, laid the cello on the seat, and secured it with the belt. He checked the buckle and shut the door. The man had moved beside the driver’s door.

David reached for the handle. The man caught the door before it opened.

Behind him, boots stopped in the snow.

“Wallet.”

David looked from one to the other.

“Oh, for Christ’s sake.”

“Wallet.”

“Fine.”

He reached into his coat.

“Slow.”

“I understand the convention.”

He handed over the wallet. The man opened it, took the cash, and checked behind the cards.

“Phone.”

David surrendered it.

“Watch.”

“No.”

The men looked at his wrist.

“Don’t make this stupid.”

“You are not taking this watch.”

“Sure about that are you?”

The first man caught David’s wrist. David pulled back. The second stepped close enough to block him against the car, and the grip tightened.
