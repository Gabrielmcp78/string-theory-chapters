--- CHAPTER TEXT BEGINS ---

David's office sat two buildings and an arts-quad crossing from the physics cluster, tucked behind a row of former faculty houses now repurposed as boutique galleries for regional heritage. Late-afternoon light seeped through warped Venetian blinds, striping stacked volumes of Annalen der Physik into a barcode of abandoned inquiries. A cello case leaned against the southern flank of the bookcase, its leather scuffed from a subway commute he no longer made.

David shouldered through the door, threw his coat toward the hook, and dropped his satchel onto the desk. He crossed to the computer and stopped dead.

Rune Ishikawa was already seated in the corner armchair, stirring a cup of Earl Grey, almost indistinguishable from the dark upholstery.

“You know, David,” Rune said, unhurried, “there simply isn't milk anywhere. I searched through chemistry. Dreadful world. Still, nothing. So here I sit, drinking an entirely naked cup of tea. Shameful.”

“Rune.” David lowered himself into his desk chair. “About the lecture—”

“They say Princeton University Press is interested in a revised monograph from you,” Rune interrupted smoothly. His manicured fingers grazed the gilt edge of an old encyclopedia. “An expansion on your decoherence paper.”

“Who's they, exactly?”

“We share an agent, David. Charles called this morning. Mentioned you’d been rather difficult to reach. Said Princeton inquired last week.”

David turned to the paper drift on his desk. He opened a drawer, rummaged through a stack of unopened mail, and produced an envelope with the press's insignia. He held it up just long enough for the letterhead to register, before Rune could see it was a subscription renewal.

“Wait and see, right?” David said.

Rune lifted one hand in a gesture that balanced congratulations with warning. “Indeed.”

Rune set down his cup. “I meant to say: it was a lovely service. Your eulogy was quite remarkable.”

David stared. “You were there?”

“I stayed forty-five minutes. Hardly an ordeal. I’ve known you twenty years, David. It is not lost on me what giving yourself to this hill took from your father. Or from you.” Rune's gaze held him with clinical precision. “Your father was a formidable man, though delicately composed. You inherited that trait.”

The compliment was disarming in its rarity. In twenty years of shared hallways, Rune had mastered proximity without disclosure.

“Thank you, Rune.”

Rune rose, smoothing a hand along the edge of the desk. “I'll leave you to your mountain of paper.” He reached the door, then paused at the threshold. “Oh, and David… Resonance equals survival?”

David froze above the mouse.

“An interesting premise.” Rune tapped the doorjamb in 7/8 time. “Resonance as a survival strategy. It has an intellectual elegance. Though one does wonder when you'll find the nerve to move beyond curating the old masters and grace us with something speculative again.”

He offered a faint nod. “Good night, Professor Lang.”

“Good night, Dr. Ishikawa.”

Rune drifted down the hall, his soft leather soles making no sound on the linoleum.

*   *   *

The lecture folder remained open on the monitor. David clicked through the slide deck: the Lang-Feldman diagram, the waveform, the cochlea, and twelve standard slides on psychoacoustics. Nothing had been altered since he built the file three years ago. The Polaroid existed nowhere on the drive, in the cache, or on the network.

He searched twice more, then looked toward the empty doorway.

Rune had left the lecture hall before those words were written.

David opened the bottom drawer and pulled out folder LXIII: Theoretical Explorations (Strike Outs). The NSF denial still bore bourbon stains from 2014; an IEEE referee had cited “excessive poetry in the equations.” He added the day's speculative scrap beneath the pile and shut the drawer.

In the corner of the desk lay the manila envelope from Schaker, Marsheck & Camden, the firm administering Arthur's estate. David looked at it, looked away, then forced himself to look again. He could not bring himself to draw the papers from their sleeve. A few marks in ink, and his father's death would pass from catastrophe into administration.

They had argued for decades about science and music: one obedient to law, the other disciplined only so it could remain human. Arthur had wanted him to stay with music, where exactness served beauty without apology. David had insisted a land bridge existed between them. Twenty years later, he still could not tell whether continuing the search was fortitude or stubbornness.

He nudged the envelope back into its corner, still unsigned.

“I will, Dad,” he whispered.

*   *   *

Beyond the glass, the East Hill quad darkened under accumulating sleet. Between the bare maples, the green bench remained visible twenty-eight feet off the path.

The ground around it remained clear: black-green grass against the white snow, as though the earth there had declined the weather.

Under dense clay and root-striated soil lay the sealed case he, Celeste, and Marcus had buried twenty years ago.

David's fingernail traced the rim of his coffee cup, finding the enamel chip left from the night he dropped it while trying to convince Arthur they knew what they were doing.

He glanced at the wall clock, then down at Arthur's watch.

Both faces agreed: it was three minutes, forty-seven seconds past time.
