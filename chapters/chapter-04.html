The Music School’s residence on East Hill had never resolved into a single idea. It was a slow-growing organism shaped by necessity and the pressure of young lives needing rooms in which to fail and begin again. The 1907 stone chapel sat at its center, cool and enduring, around which newer wings had gathered over decades. Above the musicians hung acoustic clouds: pale suspended planes stitched with open seams, so that sound wandered into rafters and the chapel attic.

Below, fluorescent corridors tunneled under the hill, binding the stone chapel to the 1974 annex. Concrete here did not merely contain music. It held it.

Roger Linn was, as always, the first to arrive. His office opened onto the catwalk directly above the rehearsal room. He was spreading scores across music stands when David shouldered through the door. Chairs stood squared, stands dressed, the room warmed into looking like any ordinary Tuesday. It was an ordinariness that did not happen by itself: quiet love without fanfare.

Prokofiev’s Second sat atop the pile, corners weighted flat, its margins crowded with penciled bowings that read like an ongoing dispute with the composer. David set his cello case down with an unmusical thud.

Linn looked up, took an inventory of face, posture, and hands, and offered his old reflex grin, bow dangling from one hand. He lifted the wood in a small gesture. On any other evening it would have meant How was your day? Tonight it asked Still standing?

David shrugged. They had spoken this grammar since their youth in Boston: exhaustion as syntax, rendered wordless by long practice.

Catherine Rieger entered in a flurry of wool, her overcoat swinging off mid-stride. She kissed Linn’s cheek, then David’s, before pausing. She had not seen him since the funeral.

“Oh, David, darling,” she murmured. “How are you? And your mother?”

“A mess and doing great,” David said. “As always.”

“Cathy and I were trying to count how many times we heard the BSO when your father was second chair,” Linn said. “Between us, maybe one-twenty-five?”

“Easily,” Catherine said, moving to her chair.

“Sounds like you both had me by a hundred and twenty-five,” David said.

Celeste Armitage appeared in the doorway, viola cased on her back. As she passed David, she touched his elbow, firm and warm. For one disorienting moment, the frequency beneath the hill seemed less important. He caught her eye; the smallest nod passed between them.

Roger tapped the corner of his stand and drew a clear, straight A. Catherine joined, then Celeste, then David, open strings bringing the air into alignment. Beating fifths narrowed as the quartet locked into a single acoustic body.

“The Kabardinian,” Roger said. “Shall we?”

SERGEI PROKOFIEV String Quartet No. 2, “Kabardinian,” Op. 92

Resonance preceded sound. David felt the cello’s ribs thrum against his sternum before horsehair met gut. The wood knew first: spruce and maple executing their arithmetic of tension, density, and cold air.

An instrument solved differential equations by vibrating.

The opening chord struck: F rooted in the bass, B-flat major above it, a bitonal figure that displaced silence with something strident and folk-like. In the first measures Roger carried the foreground, his line sharpening in real time, while David and Catherine provided sustained bass ground.

Then the meter contracted to 2/4 and half the bar vanished like a step removed mid-stride. David held his place while the rhythm dismantled its scaffolding.

Nothing supernatural occurred. Prokofiev had trained the ear through subdivision, brief metric removal, and return. But David began extracting structure that was already there.

The melody dissolved. Harmony dissolved into a lattice of ratios. A quartet was four bodies drawing sound from wood, string, and horsehair, each body a node in a network of vibration no single player controlled.

His fingers maintained accuracy. Every shift landed clean, with the inevitability of a body falling under gravity. The machine ran on; the operator had stepped elsewhere.

Past fingerboard and f-holes, past Celeste’s breathing beside him, David was mapping nodes, tracing overtones, measuring the room in frequencies, abandoning feet. The air had become legible. He read it as another man might read a loved one’s face, except the room did not love him back.

The Adagio opened in broad 12/8. David’s cello entered with a line climbing into treble clef, singing as the primary voice, while Celeste’s viola answered with sustained lyrical warmth.

Celeste listened for him in timing and vibrato, bending phrases toward him, waiting for his cello to answer. He played the notes as written: flawless, and absent as a metronome keeping time for an empty room.

Her viola took up an ascending figure beneath long slurs. She leaned toward him, breath catching on the edge of a phrase.

Twenty years compressed into a single unresolving note: weekends lost behind the closed door of his study while their shared life receded. Once, she had mistaken his distraction for depth.

At rehearsal twenty-one, Prokofiev marked the viola sul ponticello. Celeste drew her bow toward the bridge. Her sound thinned and brightened, glass entering the tone:

$$x_{\mathrm{Celeste}}(t) \xrightarrow{\mathcal F} X_{\mathrm{Celeste}}(\omega)$$

Her bow moved. Spectral brightness separated. Fundamental. Partial. Partial. Partial. The warmth left the sound first. Then the bow. Then the woman making it.

$$\text{Celeste} \longrightarrow X(\omega)$$

He stared through the score. The music returned her to ordinario, warming her tone, but David remained elsewhere.

A Saturday surfaced: Celeste standing outside his study with warm coffee, saying his name twice through the oak door. That evening she had found the cup where she set it, cold, a skin formed across the dark surface.

Her bow trembled against the C string as she leaned toward him.

“David,” she whispered beneath her line. “Stay with us. Stay with me.”

Something crossed Roger’s face, but the score continued to the final cadence. When the sound died away, Celeste set down her viola and touched David's arm.

“David. Hey. Come back.”

His eyes lowered from the ceiling and sharpened on her face.

No speech. They regrouped.

The finale began from below: violins resting while David and Celeste traded plucked bass figures, building the dance from the bottom up. Bows returned to strings.

He and Roger shared an instant of exhilaration as they climbed a fierce modulation. Sixteenth notes drove the texture forward in sharp exchanges: attack, response, attack.

Near the bridge, something caught: a metallic burr beneath the speed of the passage.

His left hand shifted, his mind supplying the physics:

$$f = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}$$

Pitch. Length. Linear density. Tension.

The finale drove into Caucasian dance: relentless motor rhythms, the cello pounding the pulse beneath the violins toward its breaking point.

The col legno arrived. Bare wood turned to the string. The stick struck. The string recoiled.

Another strike. The burr sharpened.

His bow came down hard.

SNAP!

The cello’s C string detonated under tension, a heavy silver whip springing loose with a hollow crack. The tungsten winding uncoiled against the bridge, lashing the lower bout before falling dead across his shoe.

All four froze.

Silence held for two measures. Then four people exhaled together.

Someone barked an ugly laugh of pure relief, and instantly the room detonated into uncontrollable adrenaline laughter. David doubled over his cello, bracing one hand against the floor. Catherine wiped tears from her eyes; Roger leaned back so hard his chair nearly tipped; Celeste laughed until she had to press her palms against her knees.

As the noise subsided, Catherine packed her violin with brisk efficiency. A single nod passed between her and Roger, and within moments the two had collected their cases and slipped out into the corridor, leaving David and Celeste alone.

For a moment neither moved.

David inspected the snapped string. “Really? I could’ve sworn I nailed every note. Haydn never had such a cellist.”

She stopped smiling. “Haydn?”

“Opus seventy-six, right?”

His face remained completely straight.

“David, it’s Prokofiev.”

“Oh.” He looked down at the loose wire. “Well, that explains the col legno.”

Celeste shook her head, tucking a dark strand of hair behind her ear. “David was playing Haydn all evening,” she murmured to the empty room.

At the double doors she paused, hand on the push bar. The air in the hallway seemed to drop two degrees. She turned back slowly.

“David…?”

He looked at her, his expression shuttered. “It’s nothing.”

She waited, reading him. “Get some sleep, David. Goodnight.”

“Goodnight, Cele.”

Only he called her that. She lingered for a heartbeat, then pushed through the doors into the snow.

David packed his cello into its hard case, latching the bronze buckles one by one. In the corridor, a distant vibraphone aligned with his tinnitus, a pure sustained tone that would ring in his inner ear for hours.

Above the exit doors, the clock read 8:14.

David looked down at Arthur's watch.

Three minutes, forty-seven seconds past time.
