9.2 THE CLASSROOM | Feynman Hall · Following Day

Thirty-seven students. David counted them twice from the podium while two sophomores at the back conducted an energetic dispute over midterm credit and a young man near the windows slept with his cheek flattened against a laptop, apparently confident that quantum mechanics could be absorbed through the skin.

The walk up the hill had already shaken him. Crossing the Arts Quad forty minutes earlier, the frost-killed grass near Goldwin Smith had flashed saturated magenta in the corner of his eye. When he stopped to look directly at it, the turf was merely dormant brown; when he turned away, the impossible hue flared back across his periphery, disappearing the moment he swung his chin around. He had briefly considered photographing the lawn, then realized the camera would only record the color the grass actually was.

Now, beneath the lecture lights, the crisp snap of a fresh cylinder of Hagoromo chalk against slate was an old, tactile comfort. He wrote the wave equation, established the fixed boundary conditions, and worked through the sinusoidal solutions with the steady fluency of someone who could still derive a vibrating string without assistance from sleep, memory, or a reliable nervous system.

“A remarkably efficient arrangement,” he observed, stepping back with dusted palms. “All the excitement of velocity, with none of the inconvenience of travel. I suppose for the gamers among us, that sounds rather familiar.”

Several students laughed, and he turned back to separate the harmonics into their constituent terms. But at the third panel his hand stopped dead. Beside the wave equation stood three ratios:

(2:1 \qquad 3:2 \qquad 4:3)

An octave. A fifth. A fourth.

He had no recollection of writing them, yet there they were—the exact proportional figures that had appeared in the Buffalo conference brochure, in his scratch calculations, and along the margins of his hotel legal pads.

“Professor Lang?” Rebecca sat in the second row, her pencil poised with that intense, suspicious gravity she brought to every derivation. “Are those the ratios between successive harmonics?”

“Yes. Quite right,” David said, bracketing the figures with a quick stroke. “An important distinction. One I apparently expected you to infer while I stood here admiring my handwriting.”

The class laughed, and as he looked at the notation, an idea caught him.

“Actually, let's try something.” He drew three vertical lines on the board. “Suppose I generate a composite signal containing energy at three hundred, four hundred, and five hundred hertz. No physical component at one hundred. What pitch do you perceive?”

A student in the front row raised a hand. “Three hundred? The lowest component present?”

“Intuitive, logical, and entirely mistaken,” David said, sketching a broken coordinate axis beneath the harmonics. “Those frequencies share a periodic spacing of one hundred cycles per second. Under suitable conditions, your auditory system recovers the missing fundamental. You perceive a pitch corresponding to one hundred hertz even though the waveform contains no sinusoidal component at that frequency.”

Rebecca’s hand went up immediately. “So the listener hears something that isn't really there?”

“The pitch is a real perceptual experience. What isn't there is the corresponding spectral component in the air,” he said, tapping the chalk against the dotted line. “The information is present in the relationships, but the fundamental component itself is absent. Your auditory system has answered a question the signal never explicitly asked.”

He paused, catching the puzzled tilt of several heads. “Which sounds rather more philosophical than the syllabus warrants. Speaking of intellectual misbehavior—Leonard Bernstein.”

He erased a corner of the board and drew a short musical staff. “Bernstein gave a famous series of lectures at Harvard in 1973 called The Unanswered Question, borrowing from Chomsky's linguistic theories to explore whether music possesses anything resembling an innate universal grammar.” When only a single hand went up in the rear, David smiled. “Splendid. The rest of you have six hours of spectacular intellectual misbehavior awaiting you. My mother sang in the chorus for Bernstein, which either gives me the hereditary right to sing in front of you or constitutes gross pedagogical negligence. We shall find out.”

He sang the familiar childhood taunt into the room:

Sol – Mi – La – Sol – Mi.

From the back came an immediate drawl: “Nanny-nanny boo-boo.”

Laughter rolled through the tiers. “Precisely,” David said. “Humanity's most durable contribution to acoustic communication.”

In the second row, Rebecca's pencil stopped.

“A descending minor third,” David continued, marking the interval on the staff. “And you've supplied something I didn't give you. Not just the familiar words, but their peculiar little sentiment.”

A student near the aisle raised her hand. “But isn't that learned? Children hear other children sing it, so it doesn't prove there's a universal musical grammar.”

“Certainly,” David said. “No, it doesn't. Though it does demonstrate that undergraduate skepticism is alive and well.”

The class laughed, and though she had exposed a flaw in the example, David was enjoying the diversion. He sang the opening notes again. “Now. Answer me.”

A student echoed the interval, another joined from the rear, and within seconds the entire lecture hall took up the melody. David raised his hands, egging them on. “Again!” The phrase returned louder, students turning toward one another across the desks, someone supplying an absurd bass part while two girls near the windows sang the response an octave higher.

David laughed aloud. “Merciful heavens. This is what tuition buys.”

He gave them another entrance, the room locking into phase, shouting the final cadence in a burst of collective competence. But as David raised his hands to cut them off, his eyes crossed the second row.

Rebecca sat with her knees pressed together beneath the desk, her hands buried in the sleeves of her cardigan. Her head was bowed, dark hair falling across her face, and when the boy behind her sang the refrain toward someone across the aisle, she flinched.

Then she looked up.

Their eyes met, and for an instant the lecture hall gave way to a playground in the hard sunlight of an autumn afternoon. A little girl stood beside a chain-link fence, her lunchbox hanging from one hand, while a circle of children sang the same five notes around her. Their faces were bright with the pleasure of belonging to something together, and the girl, perhaps seven years old, searched them for somewhere to go, some opening through which she might pass without having to hear the melody again.

A bell rang.

David blinked.

The lecture hall returned around him, thirty-seven students singing at his invitation, his own hands still raised above the podium. Rebecca was looking at him, her face pale beneath the dark fall of hair.

He lowered his arms.

“All right,” he said, quietly.

The singing faltered, one voice carrying on a beat beyond the others before falling silent.

“That's plenty. Thank you.”

He looked at Rebecca again, but she had lowered her eyes, and whatever question he might have asked seemed certain to make her more conspicuous. He turned toward the board.

“Now, before we all start demanding royalties from one another, there's a technical distinction to make,” he said, writing beneath the staff. “The implied tonal center of a musical phrase and the missing fundamental of an acoustic signal aren't identical mechanisms. Musical expectation involves relationships we've learned—or perhaps recognize without knowing how we've acquired them. The missing fundamental concerns periodicity in the physical signal. And the two can interact, which is partly why music gives physicists so much trouble.”

He resumed the derivation at a brisk, punishing pace, changing the boundary conditions and correcting a student rather sharply when the boy confused a fixed end with a free one: “Those are different physical constraints. You cannot simply exchange them because the algebra looks more attractive.”

He knew he was pressing too hard, but the mathematics was clean and he wanted the shelter of it. When Rebecca raised her hand to ask whether the solution would hold if tension varied along the string, he answered her with crisp, formal precision, then noticed that only three students in the entire room were still taking notes.

Setting down the chalk, he sighed. “Well. I've abused you sufficiently for one morning.”

A few students smiled in relief, and Rebecca looked up from her desk, her pencil resting across the notebook.

“Professor Lang? Should we copy that second proof?”

“Only if you're deeply devoted to gratuitous academic suffering.”

She gave a small, genuine smile. “I'll skip it, then.”

“A sensible career move. Class dismissed.”

The amphitheater emptied in its usual chaotic flood of backpacks and chatter. David remained at the podium wiping chalk dust from his hands, looking over the board until something in the bottom right corner stopped him cold. Beneath the boundary conditions stood:

65533:26553

And beside it, in his own unmistakable handwriting:

Holds, but does not open.

He stepped closer, searching the surrounding equations for some mathematical context, some half-finished note to himself. Nothing. Reaching for his phone, he considered photographing the slate, then let his hand fall away. What would the photograph establish? He knew the words were there.

Pressing his thumb against the board, he smeared the chalk until the letters dissolved into a gray smudge, though the open loop of the p remained stubbornly visible beneath the dust. He rubbed harder until his skin burned.
