17.1 The Warnings | Somerville, Massachusetts

March 21, 2005

The telephone cord stretched taut across the linoleum of the Somerville kitchen. A faint sixty-cycle hum traveled through the telephone line.

"Dad, listen to me," David said, leaning against the cold porcelain rim of the sink while outside the window a late-winter sleet ticked against the glass. "It isn't static. We checked the numbers over and over. The whole sky is ringing."

"David, son. I did not say it was static."

Arthur Lang’s voice arrived through the receiver with the familiar, dry timbre of a man who spent five hours a day listening to string sections through wooden bridge feet, tuned to the micro-tonal frictions of horsehair on sheep gut.

"No, Dad, you're hedging," David said, his words accelerating into an urgent rush. "Remember what you told me about the Fifth Suite? When you tune the top string down to G. You told me the moment you drop the tension on the spruce belly, the wood stops fighting the room. The body opens up and takes in the whole hall. You said the instrument holds an empty space."

A long silence hung on the line. In the background came the faint, metallic scrape of an ebony mute being fitted onto violin strings.

"I remember what I said, David."

"That's what this is, Dad," David said, his voice dropping into that raw, breathless certainty Arthur feared most. "It isn't random radiation. It's an open fifth, pulsing with the sun. The atmosphere and the rock—they're locked together. The whole planet is an un-damped box. It's an instrument, Dad. It's ringing."

"David." Arthur’s voice was gentle, heavy with a weariness that went back thirty years. "You know I cannot follow the numbers. You know that."

"You don't need the numbers! You understand resonance better than anyone in my department."

"No, son. I understand friction. I understand horsehair pulling across gut. I understand that when a note is finished, it dies in the curtain velvet." Arthur paused, and the receiver crackled with a soft, dry breath. "Listen to me. I have a rehearsal of the Strauss at Symphony Hall in forty minutes, and your mother is putting dinner on the stove. Don't go looking for a song where there is only wind, David. It doesn't love you back."

"Dad, it doesn't die," David whispered. "That's what we found. It doesn't die in the velvet."

"Call your mother on Sunday."

The line went dead. David held the plastic receiver to his ear for five seconds, listening to the flat, monotone hum of the Boston dial tone.

* * *

May 24, 2005 — The Eve of the Journey

"David, you are not getting in that car tomorrow. I forbid it."

"Dad, please. Two months ago you said you couldn't follow the numbers. Now you're handing down orders."

"I did not say I didn't understand the danger," Arthur snapped across the line, sharp as dry boxwood. "There is a vast difference. What possible good comes from dragging your papers to an upstate college to show Rune Ishikawa?"

David gripped the receiver until his knuckles went pale. "Dr. Ishikawa is not an administrator, Dad. He is the future of Cornell. He is the new Bethe. He is trying to protect us."

The line went silent. A sudden, rigid freeze took hold of the connection. Through the receiver came the faint, sulfurous scrape of a kitchen match, followed by a slow exhalation of pipe smoke.

"I don't even know why we're having this conversation anymore," David said, his voice tightening. "I should be talking to Elliot. He understood all of this."

"Yes, he did, son. Your grandfather most certainly did. But he is dead."

"Do not say his name to me," Arthur whispered. His voice had dropped into an icy, dangerous register David had never heard before. "Do not bring your grandfather into this fight. Even buried in the ground, the two of you come at me with your scientific swords and super-powered egos."

"Dad, Elliot spent his life listening to this exact same sound—"

"Elliot died eight months ago, David!" Arthur’s shout shook the receiver. "We buried him in September. And ever since, it's as if you have simply picked up his obsessions. My father spent the last eight years of his life trying to take back the first forty. Documenting, reliving that horror so he could burn it from existence."

"Dad, that wasn’t what he—"

"David, I will not have you lecturing me like this." "Dad, I'm not lecturing you. I'm trying to..." "Son, do you have any idea... Any idea at all what he went through? Once Sagan got sick and stopped taking Elliot's calls, your grandfather barely left his study in Cambridge. He had no one else still alive who understood what he heard. He sat there surrounded by legal boxes, sorting through papers from nineteen forty-five, terrified because he was one of the last men alive from Bethe’s division who remembered that flash in the desert! And when the lawyers finally executed the will, where did those forty crates of his life go? They were shipped to Ithaca. To Rune Ishikawa."

Arthur stopped, breathing hard into the mouthpiece.

"Dad," David said quietly. "Ishikawa offered to review the work."

"Ishikawa took delivery of my father's demons, David! And now he clearly wants to take delivery—take control—of you."

"Celeste trusts him. Marcus trusts him."

"Celeste loves you, David. She would follow you into a burning room if she thought it would keep your hands warm. Do not use that girl as a shield for your vanity."

David felt a cold weight settle behind his sternum. "It isn't vanity, Dad."

"Then what is it? Why do you have to exhume what he left in the ground?" Arthur’s voice dropped into a quiet, agonizing plea. "Son... some things are not meant for an audience. Some things are only meant to be buried."

David stared through the kitchen window into the dark street. A light rain fell across Cambridge, silvering the asphalt beneath the streetlamps.

"I have to know what he heard, Dad."

A long, ragged exhalation came through the line. The sound of a man who had fought a war for twenty-five years and finally reached the end of his strength.

"Then you are already his," Arthur said softly. "God help you, David. You are already his."

The line clicked dead.

David hung up the telephone. He picked up his coat, his leather notebook, and his bag, walked down the stairs to the curb, and got into the car.
