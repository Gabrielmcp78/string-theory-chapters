I. CHORALE | Wednesday Dinner

The cafe’s acoustic tiles could not quite contain the Wednesday-evening murmur, but Celeste had chosen their corner booth precisely for its distance from the kitchen’s iron percussion. David arrived eight minutes late, a crescent of white chalk still pressed beneath the cuticle of his thumb despite three washings. He slid into the booth, his satchel falling onto the vinyl seat beside him with the heavy, leaden thud of unread monographs. “Hmmm.” She cast a sideways glance toward her phone, then back to him. “I’m not entirely sure I recognize you coming from this side of the hour. Eight minutes late, huh?” “I know, Celeste. I’m sorry, I—” “No, no. I mean, hey, I’m a big fan of the casual arrival. Always have been, David.” “Yes, I’m well aware, but I promise, it’s not—” “Now wait. Before you toss it off, let’s take a look. Perhaps it suits you.” She looked him over in earnest this time. “I mean, yeah. It could work, if you decided to sleep once in a while. David. Have you slept?” “Well, yes. Yes, of course I have.” “When? On Sunday? David, you look like you haven’t slept since Sunday.” “Monday, thank you very much,” he corrected, settling into the booth. “I slept Monday. For three hours.” She ordered a carafe of the house pinot noir without asking. Ten years of Wednesday dinners had left them with few decisions to make. When the wine arrived, she swirled it with her right hand, her fingers curled in the light, automatic curve of a viola bow hold. Though this dinner was no longer required under their dissolved romantic tethering, both had agreed, eighteen months ago, that ten years of Wednesday suppers was too stubborn a habit to be undone by the semantics of a breakup. For David, the ninety Wednesday minutes had long since become the still point of his week: a tide that pulled at him before he knew he was moving. Hours before, he would feel it: a quiet loosening, then a tightening in the chest, the body’s old, wordless rehearsal for joy. He did not decide to go. He simply found himself ready, waiting, turned toward the hour like a shore waiting for the moon. It was not love. That much was certain. Not out loud anyway. It was order. Plain. Simple. Order had reasons; love had none. That made sense. That was all… That, and he was quite fond of the chicken piccata. “You’ve done something with your hair, yes?” David wondered, unsure which version of her he was ever seeing. “Mmm… no. No, I don’t think so.” But she was happy he noticed anything at all. “You sure? Didn’t get it cut or—” “No, David. I’d remember.” She smiled sheepishly. “Well, you look… you look beautiful.” She glanced up with a blink and a sideways smile as she tucked a wayward chunk of hair behind her right ear. David watched her tuck the hair behind her ear, the same small gesture that had mesmerized him since the day they met. For an instant, the years between then and now seemed to give way. There she was, three feet across the table, as she had been through all those years of being his, of being them. The distance was the same. Three feet. Yet she had never seemed farther away. And somewhere inside that impossible measure, between the man he was now and the two of them he could still remember being, he knew that if a Wednesday ever came when she stopped coming, it would end him. He took a sip of water, then straightened the napkin, the menu, himself. Small rituals of order. She watched the faint tremor in his wrist when he reached for the glass. She would not ask about the classroom, nor the conference over the weekend. She had decided that before her coat was off. “Long day?” she asked. He nodded without looking up. “They’re all long now.” She wanted to touch his hand but settled for refolding her napkin instead. Candlelight made him look almost well; if she did not stare too hard, she could believe it. Celeste would always come. Her friends told her she was weak for it, that she was indulging his pathology, normalizing his self-absorption. Still, she came every Wednesday. At intervals as random and unassuming as she could manage, while pretending to examine the entrées on offer, she glanced above the flimsy laminate protection of her menu to study the man she loved and lamented in equal measure. She hoped he didn’t notice. He always noticed. Christ, stop looking. Where’s the damn waiter? “Should we order?” she said. “Let’s get the waiter.” She did so very much love the Linguine Caruso. She smiled faintly at the absurdity. David thought it smelled vaguely of dead rat. Perhaps that was why she loved it: because David did not. “How’s your mom doing?” she asked. After a delay, he said, “Hmm? What was that?” “Your mom, David.” “Shit.” “David, tell me you’ve spoken to your mother since the funeral. She’s alone in that house for the first time in almost fifty years.” “No. Yes, I mean, of course I have. It’s not that. The damn lawyers. I keep missing their calls during lectures, and I… I haven’t—” He stopped, his gaze listing out the window toward the quiet street and the glowing blur of lamplight along the wet street below. “Hey.” Celeste reached for his cheek, coaxing him back without letting him turn the gesture into rescue. “It’s what you hired lawyers for. Right? So you can miss a few calls and everything your father wanted done will still get done.” “Yeah.” He found a smile for her, nearly usable. “I miss him too, David. Best father-in-law a girl never had.” The waiter arrived. He was not permitted to deliver the specials or suggest a beverage upgrade before Celeste said: “I’ll have the Caruso.” “And the piccata for me.” “Anything to drink with your meals? Another glass of—” Together, in parallel, they said, “No, thanks. Water’s fine.” They waited while the major-undeclared freshman faded from earshot. “Da-vid.” Celeste stretched the two syllables into a reprimand only ex-wives, girlfriends and mothers could achieve, a precise instrument of calibrated disappointment. “Yes. You’re right. I’ll… I’ll keep up on… on all of that.” “She needs you now more than she ever did. Don’t let your work do that thing you always let it do.” “I know. I know. I’ll call her tomorrow. She’s fine. The old battle-ax is fine.” “You and I both know that’s not true. The same way we both know you are not fine.” A pause. “And that’s… okay.” “Yeah. I know.” The conversation eased into the familiar grooves of their shared geography: department meetings, Ithaca gossip, the students they held in common. Big school, small town. Fine lines everywhere: invisible, permeable, at least until crossed and then: unflinching. Unforgivable. Somewhere between the entrées cooling and the second refusal of dessert, David reached into the satchel. His fingers found the plastic bag and the book inside it. Twenty years of evidence suggested there was a fairly reliable interval between a pleasant evening with Celeste and David finding some new way to damage it. He had never successfully calculated the mean, but he knew enough to recognize when the clock was running. Suddenly, giving her the book seemed less sentimental than prudent. “Hey, I brought you something.” She looked at the torn grocery bag and smirked. “What, the other half of Monday’s turkey sandwich? What is this.” She laughed as she took the package, her fingers tearing gently at the plastic where the seam had already failed. Beneath, lay the narrow volume. The tuning manuscript. It was bound in dark leather gone gray at the hinges, its spine cracked, the gilt title reduced to a few stubborn islands of light. She read the spine and went entirely still. “David.” “I rescued it from the parlor,” he said. “It was getting choked by Wagner and Bartok and…” “Whoa,” Celeste balked. “Tough neighbors.” “Yeah. Well, I just… I knew it belonged to you.” Her fingers moved over the warped cover with a reverence that, to David, looked like magic. Just as she moved to open the cover, something stopped her. “There’s no way I’m opening two-hundred-year-old pages over marinara. This deserves a table with a lamp and some dignity of surroundings.” “Well, that can be arranged.” She held the book against her chest. Then, as though the two objects had been waiting for one another, she reached into her viola case and brought out several folded pages. “So, I was going to ambush you after the piccata,” she said. “Pythagorean, with modern alterations.” Her free hand shaped an interval in the air between them. “Elegant in a purely musical sense. I thought we might do some stringed investigation with it later. You know, just music. Force quantum mechanics to sit in the corner like a good dog.” David looked at the charts, then at the book against her chest. Something in the arithmetic of the evening resolved without his permission. Two people had walked into a mediocre restaurant carrying, from opposite directions, the same idea. “Professor Armitage, what would you say to joining me for a little chapel break-in?” “Why, Professor Lang,” Celeste said, “I thought you’d never ask.” He signaled for the check and, for once in his adult life, did not calculate the tip with unnecessary precision. At the door he held her viola case while she wound her scarf. She let him. And while both of them noticed, neither said a word.

II. ARIOSO | The Tuning

They stopped at the apartment for the cello, and Celeste waited in the car with the book on her lap, not opening it. She rested both hands upon the cover, as if contact alone might begin whatever permission the thing required.

“You understand this is technically a crime,” David said when she turned up East Hill.

“I think God will understand, don’t you?”

“Sure, but I very much doubt he’d post bail for us.”

“Yeah. What a cheapskate.”

“I dare you to say that once we’re inside.”

The side entrance accepted her key with a strange familiarity.

Celeste looked back, a grin of success across her face. “They never digitized this back door lock for some reason. Had this key since the day you dragged me to this frozen wasteland.”

Somewhere beyond the lower corridor, a practice room leaked four bars of trumpet, stopped, started again, and found a more interesting way to fail.

The chapel was nearly dark. Moonlight and the lamps along the pathways outside gave the stained glass just enough light to scatter patches of blue, amber, green, and gold across the interior, the old design deciding what could be seen and what remained hidden.

Celeste found the rehearsal lamp near the front and switched it on. Its narrow cone cut across the colored darkness, illuminating the long table where she began arranging the book and her charts.

David lingered among the pews.

He had always loved what the chapel did to time. Here, moments seemed to loosen from their obligation to proceed, lingering in the spaces between one sound and the next, free for a little while from the necessity of arriving anywhere.

Grace notes in granite.

He wandered toward the back, counting.

Fourth row. Fourth seat from the aisle. Book four.

He lifted the hymnal from its rail and looked behind it, at the wood of the pew.

Still there.

The carving he had begun years ago.

David smiled, a small sound escaping him before he could give it the dignity of a word. He returned the hymnal to its place, then let it settle against the rail with a quiet knock.

The sound passed along the pew and returned faintly from somewhere high in the chapel, changed by the distance it had traveled.

At the front, Celeste looked up.

She had heard him.

“Don’t you dare, Lang.”

“I have said nothing.”

“No, but you were about to become unbearably mathmaticious, and you know it.”

“I was about to admire the room, thank you.” “Yes, with equations.” “Maybe I was. So what? Some rooms invite such things, you know.” “Well, this one has suffered enough, and so has the room.” “Oh, that’s very funny, Ms. Armitage. Very funny. And for the record, mathmaticious is not a word.” “It is if I say it is, Lang.” “Yes, ma’am.” She glanced back at him with a wink in her eye as she searched for supplies. The book’s pages had swollen and contracted through enough seasons that the volume would no longer lie flat without persuasion. She retrieved two felt-covered music weights from the vestibule beyond the piano, settled them across the corners, and began examining the opening chapter. The printed text began with monochord lengths, divisions of the octave, and an author untouched by the possibility that anyone might disagree. Someone had disagreed almost immediately. “David, look. There’s writing in it.” She pulled him beside her by the sleeve. A brown line of ink ran beside the second paragraph, ending in a cramped correction whose final word disappeared into the binding. Two pages on, a darker hand entered, sharper, reducing the author’s claim to three ratios and a question mark. Farther along, a pencil appeared only beside passages on beating, sympathetic vibration, and the behavior of low strings in closed rooms. Then, above an empty stretch of margin, five notes in blue-black ink, the last scratched out. “I think… yes. I think there are four distinct hands here,” she said. “Five.” He pointed to a faint gray notation curving around the oldest ink without touching it: slighter pressure, another slope, a hand with its own idea of how much space it was entitled to occupy. Celeste followed it through the margin, then turned back two pages, forward three, checking something. “No one writes over anyone else.” “Seems unusually civilized.” “Yeah, maybe. But I don’t think it’s manners.” Her finger rested where the gray hand bent itself around the brown. “They all seem to be leaving room for…” “For what?” “I don’t know yet.” Celeste returned to the blue-black figure they had found earlier and brought the lamp closer. She sang the four surviving notes, stopping where the fifth had been scratched away. “Again,” David said. She sang them once more. Several possible endings occurred to him, each less satisfactory than the last. “You're waiting for it,” Celeste said. “For a note that isn't there?” “For an ending.” She examined the erased note. “Somebody changed their mind.” He looked back at the faint gray notation they had already identified. It curved past the scratched-out note, continuing into the following margin without supplying a replacement. “That one didn't finish it either.” “Maybe they were listening for something that hadn't happened.” They read until the hands became almost social: one defending the pure fifth at any cost, one restoring the third and mourning what it displaced, the pencil counting in irregular clusters, patient as a man tapping a table while he listened for a thing he could not yet name. Celeste spread her charts beside the open book. David asked what the interval had done to deserve the word cowardice circled above it. She said it had compromised. He was reaching for the pencil on the music desk before he had decided anything, and her hand closed over his around it. “David Lang, what did I say about mathmaticianing?” “That it too is not a word.” “Don’t.” “What? It’s not.” “All right. All right. I’m just checking a single ratio.” “Oh, really. How about you’re just putting the dog back on the furniture.” “It’s just a little arithmetic, Cele. There are no animals involved here.” She smiled as he said her name. “That is what you say just before spacetime gets involved.” She took the pencil from him and tucked it, along with her hair, behind her right ear. She looked back and caught David staring at her with a ridiculous grin. For a few moments, neither turned from the other’s eyes. David’s phone broke the moment, its buzzing and screen light flooding the chapel. His mother’s name filled the display. It stopped, waited just long enough to allow the room to settle, and started again. “She knows you can see her calling, David,” Celeste said. “She also knows it’s nearly ten.” “Oh, please. Your mother has never once regarded time as an enforceable structure. It’s one of her—” “Worst qualities, I know.” “I was going to say best, actually.” David looked at her. “The two of you were always far too simpatico for my liking,” he said. “Maddening, really.” “That may be the nicest thing you have ever said to me, David Lang.” When the phone rang a third time, she said, “I see. This is exactly what you do to me too, isn’t it? You turn anyone who actually wants to talk to you into a nuisance.” “Oh, I do not.” “Answer your poor mother.” “Fine. Fine.” He swiped at the screen with mock effort and an actual smile. “Hello, Mother.” Cassandra arrived in the chapel the way she arrived everywhere: mid-sentence, at concert volume, speakerphone utterly unnecessary. “—and if she doesn’t like it, you are to bring it straight home. I have a list of people who would commit crimes for anything of Lenny’s. Well, crimes is unkind. Petty moral collapses, perhaps, but still. You cannot leave that sort of thing lying around among people with advanced degrees and no moral supervision.” “Mother, you are in the middle of someone else’s conversation again.” “Well, David, honey, whose fault is that? I called three times. If you had picked up the first time, like a good son, you would know exactly what I have been talking about for the last three minutes.” Celeste’s laugh escaped before she could stifle it, and the chapel made the joy of it larger than either of them expected. “What was that?” Cassandra asked. “Nothing, Mom.” “Is Celeste with you?” “No, Mom.” “Celeste, honey, are you there?” “Hey, Cassy,” Celeste said. “I’m here.” “Brave girl, spending time with my son at this hour.” “Stupid is probably—” “All right,” David said. “How about we not make this beat-up-on-David hour.” “Forgive my boy, Celeste. He got his moodiness from his father, you know.” A pause settled over the line. The knowledge of why belonged to all three without anyone having to touch it. “Well,” Cassandra said, more quietly. “I guess I stepped into that one, didn’t I?” “What can we do for you, Mother?” “What can you do for me? You can tell me how it went. Did she like it? Did she love it? Celeste, honey, do you love it?” “Love what, Mom?” “The book, Davy. The old tuning thing from the parlor, unless you’ve started distributing Lang-family treasures to every beautiful woman you run into on the street. I think you know what book.” Celeste looked at David. “Wait. She knows?” “What? No,” David said. “It was my idea, dear,” Cassandra said. “Mother, it was not your idea.” A small, satisfied silence crossed the line before David said, “You said, ‘Go in the parlor and see if there’s anything you want to take with you,’ then mentioned Wagner scores.” “No. I said, ‘Wonderful Wagner scores.’” “That does not make it your idea. Sorry.” “Oh, really, son?” “Yes, Mother. Really.” “Honey, what did I say after that?” David looked at the open book and felt the trap close with embarrassing elegance. “You said there were some wonderful Wagner scores in the cabinet by the—” “And where did you find the book?” Celeste’s laugh escaped before she could stifle it. “Cassy, you are a genius,” she said. “No,” Cassandra said. “I have simply spent forty-seven years trying to get this boy to do what he needs to do before he notices he is doing it. The idea came from me.” Celeste lifted the volume as if Cassandra’s victory had altered its weight, and the old paper jacket slipped free and folded onto the floor. When she bent for it, she stopped. “David.” Beneath a faded dealer’s stamp, a small engraved plate caught the lamp: FROM THE LIBRARY OF LEONARD BERNSTEIN David reached for the jacket. “Mother,” he said carefully, “where did this book come from?” “The parlor, silly.” “Before the parlor, silly.” “Oh. The Bernstein kids sent boxes after Lenny died. Scores, books, somebody’s hat. I never did find out whose. Your father alphabetized all of it and complained for a month, which is how I knew he was pleased.” Celeste had gone quiet, reading the bookplate’s edges the way she read margins. “I mean honestly, what on earth would I have done with a tuning book?” Cassandra continued. “My job was to sing the right note when Lenny wanted the right note and make the wrong note beautiful when he wanted the wrong one. He always seemed perfectly satisfied. No book required.” A pause, softer and more dangerous. “Besides. I knew who would eventually open it.” David was still assembling a response when his mother said, in exactly the same tone: “David, honey, did you tell her about the cello?” The room went still around David’s single warning: “Mom.” “What?” “No.” “No, you didn’t, or no, you won’t?” “Cassandra,” Celeste said, and there was enough care in her voice to make the care frightening, “what about the cello?” David reached for the phone. Celeste’s hand arrived first and lay flat over it, not pressing anything, simply present. “If anybody on this earth would understand it,” Cassandra said, “it’s the woman standing next to you.” Celeste kept her eyes on him. “Understand what?” He set down the bow he had not realized he was holding. “There was a repair label inside it,” he said. “From Buffalo.” “What kind of label?” “A repair strip.” Then, because each fragment purchased a moment in which he did not have to produce the next: “Old. Eighty-five. It reads E.G. for E.L. BFL 11/85. Elliot Lang was my grandfather. I don’t know what was done.” She absorbed this, her attention moving once toward the cello and back. “How long have you known?” “Four days.” Her face stayed quiet, which was worse. “And you found the shop by accident.” “I found the address by accident.” “That wasn’t what I asked.” He looked at her. “No,” he said. “It wasn’t.” Cassandra let the silence hold just long enough to prove she had heard it. “David,” she said, “either you tell her these things when they happen, or I will. You are not the gatekeeper between me and your ex—” A pause; the audible redrafting of an entire domestic constitution. “Better half, or whatever it is we are calling you tonight, dear.” A classically Cassy giggle spilled across the line. “Well,” she said, “I think my work is done here. Good night, my darlings. And, Celeste, enjoy the book. I knew you’d give it a good home.” The line clicked quiet before either of them could answer, which was, they both understood, the point. Celeste did not move toward him. For a moment the chapel seemed to keep the call for itself: the open book under the lamp, the cello waiting on its side, the phone gone dark between them as if it had never been anything but an object. “You weren’t going to tell me,” she said. “Sure, I was.” “Sure you were.” “When?” Every answer available to him became another form of delay, and to his credit he attempted none of them. Celeste held him there another moment. Then her attention shifted to the cello. “Show me.” “Wait, so you’re not pissed at me?” “Oh, I’m pissed. But I’m a hell of a lot more interested in this than I am in you.” He angled the treble f-hole beneath the rehearsal lamp and showed her: the faded strip deep against the inner rib, the ebony around the endpin fractionally newer than the wear surrounding it. Celeste examined both with her hands clasped behind her back, the way she had taught a generation of students to look at instruments that did not belong to them, and he understood that the posture was addressed to him. When she straightened, she said, “The cello gets a vote.” “In what?” “The tuning.” “That is not how physics works.” “David Lang, do you really wanna be trying to tell me how physics works? Think carefully about that before you…” “No. No, of course not, Cele…” She tucked the viola beneath her chin, tested the open C, and let the note settle. The silence that entered afterward had weight. “What?” he asked. “What is it?” “Nothing,” she said, close to breaking. “Celeste—” A moment’s silence flattened out between them before Celeste jumped back in. “The fact that your mother trusts me with what you hide from me. The fact that you hide it anyway. The fact that this instrument has had a secret longer than either of us has been alive.” She drew the C again, quieter. “And the fact that if we stand here discussing your talent for concealment, you will become unbearably precise and I will become mean.” “I would never accuse you of imprecision.” “That is because you value your life.” He almost smiled, but the attempt did not survive. She lifted her bow. “Open C.” David sat down, brought the cello’s neck to his shoulder, and felt the familiar, heavy wood press against his chest. He turned the peg of his C-string. It resisted, cold wood sticking in the box, and he had to press the palm of his hand hard into the scroll to force it to hold. The peg clicked twice, vibrating up his forearm, his thumb aching from the counter-pressure. When he drew the bow, the first note was rough, a raw friction that shook through his collarbone. Celeste entered an octave above him on her open C, then swept cleanly to the open G: a pure, resonant fifth suspended above his bass. Her viola’s tone was bright, almost clinical, cutting through the damp chill of the stone room. Celeste listened with her whole body, head inclined, bow arm suspended, the line of her throat lengthening above the viola as though the room had asked a question only she could hear. David watched too long, then looked away too late. “Again,” she said. He gave her the note again. She entered beneath it, correcting by less than speech could have named, and his hand found the adjustment before his mind reached it. The sound changed (closer rather than louder), and they adjusted the fifth between them, then adjusted it again, following her chart until their breathing began to find the same interval. She leaned into the upper edge of the pitch; he answered from below. When she moved, he knew where she was going a fraction before she arrived, and when he resisted, she smiled without looking at him, because resistance was also information and she had always known how to use it. The two instruments began to occupy the room more completely, the floor accepting the sound before the walls did. But beneath all of it their bodies remembered a fluency their words had lost: her bow asking, his hand answering; her shoulder opening the phrase, his bass gathering it; each correction a pressure passed between them and returned transformed. For a few measures, they were perfect together, the danger inside the perfection part of its beauty. Celeste lowered one string by less than he could reliably measure. “Again.” They played, and the interval opened like a door neither of them had meant to touch. David felt it first in the endpin, a faint change in resistance where the cello met the floor, then in the cup of his left hand as the neck answered against his thumb. Celeste heard it before he could name it. He knew because her bow slowed without losing the line. “There,” she said. “I know.” “No, you don’t. Listen.” He did, following her through the marginal corrections one by one, accepting none as authority. A pure fifth. A narrowed third. The disputed comma. The five-note figure in blue-black ink, its ending scratched away by someone dead long enough to have become handwriting. Celeste supplied an ending; David rejected it. She played it again quietly, with a change in the weight of her arm that made refusal feel suddenly ill-mannered. He found a bass beneath her answer, four measures that returned without quite repeating. The bass returned until repetition became ground, each cycle altered by the pressure of what had preceded it. They passed the figure between them, widening and narrowing its interior space, until the room held the shape of a passacaglia neither had chosen and both recognized. Near eleven she missed an entrance because he altered the bass beneath her. “That is not what you played before.” “Well, it's what I meant to play before, so...” “So… You don’t get to revise retroactively.” “Excuse me, I’m a physicist, of course I do.” “That is not the exoneration you think it is, mister.” “Hey, I know it’s been a while since you put on a lab coat, but um… physics doesn’t really exist without retroactivity.” “Infuriating man.” And the grin was no longer something she could stifle. “That’s right. I am. Thank you.” She struck the passage again, hard enough to make him chase her, and he did. The argument crossed three keys, lost all contact with Pythagoras, and acquired instead the dangerous fluency of two people who knew exactly how far the other could be pushed. Her bow found the edge of his timing and leaned there. His bass moved under her, refusing to give way until the refusal itself became support. They arrived together on an F neither had intended and could not hold for laughing. David lowered his bow, but something continued: a low response inside the cello, thinner than the note they had played, lasting perhaps two seconds before it slipped beneath hearing. Celeste’s laughter faded. “Again,” she said. They tried and got nothing. He reset the endpin, checked the bridge, touched the string lightly as though the instrument might be withholding out of offense. “Again.” Again, nothing, only the room, the ticking heat in the lower pipes, and the chapel holding its opinion. Celeste looked toward the open book. He followed her gaze. Neither said anything, and both noticed the other not saying it. They played until nearly midnight, pursuing the sound long after the cello had returned to the ordinary loyalties of wood, gut, and varnish. Each attempt clarified only the distance between repetition and return, and still they played.

III. RECITATIVUM | Break-through

Morning came with a clear skull, an unknotted jaw, and the uncommon sensation that his life had returned to scale. He dressed without searching his reflection for evidence of collapse, carried the cello to campus because leaving it behind felt suddenly unthinkable, and reached his office before nine.

For nearly an hour, it held. Then he opened the notebook.

Across the final page, beneath a passage he did not remember writing, he had drawn the residual tone as Celeste described it: the original pitch, the cello’s delayed response, the two-second decay, the failed attempts to reproduce it. Beside those marks sat the measurements they had taken before abandoning the experiment: string length, room temperature, approximate bow pressure, the compromised interval Celeste had insisted on preserving, and the accidental F that had made them laugh because neither had put it there and both had arrived.

The figures had seemed almost tender in the chapel. Under the office lights, they began to arrange themselves.

David turned to the triptych blackboard and opened all three panels.

He began with the response curve. The cello had not merely echoed the final pitch. Its body had retained the pressure of the route that produced it; the same note, approached through a different progression, had produced nothing. The instrument had remembered the path.

He wrote the phrase at the top of the center board, then immediately crossed it out. Memory invited metaphor. The mathematics required something cleaner.

PATH-DEPENDENT RESONANT RETENTION

Below it he reconstructed the sequence from the night before: the room, the viola, the cello, Celeste’s altered scheme, his bass refusing and then following, the disputed interval, the repair beneath the varnish. Each variable entered separately until the board became a web of coupled oscillators whose present state depended upon the route by which the system had arrived there.

The model resisted him for forty minutes until one term moved: a boundary where he had been treating the term as a constant. He carried it across the equation, normalized the decay curve against the instrument’s fundamental, and watched the remaining values collapse toward a common surface.

David stepped back and checked the work again, slower. The convergence remained.

At ten-thirty, the center board had filled. The left panel held the experimental sequence from the chapel. The right held comparative data from archived acoustics studies, coupled-pendulum systems, neural entrainment models, and two unpublished vibration papers he had kept because their anomalies had irritated him for years.

He corrected for scale, material density, and damping. The results did not become identical, but they occupied the same narrow geometry. He began calculating faster.

What Celeste had called leaving room for another hand now appeared as a measurable property: each system status retained prior constraint while admitting later influence. The response did not erase what came before. It incorporated it. The apparent noise in the data was evidence of hidden path.

By noon, seventeen pages covered his desk. By three, the sky beyond his office had lowered into a single white surface, and the university had begun its quiet winter rearrangements. Advising appointments became emails. Graduate students discovered urgent reasons to continue working from home. Somewhere below, a maintenance truck made its second pass along the service road, erasing its own earlier tracks with bureaucratic commitment.

David did not notice until the final diagram stopped becoming more correct. It resembled neither a waveform nor a field map. It was a folded manifold with several local states projecting toward the same attractor, each marked by a different physical history.

The cello’s response sat at the center, modest and undeniable. David stared at it until the office disappeared.

“This is it,” he said.

The words entered the room softly. He said them again, testing whether certainty changed when spoken aloud.

“This is actually it.”

At 3:47 he was in the department office feeding pages into the copier. He called Rune’s secretary while the machine sorted the sets. Jessica answered over a murmur of phones, coat sleeves, and someone asking whether the west lot had been plowed yet.

“Oh yes, Professor Lang,” she said. “I have you with Dr. Ishikawa at five-thirty.”

David shifted the receiver against his shoulder. “I was calling to see whether he had time.”

“Yes. Five-thirty.”

“So he’s free then.”

“For you, Professor, yes.”

Her politeness held something sealed inside it as David looked down at the copied pages. Along the first sheet, his title stood in temporary capitals:

HYSTERETIC RESONANCE IN PATH-DEPENDENT COUPLED SYSTEMS

He did not remember entering anything into his calendar. “Did I arrange this yesterday?”

A pause, slight enough to deny itself. “The appointment was requested on your behalf.”

“By whom?”

“I believe Dr. Ishikawa thought it best that the two of you speak.”

The copier released the final set as David thanked her and hung up.

For the next ninety minutes he reviewed the paper with increasing calm while the building emptied around him. Rune would see the distinction immediately. The model made a falsifiable claim. It predicted where the residual energy should appear. It even explained why they had failed to reproduce the cello’s answer: once they repeated the sequence deliberately, they had changed the path.

At 5:28 he stood outside Rune’s door with three copies beneath his arm and Celeste’s laughter still present in him, less an echo now than evidence, and knocked.

IV. TURBA | The Guillotine

“David, come in.”

Rune sat behind his desk, reading a document on his monitor. The window behind him had gone nearly opaque with weather; beyond it, campus lights showed only as softened yellow defects in the moving white.

“Thank you for staying, given the weather,” Rune said. “Apparently frozen water has become something young people pay attention to in Ithaca.”

David kept the copies under his arm. “After twenty years up here, snow stops registering as weather. It becomes a beautiful, carefree day with poorer traction.”

“A local adaptation.”

“A regional illness.”

Rune almost smiled, then returned his attention to the screen.

PROFESSOR DAVID LANG EVIDENTIARY INCIDENT SUMMARY

The heading occupied the screen above three pages compiled by Rune’s secretary and the Dean’s office. At the bottom, several recommendations had been marked (Letter of Direction, Disciplinary Action, Dean of Students Referral), the last carrying an asterisk and a note in the Dean’s hand.

Your discretion on all, Rune. Terrible business. I’m sorry.

Rune removed his glasses. “David, sit down.”

David placed the copies on the desk without looking again at the screen. “Before we do whatever that is, I need you to see this.”

Rune’s attention moved to the pages. “The work from yesterday?”

“No. Yesterday was wrong.”

Something altered in Rune’s face: relief. David opened the first copy and turned it toward him. “The premise was wrong. I kept looking for fixed harmonic relations when the phenomenon is path-dependent. The present state carries information about the route into it.”

Rune glanced at the title, then at David. “Start at the beginning.”

David did, describing the sequence at the chapel without naming what had made it possible: the altered tuning, the disputed interval, the accidental F, the laughter that had broken them open for one measure before the cello answered after both instruments had stopped. He explained the failed repetitions, the measurements they had taken before the room changed, the realization that deliberate return was not return at all because the path had already been altered.

Rune interrupted only once: “The response lasted how long?”

“Approximately two seconds.”

“Recorded?”

“No.”

“Measured?”

“Partially. We had already taken comparative readings during the tuning.”

“Who is ‘we’?”

David heard the question inside the question. “Celeste and I.”

“I see.” Rune looked down again as David moved into the model. Path dependence. Hysteresis. Coupled oscillators. Residual energy distributed according to prior constraint. Comparative systems whose anomalies had seemed unrelated until their histories were treated as boundary conditions rather than discarded as noise. He showed the correction procedure, the response curves, the narrow geometry that remained after scale and damping density had been accounted for.

Rune read without expression. Forty-three seconds passed after David stopped speaking before he turned back to the first page. “The cello event generated the model.”

“It revealed the missing variable.”

“That is a better sentence,” Rune said, “but not a different answer.”

“Well, I would argue that…”

Rune simple lifted his gaze over the rim of his glasses. No words were needed.

“Yes. I mean, No. It’s not.”

“And the event was neither recorded nor reproduced.”

“The inability to reproduce it is predicted by the model.”

Rune’s pen came to rest beside the equation. “So, and I want to be precise about this, a model developed after an unreproduced event predicts that the event will resist reproduction.”

David heard the shape of the objection before Rune completed it.

“That doesn’t invalidate the mathematics.”

“No. It invalidates your use of the event as evidence for the mathematics.”

Rune drew a small line beneath the normalization term. “Why this reference state?”

“It allows comparison across systems with different scales and damping coefficients.”

“It also forces each system’s largest deviation into the same range.”

“The geometry remains after correction.”

“Because you selected the correction that preserved it.”

“I tested six alternatives.”

“And how many failed to converge?”

David hesitated. “Four.”

“And they are where, exactly? I do not see that data represented here.”

“They were inappropriate to the physical behavior.”

“According to what independent criterion?”

The office seemed to lose several inches in every direction as Rune continued reading. “This archived pendulum study. You have treated mechanical friction as equivalent to retained state.”

“At the level of the response function—”

“At the level of the response function, many unrelated mechanisms can be made to resemble one another after normalization. That does not make them one mechanism.”

He turned a page. “The neural data already contain temporal smoothing. The acoustics study discarded the outliers you are now treating as path evidence. This vibration paper changed mounting materials between trials. You have absorbed every uncontrolled difference into one explanatory category.”

“They occupy the same manifold.”

“David, they occupy the manifold because this particular manifold was custom fitted to contain them.”

“No, Rune. Look at the boundary term. Just look.”

“I am. David, I am absolutely looking at the term.” Rune’s voice remained quiet, which made resistance feel increasingly childish.

“You have seven adjustable parameters in the general model and eleven in the extended case. With that degree of freedom, convergence is not surprising. It is nearly guaranteed.”

“The parameters correspond to real properties.”

“Some do. Some are inferred from the behavior you are claiming they explain.”

Rune tapped the paper once. “That is circular.” The word landed without volume.

David reached for the second copy and opened to the final diagram. “Then ignore the comparative systems. The cello alone demonstrates retained constraint. The response depends upon the sequence.”

“The cello may demonstrate any number of things. Sympathetic vibration. A body mode. A room mode. A repair altering the instrument’s local response.” Rune paused because he understood what it would cost. “Or an observation made by two exhausted people who were not recording the event.”

“That’s… that is not fair, sir.”

“This is science, Professor Lang. Fairness simply doesn’t factor.”

David, who had been barreling in relative silence toward the edge of many inadvisable reactions finally failed to respond in civil kind. He slammed his fist on the desk between them and yelled, “Damn it, I know what I heard.”

“I believe you heard something.”

The distinction entered David like ice water as Rune continued. “What I do not believe, and frankly what I will not accept, David, is that you have somehow cosmically earned or falsafiably demonstrated the movement from something happened in one instrument to matter preserves relational history through a universal resonant geometry.”

“It isn’t merely the instrument.”

“No. You’re absolutely right. It is not merely the instrument. It became the pendulum, the cortex, the archived noise, and every other system you could fit after you made the decision as to what the pattern meant.”

David stood; Rune did not. For a moment neither man touched the pages between them.

Finally, and desperately under his breath David said, plainly, “You asked me to walk you through this.”

“And so I did.”

“You had already decided?” David pulled away from the table, pressing his palm and fingers through his hair as certain realities settled over him.

“No, David. No that is not the case.”

“The Dean certainly had though. Yes?”

“The Dean gave me discretion.”

“And all this was… was what? One final courtesy before you made it official?”

Rune’s eyes lifted. “No.”

The answer carried enough injury to stop David. Rune closed the paper, though he kept one hand upon it.

“David, there is not a single bone in my body that doesn’t want everything you have ever brought to me to be absolutely real. I wanted the work to be real. I want you to succeed, David. You must know this.”

Rune removed his glasses and set them beside the pages. “You know, when I was twenty-six, I built a model connecting spiral density in galaxy surveys to non-baryonic-matter distribution. The mathematics was elegant. Several rather prominent people, mentors, encouraged it. One of them was a Nobel Laureate, which made the encouragement especially poisonous.”

David stood motionless and increasingly numb while Rune continued. “I had normalized three separate data sets against different baselines. Each choice was defensible by itself. Together, they manufactured the correspondence I believed I had discovered. I spent eight months proving a decision I had already buried inside the method.”

Rune looked at David’s diagram. “What you have here is not nonsense, David. But it is also not sound science. That is why it is dangerous.”

The quad beyond the window shone beneath a hard, indifferent light. Students crossed the grass in coats left open to the cold.

Rune rose and came around the desk. “The model fails, Professor Lang.” The words were plain; they required no metaphor.

He stopped in front of him, close enough that David could see the fatigue beneath his eyes.

“And I’m afraid the fact that it fails does not intrinsically tell us what the cello did.”

For one second, hope returned in a form too small to survive. Then Rune continued.

“What it does, however accomplish, is harder to admit. Harder for me to say. It tells me that you are no longer able to separate an event from what you need the event to prove.”

David’s gaze dropped to the pages as Rune placed a hand upon his shoulder. The contact was careful and therefore unbearable.

“David, last week I watched you lose the room and then help me pretend you had not. You have missed conferences. Your students are leaving. Nature Physics rejected the synesthesia paper because the central claim grossly exceeded the evidence. Celeste called me after the seminar because she believed what I had seen was no longer contained inside a classroom. Yesterday you frightened a room full of people who genuinely care about you, and frankly I’m not convinced you even know you were doing it.”

“She called you.”

“Yes.”

The answer opened another chamber inside the day. Rune already knew; what changed was that Celeste had carried what she knew to him.

Rune withdrew his hand. David looked at him as the phone buzzed.

“Dr. Ishikawa,” Jessica said through the speaker, “the Dean of Students is on line one.”

Rune closed his eyes briefly. “Thank you, Jessica.”

He gathered two clean copies beneath one hand and pushed the marked working set across the desk toward David.

“Leave those,” he said, indicating the copies he had retained, while David slid the marked pages into his satchel.

Rune took the call. David stepped into the waiting area.

By the time Rune finished the two-minute call, Professor Lang was three buildings and half a parking lot away.

“I understand, sir. Absolutely. Thank you.” Rune hung up the receiver and moved to open the door expecting David to be there waiting. “Sorry about that, David. Come on–”

Rune looked at the empty waiting area, then at his secretary Jessica, who simply shook her head and gestured to the main entry to the suite.

Rune moved swiftly back into his office and to the window overlooking the Arts Quad in time to see David course over the last rise at the far south west edge and out of sight into the oncoming white.

“Jessica,” Rune called over the interoffice system.

“Yes, Doctor.”

“I need Professor Armitage on the line. Now.”

Rune took his own phone from the inside pocket of his jacket and sent a direct message to a number with no contact information attached.

Downbeat. Go. Now. R.I.

“Dr. Ishikawa, I have Professor Armitage on line two, sir.”

“Thank you, Jessica.”

He slipped his cell back inside his jacket, lifted the office receiver, and turned in his chair to look through the window once more into winter-whitened night.

“Celeste, dear. It’s Rune.”

V. VIA DOLOROSA | Into the Storm

The wind had risen while they were inside, and the early exodus had given the storm the pleasure of looking theatrical. Upper campus had thinned to lit windows, plow noise, and the occasional student hurrying with the embarrassed speed of someone who had dressed for principle rather than weather. Loose snow drove sideways against the glass and flattened there before dissolving.

His coat remained unbuttoned. His scarf had come loose.

Celeste’s name appeared on the dark face of the phone. He watched it ring until the screen gave up, then ignored the message that arrived a moment later.

The faculty lot lay beyond the science buildings, down the concrete stairs and across a service road already whitening along its edges. At the first step, his left foot landed slightly beyond the agreement he had made with gravity. His weight followed after the stair had ended, pitching him toward the rail. The cello case struck one iron support with a hollow, ugly report.

David caught himself and remained there for several seconds, one hand around the rail, the other gripping the case handle while the storm moved through the gap between buildings. The body, annoyed at having been consulted too late, reported several conditions at once: left ankle stable, right palm cold, ribs intact, dignity unrecoverable.

The left foot felt normal when he tested it. Good. Excellent. A triumph for Newtonian mechanics. He descended.

At the bottom of the stairs, a man stood beneath the covered loading dock, smoking beside a yellow salt bin. David first registered only the ember, then the coat, then the face turning toward him. For one irrational instant he believed it was Rune.

The maintenance worker he had actually seen, or someone dressed as one, had heavy shoulders, wet cuffs, and a paper cup balanced on the salt bin beside him.

David crossed the service road. The man looked away before David passed, either uninterested or performing disinterest with unusual care. At the curb, a campus shuttle rolled by with three passengers inside, all lit blue by their phones. No one looked up.

By the time David reached the parking-lot entrance, the dock was empty and the snow had begun coming harder.

His car stood at the far edge of the faculty section beneath a sycamore whose last leaves had survived principally through spite. David took out the keys and pressed the remote. Nothing. He pressed again, and the cello answered from inside its case: a low internal pressure through the handle and the bone at the base of his thumb. David stopped with the keys raised before pressing the remote a third time. Across the lot, headlights flashed. At nearly the same instant, something inside the cello gave the same faint response, a thin stirring beneath the black shell.

The strings, the bridge: a sympathetic answer to the car’s confirmation chirp.

He looked toward the car, measuring distance. Forty-two feet, perhaps forty-three. The remote signal itself was radio frequency, useless to gut and wood. The locking mechanism had emitted a short tone, yes, but wind and snowfall should have eaten most of it before it reached him.

Still, sympathetic resonance could be absurdly delicate when an object had been waiting for permission to answer.

He pressed again. The car chirped, but the cello gave him only the ordinary weight of itself in his hand.

A snowplow scraped somewhere below the lot, metal against pavement, the ordinary world clearing its throat.

David lowered the keys as the phone rang again. Celeste. This time he answered.

“What?”

Silence first; then her voice, lower than he expected. “Where are you?”

“Leaving campus.”

“Are you in the lot?”

“Yes.”

“David, what happened?”

He looked across the lot at the car. Two clean copies remained on Rune’s desk. The marked working set lay folded inside his satchel.

“He told me I need time away.”

Celeste breathed in, and it was not surprise. “You knew.”

“No.”

“You called him.”

“Yes.”

“Before today.”

“After the seminar.”

“Before the cello.”

She did not answer quickly enough. “Yes.”

Snow gathered along the cello case in small, dissolving constellations. “I see,” David said.

“David, listen to me. I called because you lost time. You wrote five minutes of equations you could not remember writing. You almost collapsed in front of your students. I did not call because I think you’re wrong about everything.”

“Only about the part that matters.”

“That is not what I said.”

“It’s what Rune said.”

“Rune isn’t me.”

“No. Rune tells me when the Dean is on the other line and my work should stay on his desk. Very different office.”

“Don’t do that.”

“What?”

“Make it cleaner than it is.”

“That was the institutional ambition, I believe.”

“Where are you exactly?”

“At my car.”

“Stay there.”

The instruction arrived with the old authority of intimacy, the one she used when a string was about to break or a student had confused panic with interpretation.

“No.”

“I’ll come get you.”

“I have a car.”

“You also have not slept, you are furious, and there is a severe-weather advisory.”

“I slept seven hours.”

“David.”

“Double down on seven uninterrupted hours. Personal best. There may be a certificate.”

“Please.”

The word entered quietly and changed the argument. He closed his eyes. Rune’s office returned in fragments: the incident report, the pen moving through his work, the hand on his shoulder, the final diagram left open beneath the administrative page.

What you have here is better than nonsense. That is why it is dangerous.

“I need to go home,” he said.

“Then let me drive you.”

“No.”

“Why?”

Because she had called Rune. Because Rune had believed her.

Because if she came now, he might forgive her before he understood what forgiveness would cost.

Because if he let himself be rescued one more time by the person he had ruined and still needed, he might never discover whether he had any shape left without her hands on the edges.

“I need an hour in which no one is deciding what is best for me.”

Celeste was quiet before saying, “All right,” then, “Take the hour.”

“I’m forty-seven.”

“That has never stopped you from requiring supervision.”

The joke should have helped; it almost did. “I’ll call.”

“Say it properly.”

“I will call when I can.”

“That is not properly.”

“It’s what I have.”

“David.”

“What?”

“About last night.”

He said nothing, so she continued. “I don’t know what it was,” she said. “I don’t know what it proves. But I heard it too. After the F. After the passacaglia. The cello answered when neither of us was playing.”

The lot, the snow, the institutional catastrophe of the afternoon all moved half a step away.

Then the low string inside the case trembled again. David looked down. This time there had been no chirp. No nearby engine. No admissible cause whatsoever.

“David?”

“I have to go.”

“Wait. What happened?”

He ended the call, and for several seconds the phone remained in his hand while the cello’s response faded against his leg. Then the cold entered his fingers, and he pocketed it.

He opened the rear door, laid the instrument across the seat, and secured it with the belt as he had done for years. The belt clicked; the gesture embarrassed him, this little parental tenderness performed for wood and gut while every human arrangement in his life stood unsafely unfastened.

Only after closing the door did he notice the man standing between the adjacent vehicles.

The maintenance worker had vanished. This man was younger, in a thin coat, hood drawn forward, one hand buried in the front pocket, the other holding a cigarette protected inside his palm. He had been there long enough for snow to gather along his shoulders.

“Professor Lang?”

David turned as the man took one step nearer. “You David Lang?”

The use of his full name made the weather seem unusually steel-like.

“Yes.”

A second man appeared behind the car, broader through the chest, moving without haste. He wore no hood. Snow darkened his hair and failed to impress him.

The first man glanced toward the cello in the rear seat. “You got a minute?”

“No.”

David opened the driver’s door, but the broader man closed it with one palm against the metal. A correction, almost polite.

“Come on,” David said. “Whatever this is, I am not in the mood.”

The first man smiled around the cigarette. “Yeah. We can see that.”

“Move your hand.”

The broader man looked toward the far end of the lot. No one was close. A shuttle hissed at the stop near the library, accepted one late passenger, and pulled away without urgency. The storm filled the space it left behind as David became aware of the keys between his fingers.

The first man said, “Wallet.” The simplicity of it almost relieved him.

“Fine.”

David reached inside his coat as the broader man shifted nearer. “Slow.”

“I understand the convention.”

His hand found the wallet and drew it out. The first man took it, opened the billfold, removed the cash, and checked behind the cards with the weary efficiency of someone performing badly paid work.

“Phone.”

David handed it over, and the first man said, “Watch.”

“No.”

Both men looked at David’s wrist before the first said, “Don’t make this stupid.”

“It belonged to my father.”

“That ain’t our problem.”

“No,” David said. “It is not.”

He should have surrendered it; every measurable fact supported surrender: two men, poor visibility, no witnesses close enough to matter, a cello in the back seat worth more than the car, his own body unreliable and his judgment already entered into evidence.

The broader man took his wrist. David pulled back before thought could approve it, and the grip tightened.

VI. CRUCIFIXIO | It Takes a Beating

The clasp on the watch was old, stiff, designed for a man who had removed it only by ritual and never by force. The first man’s fingers slipped. He swore, and David laughed.

It was the wrong response. He knew it as the sound left him: wet, broken, unhinged. Arthur’s watch had survived sixty years of orchestra pits and airport security and the hands of a dead man, and the absurdity of its resistance broke through him with the force of insult.

The first man hit him, the blow catching his cheekbone and turning the world white. A brief, total subtraction took the storm, the parking lot, the two men, the phone in another man’s pocket, the watch still trapped beneath his sleeve, and folded them into one clean electrical failure.

When sound returned, it came back too loudly. The second man tried the watch himself. His fingers were thicker. He got the clasp halfway open before David’s free hand found his jacket and pulled.

They both went down, and the keys flew from David’s grip and vanished into the snow. A boot caught him in the ribs. He curled around the pain, one hand scraping asphalt for anything useful, anything sharp, anything that would return the world to sequence.

Another kick landed in his abdomen, and he stopped moving. For a moment there was only breathing, wind, the distant scrape of the plow, and the ugly little labor of the second man working the clasp.

Then the cello pulsed from inside the car, entering the lot first as pressure: a low vibration moving through the rear seat, the door, the frozen air, the soles of their shoes.

Both men looked at the car. David, curled on his side between the vehicles, kept his eyes closed while blood ran from his nose to the pavement.

“What the fuck was that?” the second man said.

When the first man opened the rear door, David moved before he knew he was moving. He caught the man’s sleeve.

The second struck him behind the ear. When he came back, the watch was free.

The second man held it in one hand, strap broken, crystal catching the lot light. David lunged. His fingers closed on air. The man jerked back, and the startled watch flew from his grip in a bright, spinning arc over the curb, past the salt bin, and into the winter shrubs beside the loading dock.

“Motherfucker,” the second man said.

He took two steps after it, then stopped. Someone was coming. David heard only the storm and the blood inside his own head, but the men heard more, or felt more.

The first straightened, his face turned toward the covered dock. The second backed away from the shrubs, suddenly empty-handed, suddenly finished with the watch, the car, the cello, the whole afternoon.

A small, dark shape moved behind the veil of snow near the loading bay, unhurried.

“Leave it,” the first man said.

“What?”

“Leave all of it.”

The second looked once at the cello case, once toward the figure approaching through the storm, and dropped the keys near the driver’s door.

Cash, phone, wallet: enough. The watch could remain abandoned in the shrubs, the cello breathing in the car, and David on the ground with his ruined papers while the storm did its patient work.

He tried to crawl toward the bushes, but the body declined. He tried again because the watch was there, because Arthur was there, because the thieves had failed and failure required recovery before the universe changed its mind.

His palm dragged through slush and his ribs lit white. The shrubs were only a few yards away and might as well have been Buffalo.

“No,” he said.

The word fogged once in front of him and disappeared as boots stopped beside him.

VII. DEPOSITIO / SEPULTURA | The Clear Snows of Fall
Largo, sospeso, quasi senza tempo

“You will freeze out here if you stay like that.”

The voice was close, low, and practical, edged by a dry cadence he could not place because pain made geography unreliable.

David lifted his head. A woman stood beside the open rear door of the car: short, wrapped in dark wool, her face lined without softness. Her pale eyes examined him as though injury were an instrument and he had been badly handled by amateurs.

“Missing something?” she said, extending her hand toward him.

In it, she held Arthur’s watch, smeared with road grime and slush, its crystal cracked and the leather strap hanging torn from the bezel. Her fingers clamped hard against the steel casing, holding the metal firmly as if contact itself required concentration.

“Where did you—”

“In the boxwood beside the dock. It was ringing like an alarm. Metal remembers where it was struck.”

She crouched, though the motion was stiff, bracing her other hand against the car door to anchor herself against solid steel. She pressed the watch into his palm, closing his battered fingers around it.

“Let me see your eyes.”

“What?”

“Eyes. Yours.”

She took his chin and turned his face toward the sodium light. Her touch was cold and strangely light, the pressure wavering for a fraction of a second before finding bone. Her thumb lifted one eyelid, then the other. The examination was brisk and clinical.

“Concussion?” he muttered through split lips.

“Likely, yes. Hope you didn’t have any sleeping plans for later.”

“Are you a doctor?”

“I was once,” she said, her mouth tightening into a thin line. “Though the title can be so easily bandied about these days.”

“Lady, I’m really not in a mysterious mood.”

“Of course, David.”

“How the hell—”

“Do I know your name? It’s an old tale. And one we do not have time for on this night.”

“Wait. Please.” David’s eyes flittered closed, his head flopping to one side.

The old woman reached into the snow and pressed a handful of slush into David’s face. He snapped back into the now.

“How about you stay right here with me for a bit. No nodding off to another dimension, okay. Neither one of us has that kind of time.”

She took his cut hand next. The broken crystal had opened his palm; blood followed the creases toward his wrist.

She glanced from his hand to the watch, then toward the cello case in the back seat. Her face shifted by half a degree, the lines around her mouth deepening with quiet recognition. She reached into the car, her gloved fingers settling onto the worn latches of the case.

The wood inside answered immediately: a low, resonant vibration that stirred through the spruce belly and hummed against the seat cushions.

“Good spruce,” she murmured, her hand moving along the upper bout with the practiced certainty of someone reading grain in the dark. “Still holding its arch. Elliot always insisted on an extra millimeter through the soundpost plate. Stubborn man.”

David went rigid, the cold in his ribs suddenly distant. “You knew Elliot.”

She looked at him, her pale eyes flat and unimpressed by the question.

“Well enough to argue with him for six months about your bass bar.”

The sentence went through David like a needle. He stared at the woman in the snow, trying to reconcile the figure standing in the slush with the instrument he had played since boyhood.

“You worked on this cello.”

“I saw what your grandfather wanted it to become,” she said, her mouth tightening. “Your bridge is still three tenths of a millimeter too high.”

She glanced down at the slush beside David’s boots, where the trampled, snow-soaked pages of his manifold paper lay half spilled from his satchel. She laughed under her breath, a dry, rasping sound against the wind.

“Is that your blood all over the pages, or… oh my… Rune’s red pen bleeding. Perhaps I should have brought this to you sooner.”

She reached into the pocket of her heavy wool coat and pulled out a slender tuning fork of dark, unpolished steel. She did not flourish it or hold it like a relic. She handled it the way a joiner handles a rasp.

“Here,” she said, thrusting the stem into his uninjured hand. “Take this thing away from me.”

David looked at the dull metal, his knuckles trembling with cold. “What is this?”

“Jesus, you must really be hurt. It’s a tuning fork, Lang. Try putting it near the instrument.”

David lifted the fork toward the open door.

Before the steel touched the case, the tines woke without being struck. A clean, faint singing rose off the metal, setting the rear window glass humming. From inside the velvet case, the cello answered with an immediate sympathetic thrum, deep and low, locking into phase across the foot of freezing air between them.

The woman watched the vibration settle, a faint glimmer of craft satisfaction crossing her face.

“Good,” she murmured. “Still paired.”

“That’s a C-sharp,” David said, the instinct of twenty years in the department surfacing before pain could stop it. “One thirty-six point nine, or slightly below. If you calculate the ambient temperature and the barometric drop—”

“Stop,” she said.

The word cut through the wind like a shear.

“That is exactly what you keep doing.”

David blinked, snow stinging his cut cheek. “Doing what?”

“Listening to the number, ignoring what answered.”

She swayed slightly, her boot sliding half an inch through the slush, and caught the roof of the car with her free palm. The metal gave off a dull, hollow thrum beneath her hand. Her breath came shallow and harsh, her knuckles white against the painted steel as if forcing the car to keep meeting her skin.

David looked up at her. “Are you all right?”

“Old occupational injury,” she said, waiting until her breathing leveled. “This hill and I don’t agree anymore.”

“What hill?”

“East Hill,” she said, her eyes fixed on the gray veil of snow beyond the lot. “The ground here has a foul memory.”

Her toe nudged the edge of a soaked sheet.

“You keep trying to make everything agree,” she said.

David’s breath hitched. “The model—”

“The interesting thing is usually the piece that refuses your model,” she said flatly. “Stop worshipping the pattern. Look at the resistance.”

She looked from the ruined pages straight to the cello case.

“The passive stage is done,” she said, reaching down to grab his shoulder. Her grip was cold and stiff, her boots grinding into the asphalt as she hauled him upright against the car. “Get in. Turn on the heater. Put the instrument in the front seat and get off this hill before your marrow decides to phase-lock with the rocks.”

David leaned against the car, his side burning with pain, his fingers clenched around the dark fork. “Wait. Who are you?”

She didn’t answer. She stepped back into the snow, turning toward the service road with slow, deliberate strides, planting each boot firmly into the frozen ground.

Behind him, the cello hummed once from inside the case: a low, solitary fifth that dissolved into the wind.

“By the way,” the woman called back through the falling snow.

He turned toward the sound. She had stopped beneath the yellow light of the loading dock, one hand resting on the salt bin to steady her balance.

“You should really have that watch cleaned,” she said. “So filthy you cannot even read the inscription.”

He looked down at Arthur’s watch in his palm. The steel backing was blackened with grease, dried blood, and decades of accumulated skin.

“How did you know there was an inscrip—”

He looked up.

The service road was empty. Snow fell uninterrupted through the yellow halo of the loading-dock lamp. On the salt bin, a paper cup had tipped on its side, rolling gently in the draft.

David looked down at the watch. He wiped the casing with the edge of his sleeve, then used his thumb to scour the grime away, scraping the cold metal until the hardened residue peeled back.

Letters emerged beneath the lamplight, shallow and precise, cut into the steel where the back had rested against his pulse:

In Time; E.G.

VIII. RELIQUIAE | Up From the Depths

The cello answered once more from inside the case, only the first interval, but enough to make David turn.

Nothing remained of the magenta thread. The men were gone. So was the woman. Snow was already softening their footprints.

That troubled him more than if she had vanished cleanly.

Arthur’s watch remained, cracked and cold against his wrist, its strap torn where the buckle had fought them. He remembered a hand at his arm, another at the clasp, the heel coming down near his ribs; remembered the stupid, animal twist of his body around the case, as if Arthur had been there in the snow with him and the only instruction left between them was to hold the instrument, hold the line.

His phone and wallet were gone. The keys lay beside the open car door, already dimming beneath white. He picked them up because leaving them there was a stupidity he could still identify.

He still had the cello, but the thought did not become relief; it became pressure.

David lifted the case from the seat.

Pain drove through his side so completely that the lot emptied of everything else. He bent over the instrument, unable to lower it without allowing the weight to pull through his ribs. For several seconds he remained half standing, half folded, breath coming in ugly pieces.

When the spasm released, he brought the strap over his good shoulder and gathered what remained of the satchel from the floor behind the driver’s seat: pens, receipts, a faculty parking permit, and the marked working set of the manifold paper, wet and trampled.

Rune’s red circles had widened in the meltwater until each objection became a pale wound in the page.

One sheet had folded beneath a boot print. David lifted it by the corner.

The rejected geometry occupied the center: the attractor Rune had dismantled. Two narrow trails crossed the page; one followed the curve, while the other left it.

The second path was not ink. It had no written edge, no graphite or toner to explain it. The paper had simply dried differently along that line, its fibers compressed into a shallow channel running from the false attractor toward the blank margin.

He looked at it until the cold got through his knees, then put the page away.

Driving was out of the question. The thought arrived not as prudence but as insult. He could see the driver’s seat, the smeared glass, the keys cold inside his hand. He could get home, clean up, call no one, explain nothing.

He took one step toward the front of the car and nearly went down; the cello struck his thigh, and the breath left him.

For an instant he saw the steering wheel not as an option but as an instrument of public death: his blood upon the airbag, his car folded around a lamppost, some undergraduate finding him because he had preferred dignity to gravity.

“All right,” he said, the words sounding childish inside the empty lot.

The tuning fork lay across his palm, narrow and cold, the stem darkened where his blood had dried along it. Where it had rested against the crescent cut, a blue line had appeared.

It was not ink, not exactly a bruise: cerulean at the edges, darkening toward the center as though the color had been pressed into him from beneath the skin.

Excellent. New symptom. Efficient evening.

He should have put it away.

Instead he held it against the cello case, metal to cracked vinyl, cold to cold.

No sound came at first. Then the case answered with a pressure so low it seemed less like vibration than fit. It entered through his palm, crossed the wrist, and found the bruise beneath his ribs.

David shut his eyes. When he opened them, the lot tilted.

“Home,” he said.

The cello answered, not loudly or helpfully, but with one interval held inside the case, and beneath it the fork gave off a thread of resonance too high for hearing. David felt it in his teeth, in the hinge of his jaw, in the bruised place behind the sternum.

He began to walk.

The first stretch was ridiculous. He had no better word for it. A tenured professor, beaten in a parking lot, carrying a cello that should have been stolen and was instead answering from inside its case, with a paper half dissolved in his bag and a tuning fork biting cold into his palm.

He almost laughed, but the attempt broke against his lip, and then the pain came back hard enough to take the joke with it.

He stopped beside the service road and bent over the case.

This is insane.

The cello became weight again, dead wood and string, thirty pounds of inheritance dragging at his shoulder while the hill below campus fell away through snow and sodium light.

David put one hand upon the case.

“No,” he said.

It was unclear whether he meant the instrument, his body, or the universe at large. The distinction did not seem useful. He tightened his grip around the fork until the blue line in his palm darkened.

“You get home.”

The fork answered first, not with sound, but alignment.

The pain did not vanish. Nothing as generous as that. It narrowed. His breath, which had been arriving in panicked fragments, found a count without becoming comfortable. The cello case moved against his leg at the end of each measure, not lighter, never lighter, but less opposed to him.

He descended as snow took the hill from him in sections. The faculty lot disappeared first, then the service road, then the line of buildings whose windows had already returned to institutional sleep.

Behind him, the tire ruts softened. The men’s prints blurred and joined the old tracks of maintenance carts and students and whatever ordinary traffic would be blamed for them by morning.

The place where the woman had stood vanished with everything else.

He kept walking.

At State Street, the pharmacy window gave him back a face bloodied from hairline to jaw, one eye narrowed behind the swelling, coat torn where the pavement had taken him.

A sane man would go inside, ask for help, surrender the evening to fluorescent light, insurance forms, police language, careful hands, concussion protocol.

Had he lost consciousness? Yes. Was he confused? Yes. Had he experienced visual disturbance? Yes. Auditory disturbance? Don’t be provincial. Had he seen anyone unusual after the assault? No physician would ask it that way.

That was the trouble with sane institutions. They arrived at the unsayable by degrees.

Rune’s voice entered with clinical patience: Have you considered that your certainty may be the symptom?

David looked at the reflection.

“Have you considered,” he said to the glass, “that your timing is poor?”

The automatic doors opened for a customer leaving with cough syrup and a lottery ticket. Warm pharmacy air reached him, smelling of rubber mats and artificial orange.

For one second he nearly went in.

The cello pressed against his leg: not a command, but a reminder.

Home.

He moved on.

After that the city narrowed to surfaces: curb, snowbank, black ice beneath fresh powder, brick wall, parking meter, the blue grocery awning beating itself against its frame.

He learned not to think beyond the next object. When he did, the distance opened and the pain rushed in to fill it; when he held only the next step, the fork and cello and whatever was left of him found one another again.

Three steps, then three more.

Every time the rhythm held, something in him spent itself to maintain it. Heat left him, and his fingers lost their finer instructions.

Twice the cello struck a post or the corner of a wall because his body understood distance half a second too late.

By the final hill, defiance had become too heavy for language. No wit or theory remained, only refusal.

He reached his building with the cello case dragging against his thigh and the tuning fork sealed inside his fist. His door sat upon the first floor, recessed just far enough from the street for snow to gather along the threshold.

He got the case through first, badly. The lower bout knocked against the jamb and sent the contact through his shoulder so sharply that the room flashed white.

His fingers missed the lock, then missed again. The key scraped uselessly beside the plate before finding the slot.

Warm air met him inside and made him weaker.

The apartment retained the morning’s disorder: coffee darkening in the press, loose sheets from the morning’s breakthrough, the chair pulled away from the slate wall.

Nothing had moved while he was gone.

David meant to close the door and got it mostly shut, but the latch did not catch, leaving a thin seam of winter air at his back.

The cello case slid down his leg. He tried to lower it carefully and failed.

It struck the floor with a dull wooden complaint.

David went down with it, not gracefully, not even completely, folding across the case where it lay beside the couch, one arm trapped beneath him, the other still closed around the fork. The broken watch pressed between wrist and rib. Snowmelt ran from his coat into the floorboards.

The door remained open several inches.

The last thing he heard was snow moving at the threshold.

Then everything went under.
