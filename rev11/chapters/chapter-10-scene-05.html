V. VIA DOLOROSA | Into the Storm

The wind had risen while they were inside, and the early exodus had given the storm the pleasure of looking theatrical. Upper campus had thinned to lit windows, plow noise, and the occasional student hurrying with the embarrassed speed of someone who had dressed for principle rather than weather. Loose snow drove sideways against the glass and flattened there before dissolving.

His coat remained unbuttoned. His scarf had come loose. Behind him, an official process had begun the sacred university labor of turning catastrophe into paragraphs with signature lines. Rune would be using his quiet voice. Jessica would be pretending not to hear. The Dean of Students, somewhere behind a different door, would be receiving the phrases by which a man became manageable.

Celeste’s name appeared on the dark face of the phone. He watched it ring until the screen gave up, then ignored the message that arrived a moment later.

The faculty lot lay beyond the science buildings, down the concrete stairs and across a service road already whitening along its edges. At the first step, his left foot landed slightly beyond the agreement he had made with gravity. His weight followed after the stair had ended, pitching him toward the rail. The cello case struck one iron support with a hollow, ugly report.

David caught himself and remained there for several seconds, one hand around the rail, the other gripping the case handle while the storm moved through the gap between buildings. The body, annoyed at having been consulted too late, reported several conditions at once: left ankle stable, right palm cold, ribs intact, dignity unrecoverable.

Anger changed stride. Grief changed balance. Three nights of bad sleep made errors of timing plausible, even boring. A new law of nature was hardly required; a departmental incident report blooming somewhere behind him could easily find ordinary cause enough.

The left foot felt normal when he tested it. Good. Excellent. A triumph for Newtonian mechanics. He descended.

At the bottom of the stairs, a man stood beneath the covered loading dock, smoking beside a yellow salt bin. David first registered only the ember, then the coat, then the face turning toward him. For one irrational instant he believed it was Rune.

The maintenance worker he had actually seen, or someone dressed as one, had heavy shoulders, wet cuffs, and a paper cup balanced on the salt bin beside him.

David crossed the service road. The man looked away before David passed, either uninterested or performing disinterest with unusual care. At the curb, a campus shuttle rolled by with three passengers inside, all lit blue by their phones. No one looked up.

By the time David reached the parking-lot entrance, the dock was empty and the snow had begun coming harder.

His car stood at the far edge of the faculty section beneath a sycamore whose last leaves had survived principally through spite. David took out the keys and pressed the remote. Nothing. He pressed again, and the cello answered from inside its case: a low internal pressure through the handle and the bone at the base of his thumb. David stopped with the keys raised before pressing the remote a third time. Across the lot, headlights flashed. At nearly the same instant, something inside the cello gave the same faint response, a thin stirring beneath the black shell.

The strings, the bridge: a sympathetic answer to the car’s confirmation chirp.

He looked toward the car, measuring distance. Forty-two feet, perhaps forty-three. The remote signal itself was radio frequency, useless to gut and wood. The locking mechanism had emitted a short tone, yes, but wind and snowfall should have eaten most of it before it reached him.

Still, sympathetic resonance could be absurdly delicate when an object had been waiting for permission to answer.

He pressed again. The car chirped, but the cello gave him only the ordinary weight of itself in his hand.

A snowplow scraped somewhere below the lot, metal against pavement, the ordinary world clearing its throat.

David lowered the keys as the phone rang again. Celeste. This time he answered.

“What?”

Silence first; then her voice, lower than he expected. “Where are you?”

“Leaving campus.”

“Are you in the lot?”

“Yes.”

“David, what happened?”

He looked across the lot at the car. Two clean copies remained on Rune’s desk. The marked working set lay folded inside his satchel.

“He told me I need time away.”

Celeste breathed in, and it was not surprise. “You knew.”

“No.”

“You called him.”

“Yes.”

“Before today.”

“After the seminar.”

“Before the cello.”

She did not answer quickly enough. “Yes.”

Snow gathered along the cello case in small, dissolving constellations. “I see,” David said.

“David, listen to me. I called because you lost time. You wrote five minutes of equations you could not remember writing. You almost collapsed in front of your students. I did not call because I think you’re wrong about everything.”

“Only about the part that matters.”

“That is not what I said.”

“It’s what Rune said.”

“Rune isn’t me.”

“No. Rune tells me when the Dean is on the other line and my work should stay on his desk. Very different office.”

“Don’t do that.”

“What?”

“Make it cleaner than it is.”

“That was the institutional ambition, I believe.”

“David.”

“Very white paper. Excellent margins.”

“Where are you exactly?”

“At my car.”

“Stay there.”

The instruction arrived with the old authority of intimacy, the one she used when a string was about to break or a student had confused panic with interpretation.

“No.”

“I’ll come get you.”

“I have a car.”

“You also have not slept, you are furious, and there is a severe-weather advisory.”

“I slept seven hours.”

“David.”

“Double down on seven uninterrupted hours. Personal best. There may be a certificate.”

“Please.”

The word entered quietly and changed the argument. He closed his eyes. Rune’s office returned in fragments: the incident report, the pen moving through his work, the hand on his shoulder, the final diagram left open beneath the administrative page.

What you have here is better than nonsense. That is why it is dangerous.

“I need to go home,” he said.

“Then let me drive you.”

“No.”

“Why?”

Because she had called Rune. Because Rune had believed her. Because last night she had placed the Bernstein book beneath the lamp, read the marginal hands correctly before he had finished inventing the wrong explanation, and still let him leave with the cello behind him and no notebook open on the passenger seat.

Because the cello had answered the car and then refused to do it again

Because if she came now, he might forgive her before he understood what forgiveness would cost.

Because if he let himself be rescued one more time by the person he had ruined and still needed, he might never discover whether he had any shape left without her hands on the edges.

“I need an hour in which no one is deciding what is best for me.”

Celeste was quiet before saying, “All right,” then, “Take the hour.”

“I’m forty-seven.”

“That has never stopped you from requiring supervision.”

The joke should have helped; it almost did. “I’ll call.”

“Say it properly.”

“I will call when I can.”

“That is not properly.”

“It’s what I have.”

“David.”

“What?”

“About last night.”

He said nothing, so she continued. “I don’t know what it was,” she said. “I don’t know what it proves. But I heard it too. After the F. After the passacaglia. The cello answered when neither of us was playing.”

The lot, the snow, the institutional catastrophe of the afternoon all moved half a step away.

Then the low string inside the case trembled again. David looked down. This time there had been no chirp. No nearby engine. No admissible cause whatsoever.

“David?”

“I have to go.”

“Wait. What happened?”

He ended the call, and for several seconds the phone remained in his hand while the cello’s response faded against his leg. Then the cold entered his fingers, and he pocketed it.

He opened the rear door, laid the instrument across the seat, and secured it with the belt as he had done for years. The belt clicked; the gesture embarrassed him, this little parental tenderness performed for wood and gut while every human arrangement in his life stood unsafely unfastened.

Only after closing the door did he notice the man standing between the adjacent vehicles.

The maintenance worker had vanished. This man was younger, in a thin coat, hood drawn forward, one hand buried in the front pocket, the other holding a cigarette protected inside his palm. He had been there long enough for snow to gather along his shoulders.

“Professor Lang?”

David turned as the man took one step nearer. “You David Lang?”

The use of his full name made the weather seem unusually steel-like.

“Yes.”

A second man appeared behind the car, broader through the chest, moving without haste. He wore no hood. Snow darkened his hair and failed to impress him.

The first man glanced toward the cello in the rear seat. “You got a minute?”

“No.”

David opened the driver’s door, but the broader man closed it with one palm against the metal. A correction, almost polite.

“Come on,” David said. “Whatever this is, I am not in the mood.”

The first man smiled around the cigarette. “Yeah. We can see that.”

“Move your hand.”

The broader man looked toward the far end of the lot. No one was close. A shuttle hissed at the stop near the library, accepted one late passenger, and pulled away without urgency. The storm filled the space it left behind as David became aware of the keys between his fingers.

The first man said, “Wallet.” The simplicity of it almost relieved him.

“Fine.”

David reached inside his coat as the broader man shifted nearer. “Slow.”

“I understand the convention.”

His hand found the wallet and drew it out. The first man took it, opened the billfold, removed the cash, and checked behind the cards with the weary efficiency of someone performing badly paid work.

“Phone.”

David handed it over, and the first man said, “Watch.”

“No.”

Both men looked at David’s wrist before the first said, “Don’t make this stupid.”

“It belonged to my father.”

“That ain’t our problem.”

“No,” David said. “It is not.”

He should have surrendered it; every measurable fact supported surrender: two men, poor visibility, no witnesses close enough to matter, a cello in the back seat worth more than the car, his own body unreliable and his judgment already entered into evidence.

The broader man took his wrist. David pulled back before thought could approve it, and the grip tightened.
