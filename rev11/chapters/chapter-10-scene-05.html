10.5 VIA DOLOROSA | Into the Storm
Mosso e inquieto, con passo irregolare

The wind had risen while they were inside, and the early exodus had given the storm the pleasure of looking theatrical. Upper campus had thinned to lit windows, plow noise, and the occasional student hurrying with the embarrassed speed of someone who had dressed for principle in defiance of the weather. Loose snow drove sideways against the glass and flattened there before dissolving.

His coat remained unbuttoned. His scarf had come loose. Behind him, an official process had begun the sacred university labor of turning catastrophe into paragraphs with signature lines. Rune would be using his quiet voice. Jessica would be pretending not to hear. The Dean of Students, somewhere behind a different door, would be receiving the phrases by which a man became manageable.

Celeste’s name appeared on the dark face of the phone. He watched it ring until the screen gave up, then ignored the message that arrived a moment later.

The faculty lot lay beyond the science buildings, down the concrete stairs and across a service road already whitening along its edges. At the first step, his left foot landed slightly beyond the agreement he had made with gravity. His weight followed after the stair had ended, pitching him toward the rail. The cello case struck one iron support with a hollow, ugly report.

David caught himself and remained there for several seconds, one hand around the rail, the other gripping the case handle while the storm moved through the gap between buildings. The body, annoyed at having been consulted too late, reported several conditions at once: left ankle stable, right palm cold, ribs intact, dignity unrecoverable.

Anger changed stride. Grief changed balance. Three nights of bad sleep made errors of timing plausible, even boring. A new law of nature was hardly required; a departmental incident report blooming somewhere behind him could easily find ordinary cause enough.

The left foot felt normal when he tested it. Good. Excellent. A triumph for Newtonian mechanics. He descended.

At the bottom of the stairs, a man stood beneath the covered loading dock, smoking beside a yellow salt bin. David first registered only the ember, then the coat, then the face turning toward him. For one irrational instant he believed it was Rune.

The maintenance worker he had actually seen, or someone dressed as one, had heavy shoulders, wet cuffs, and a paper cup balanced on the salt bin beside him.

David crossed the service road. The man looked away before David passed, either uninterested or performing disinterest with unusual care. At the curb, a campus shuttle rolled by with three passengers inside, all lit blue by their phones. No one looked up.

By the time David reached the parking-lot entrance, the dock was empty and the snow had begun coming harder.

His car stood at the far edge of the faculty section beneath a sycamore whose last leaves had survived principally through spite. David took out the keys and pressed the remote. Nothing. He pressed again, and the cello answered from inside its case: a low internal pressure through the handle and the bone at the base of his thumb. David stopped with the keys raised before pressing the remote a third time. Across the lot, headlights flashed. At nearly the same instant, something inside the cello gave the same faint response, a thin stirring beneath the black shell.

The strings, the bridge: perhaps a sympathetic answer to the car’s confirmation chirp.

He looked toward the car, measuring distance. Forty-two feet, perhaps forty-three. The remote signal itself was radio frequency, useless to gut and wood. The locking mechanism had emitted a short tone, yes, but wind and snowfall should have eaten most of it before it reached him.

Still, sympathetic resonance could be absurdly delicate when an object had been waiting for permission to answer.

He pressed again. The car chirped, but the cello gave him only the ordinary weight of itself in his hand.

A snowplow scraped somewhere below the lot, metal against pavement, the ordinary world clearing its throat.

David lowered the keys as the phone rang again. Celeste. This time he answered.

“What?”

Silence first; then her voice, lower than he expected. “Where are you?”

“Leaving campus.”

“Are you in the lot?”

“Yes.”

“David, what happened?”

He looked across the lot at the car. Two clean copies remained on Rune’s desk. The marked working set lay folded inside his satchel.

“He told me I need time away.”

Celeste breathed in, and it was not surprise.

“You knew.”

“No, David. I was afraid it might come to that.”

“You called him.”

“Yes.”

“Before today.”

“After the seminar.”

“Before the cello.”

She did not answer quickly enough. “Yes.”

Snow gathered along the cello case in small, dissolving constellations.

“I see,” David said.

“David, listen to me. You lost time. You wrote five minutes of equations you couldn't remember writing, and you nearly collapsed in front of your students. Roger was frightened. I was frightened. And every time I tried to talk to you about it, you gave me some clever little account of how perfectly well you were managing. What exactly was I supposed to do?”

“You could have called me.”

“I did call you. David, I have been calling you. Half the time you don't answer, and when you do, you tell me there's nothing to worry about.”

“Ah. So we have a reporting structure now. Roger briefs you, you brief Rune, and Rune briefs the Dean. Very efficient. I suppose I'll receive the minutes once everyone has decided what to do with me.”

“Don't do that.”

“What?”

“Make it cleaner than it is.”

“Oh, this is anything but clean, Celeste. You of all people should know that. I went into that office with twenty years of work, and I came out with instructions to stop doing it. My own work. And somehow he's managed to make the whole thing sound like a favor.”

“I didn't ask Rune to take your work. I asked him to look at what was happening to you. Those are not the same thing.”

“Aren't they?”

“David, whatever happened in that office, it doesn't change what happened in the lecture hall. You were terrified. Roger told me you couldn't even answer him. Do you understand how frightening that was?”

“I understand perfectly well what happened in the lecture hall. I was there.”

“No, David. For part of it, you weren't. That's precisely what frightened us.”

David shifted the cello case against his leg.

“And so you went to Rune.”

“Yes. Because I thought he might know something we didn't.”

“Oh, I imagine he knows a great deal we don't.”

“Then why did you go to him yourself?”

The question caught him, but only briefly.

“Because I wanted answers. Because I thought, foolishly, that after twenty years the man might finally have developed the professional decency to give me one.”

“And did he?”

“He gave me an incident report, a lecture on responsible conduct, and a very generous offer to keep my work on his desk while I took some time to recover my senses.”

Celeste was quiet.

“And you knew he would do something like that,” David continued. “You knew exactly what sort of man you were calling.”

“I knew he might be able to help you.”

“Help me. Yes, of course. There it is again.”

“David.”

“No, it's rather extraordinary when you think about it. Twenty years ago, the three of us stood in a room while that man explained which parts of our lives we were going to be permitted to keep. We signed, we nodded, we thanked him for his generosity, and then we went off to become the respectable little professionals he'd arranged for us to be. And here we are again. Same man. Same office. Same damned conversation. Only this time, you called ahead to make reservations.”

“That is a vicious thing to say to me.”

“Is it inaccurate?”

“Yes, David. It is. And if you'd stop for a moment and listen to what I'm actually saying, you might understand why.”

“Oh, I think I understand rather well. The only difference now is that you're safely over in Music. You've made a very nice life for yourself there, Celeste. No more forbidden experiments, no more unsanctioned theories. This particular brand of insanity no longer threatens your career. So now you get to be concerned. You get to stand at a comfortable distance, make the responsible telephone calls, and tell yourself you've done something kind.”

She let the accusation hang.

When she answered, her voice had changed.

“You think I called Rune to protect my career?”

David looked down at the cello.

“I think you called Rune to make certain he understood you weren't involved.”

“Jesus Christ, David.”

“Celeste—”

“No. You don't get to dress that up now. You think I would use what happened to you, what happened in that classroom, to make myself look better in Rune Ishikawa's eyes?”

“I think you've become very good at knowing which side of a closed door you want to be standing on.”

For several seconds she said nothing.

Then, quietly:

“You really are determined to make me regret caring about you, aren't you?”

He opened his mouth, but she continued.

“I was in that room twenty years ago, David. I signed the same agreement you did. I watched everything we'd worked for disappear into Rune's hands, and I lived with what came afterward, just as you did. You don't get to take those twenty years away from me because you're frightened now. And you certainly don't get to tell me that the life I've built since then means I no longer have anything at stake.”

“I didn't say you had nothing at stake.”

“You said quite enough.”

The wind pushed a thin drift of snow across his shoes.

“Look,” David said, more quietly, “I don't know what the hell is going on. I don't know why my hand stopped working or where those minutes went. I don't know why that frequency keeps turning up, or why the cello is behaving as though there's someone on the other end of it. But something is happening, Celeste. Something real. And I cannot sit down and wait while Rune decides which parts of it I'm allowed to investigate.”

“I haven't asked you to do that.”

“He has.”

“Then be angry with him. I won't stop you. But don't confuse my being frightened for you with my agreeing with everything he's done.”

David stared toward the car. Snow was beginning to collect along the windshield.

“I can't sign away my right to know what's happening to me,” he said. “Not again.”

Celeste exhaled.

“I know.”

“No, I don't think you do. Because now it isn't just the work. It's my own body, my own mind, and whatever is happening inside them. And everywhere I turn, someone who loves me is standing there explaining why I ought to let somebody else decide what comes next.”

“Are you at your car?”

“What?”

“Are you at your car yet?”

“No.”

“Stay there. I'll come get you.”

The instruction arrived with the old authority of intimacy, the one she used when a string was about to break or a student had confused panic with interpretation.

“No.”

“David, please. You haven't slept properly, you're furious, and there's a severe-weather advisory. I am not particularly interested in arguing with you about your driving abilities on top of everything else.”

“I slept seven hours.”

“David.”

“Seven uninterrupted hours. Personal best. There may be a certificate.”

“Wonderful. I'll frame it for you. Now would you please let me drive you home?”

“I have a car, Celeste.”

“Yes, I'm aware. I've ridden in it. I've even survived the experience.”

Despite himself, he almost smiled.

“Look, I appreciate the offer, but I need to go home. I need to sit in my own apartment without someone watching to see whether I'm about to come apart.”

“I wouldn't be watching you.”

“Yes, you would. And we'd both spend the evening pretending otherwise. I can't do that tonight.”

Because she had called Rune. Because Rune had believed her.

Because if she came now, he might forgive her before he understood what forgiveness would cost.

Because if he let himself be rescued one more time by the person he had ruined and still needed, he might never discover whether he had any shape left without her hands on the edges.

The silence that followed was different.

David closed his eyes. Rune's office returned in fragments: the incident report, the pen moving through his work, the hand on his shoulder, the final diagram left open beneath the administrative page.

What you have here is better than nonsense. That is why it is dangerous.

When Celeste spoke again, she sounded tired.

“All right. But I want you to understand something. I am not trying to take your work away from you, and I'm not trying to make decisions about your life behind your back. I called Rune because I was afraid something was happening to you that none of us understood. Perhaps I should have told you before I did it. I can accept that. But I won't apologize for being frightened.”

David rubbed his thumb along the handle of the cello case.

“I need an hour,” he said. “Just one hour in which no one is deciding what is best for me.”

“All right,” she said. “Take the hour.”

“I'm forty-seven, Celeste.”

“That has never stopped you from requiring supervision.”

The joke should have helped. It almost did.

“I'll call.”

“Say it properly.”

“I will call when I can.”

“That is not properly.”

“It's what I have.”

She allowed the silence to settle.

Then:

“David. There is something else.”

“What?”

“Last night.”

He waited.

“I've been thinking about it all day. About what happened after the F, after the passacaglia. I've gone back through the music, and I still can't account for it. I don't know whether it has anything to do with what you're experiencing, and I don't want you to take this as some grand confirmation of your theory.”

“Celeste—”

“But I heard it too, David. The cello answered when neither of us was playing. I heard it, and I felt it, and I have no explanation for it.”

David lowered his eyes to the case.

For the first time since answering the call, he had nothing with which to contradict her.

The lot, the snow, the institutional catastrophe of the afternoon all moved half a step away.

Then something stirred beneath his fingers.

A faint pressure traveled through the handle, slight enough that he might have mistaken it for the beginning of a tremor in his own hand.

He looked toward the car.

No chirp.

No nearby engine.

Nothing he could hear that might have set the strings moving.

The low string trembled again.

“David?”

He held the case away from his leg.

“I have to go.”

“Wait. What happened?”

He ended the call, and for several seconds the phone remained in his hand while the cello's response faded against his leg. Then the cold entered his fingers, and he pocketed it.

He opened the rear door, laid the instrument across the seat, and secured it with the belt as he had done for years. The belt clicked; the gesture embarrassed him, this little parental tenderness performed for wood and gut while every human arrangement in his life stood unsafely unfastened.

Only after closing the door did he notice the man standing between the adjacent vehicles.

The maintenance worker had vanished. This man was younger, in a thin coat, hood drawn forward, one hand buried in the front pocket, the other holding a cigarette protected inside his palm. He had been there long enough for snow to gather along his shoulders.

“Professor Lang?”

David turned as the man took one step nearer. “You David Lang?”

The use of his full name made the weather seem unusually steel-like.

“Yes.”

A second man appeared behind the car, broader through the chest, moving without haste. He wore no hood. Snow darkened his hair and failed to impress him.

The first man glanced toward the cello in the rear seat. “You got a minute?”

“No.”

David opened the driver's door, but the broader man closed it with one palm against the metal. A correction, almost polite.

“Come on,” David said. “Whatever this is, I am not in the mood.”

The first man smiled around the cigarette. “Yeah. We can see that.”

“Move your hand.”

The broader man looked toward the far end of the lot. No one was close. A shuttle hissed at the stop near the library, accepted one late passenger, and pulled away without urgency. The storm filled the space it left behind as David became aware of the keys between his fingers.

The first man said, “Wallet.”

The simplicity of it almost relieved him.

“Fine.”

David reached inside his coat as the broader man shifted nearer.

“Slow.”

“I understand the convention.”

His hand found the wallet and drew it out. The first man took it, opened the billfold, removed the cash, and checked behind the cards with the weary efficiency of someone performing badly paid work.

“Phone.”

David handed it over, and the first man said, “Watch.”

“No.”

Both men looked at David's wrist before the first said, “Don't make this stupid.”

“It belonged to my father.”

“That ain't our problem.”

“No,” David said. “It is not.”

He should have surrendered it; every measurable fact supported surrender: two men, poor visibility, no witnesses close enough to matter, a cello in the back seat worth more than the car, his own body unreliable and his judgment already entered into evidence.

The broader man took his wrist. David pulled back before thought could approve it, and the grip tightened.
