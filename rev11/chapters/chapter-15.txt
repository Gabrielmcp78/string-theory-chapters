15.1  Preliminary Observations

What little sunlight survived the city's particulate haze entered through windowpanes that hadn't seen fresh weatherstripping since the Eisenhower administration. Yellowing NASA flux graphs and annotated calendars obscured most of the glass, lending an unhealthy pallor to the man hunched above the table.

The bruise on David's cheek had faded from deep aubergine to a convalescent gray, the kind of battle scar no scientist expects to encounter in the mirror. He had grown accustomed to its shadowed presence. He saw it as a marker now. A talisman, perhaps.

The table was a topography of obsession. Manuscript folios, birth registers and death rolls mingled with solar ephemerides and a monograph on The Waxing and Waning of Geniuses, 1640–1911. At the edge, a row of index cards advanced, each marked with a birthdate and location in his compressed hand. Near the lamp, a dark, prism-edged shard sat beside a pen, swallowing the light that touched it.

The line of names halted at a space cleared for one object: the tuning fork. He'd measured its mass, mapped its decay on a battered oscilloscope, even attempted to analyze its alloy by licking it and calibrating the aftertaste.

It defied classification.

More troubling was the phenomenon he had now observed more than once: the fork had begun to vibrate in response to certain equations or dates.

Not all equations.

Certain ones, as if it had developed opinions about the matter.

A minor in mythology might have been a good idea, dumbass.

Leibniz, 1646, Leipzig.

As David moved the card rightward, aligning its date with a Schwabe cycle peak scrawled in Sharpie, the fork pulsed, a vibration that ran the length of his radius and ulna.

His first instinct was to dismiss it as a physiological response with no bearing on the experiment. But as he withdrew the card, the vibration ceased. On the second approach it returned earlier, apparently anticipating the alignment rather than responding to it.

David did not understand what he was scientifically to do with this information, so he took a deep breath instead. And as it happened, that was the most scientifically useful thing he had done all day.

No decision announced itself.

Euler and Faraday came next, their birthdates aligned in the same manner. The fork registered both: different by pitch, but nearly identical in response.

Okay. More of that then.

Then came Newton.

That card he had set apart, partly in defiance, partly in reverence. Newton's birth occurred in the trough of the Maunder Minimum: the sun's long silence.

All right, Ike, show me what you can do, sir.

At the beginning of the trough, the fork remained completely still. At the peak, more than seventy years later, nothing.

David held the card above the chart, studying the interval of solar inactivity and the extraordinary human life contained within it.

Without any intention he had registered, Newton was now hovering above the entire bracket of the trough.

The fork began to vibrate.

The response startled him badly enough that Newton slipped from his fingers. By the time David had risen from the chair, the fork had gone silent, leaving him with the absurd impression that he had interrupted something.

Jesus. Fine. I hear you. Fine.

Still unsteady, he braced himself against the table, and at the accidental brush of his fingers against the tines, the vibration returned.

He was going to have to reconsider the nature of the silence itself.

David reassembled the sequence as a spiral. Newton went at the center of the trough, a stone in a drained riverbed, the others arrayed outward from the silence.

At the blackboard, David began with the harmonic series. Below it came the solar cycles, peaks and troughs rendered as a step function; to the right, the birth years of his cohort, each plotted against flare, Newton's lifespan stretched beneath them as a quiet bar.

By the time he ran out of things to add, his hands were glazed with chalk and sweat. The fork was warm as blood.

Resonance math, solar epochs, human genius. The chalked arcs converged on flare and spanned silence. Once or twice he rested the fork against the board without quite knowing why.

Eventually the body asserted itself sufficiently to negotiate a retreat to the deep leather chair beside the fireplace.

Evening light, crimson and low, threaded through the glass, striking the fork's tines at a critical angle. Light flared through the room and the dark shard beside it received the beam.

White sheared into spectral bloom across the cards.

Looking through it, David could imagine the city's orange haze stripped away, revealing a structured magenta lattice stretching across the horizon. The geometry hung over rooftops and smokestacks with the quiet authority of a score waiting for its musicians. Familiar forms resolved into patterns that felt intimate and immense, descending from the star itself.

He left it all exactly as it was and went to make coffee.


15.2  Method and Resonance

David stood in the room's geometrical center, shirt untucked, cuffs stippled white, and regarded the blackboard like a criminal about to confess. A latticework had emerged overnight: dates, cosmic angles, irrational numbers repeating in motif, musical notation woven between set theory and prime factorization.

The alignment seemed too precise to dismiss. Solar cycle maxima coinciding with birth years of history's titans, a pattern carrying the familiar irritation of arriving late to something that had apparently been happening for centuries. Even the blank spaces participated. Places where chalk refused to cohere appeared to resolve into rhythm, held lightly enough that the next breath might dissolve it.

He paced with a quartet's inner pulse counting beneath the movement. Every fourth stride returned him to the cello in the corner. For days he had been content to let mathematics perform music's duties. The arrangement had seemed elegant enough until he looked at the instrument again and felt the deception collapse.

A ventriloquist could be impressive right up until you remembered what the act actually required. Then all the mystery drained away and you were left staring at a grown man with his hand up a puppet's ass.

The cello waited where he'd left it.

Enough of that.

When he finally sat, the instrument settled against his sternum with the cold familiarity of something neglected but not abandoned. Beneath his hands the strings drifted back into agreement. Nearby, the tuning fork occupied its usual place on the desk, radiating the confidence of an object that had never once felt obliged to explain itself.

He drew the bow across the open A and let it continue as a drone. Against it, on the D string, he began testing intervals, moving downward with the dull patience of someone expecting the universe to behave itself. Minor second, major second, minor third, major third: silence. The fourth produced nothing. Neither did the tritone.

He let the interval widen once more, releasing the open D beneath the A, and before he had time to decide whether he expected anything at all, the fork rang.

Unstruck.

The interval changed the room.

Air gathered between f-holes and chalkboard, thickening toward a standing wave until the fork broke into a microscopic orbit and began singing a phantom overtone two octaves above the sounding fundamental.

When the bow stopped, the tines settled. Proximity alone couldn't explain it, nor could some sympathetic buzz he had mistaken for signal.

He sounded the fifth again. The fork answered with the same clean certainty, and when he repeated the interval it answered again, indifferent to his growing suspicion that repetition ought to make the result less impossible rather than more.

Only the fifth.

Another explanation quietly left the building.

He wrote down the timbral signature before the thought had fully assembled itself, the way you catch a falling glass before deciding whether it deserves saving. Pitch alone was insufficient. If the fork cared about the fifth, he needed to know what it meant by fifth: the frequency relation, the place on the instrument, the particular string, the way the vibration entered the wood.

He found the same D on the G string and sounded it beneath the open A.

Nothing.

David held the bow there a moment longer, listening past the note into the absence.

Interesting.

The interval had survived. The response had not.

That eliminated the simplest version of the idea and made the remaining ones considerably worse.

He returned to the D string, but this time did not stop the note in the ordinary way. With a light touch at the one-third node, the string answered in its third harmonic, A sounding an octave and a fifth above the fundamental, the same proportion lifted into another register.

The fork rang immediately.

David stared at it.

Not the note, then. Or not only the note.

Something about the relation itself, or the way the instrument embodied it, was carrying across.

As he leaned harder into the bow, sound struck shelves, books, equations, and returned altered. For a moment the room seemed to lock into phase. Chalk dust hung suspended in amber, arranging itself into fleeting Chladni geometries before gravity reclaimed jurisdiction.

The resonance climbed through neck and nut, entered his bones, and found the bruise in his jaw with enough certainty to suggest a prior acquaintance.

Wood. Flesh. Waveform.

The distinctions became negotiable.

The bruise, apparently, had joined the experiment. For every three pulses in the room, it seemed to answer twice, locking him into a three-over-two relation with the room itself: the perfect fifth again.

When the bow left the string, silence arrived compressed rather than empty. The after-sound lingered in his skull until it ceased to resemble music and approached something older. Through the dark shard on the desk, the magenta lattice beyond the window seemed to tighten and clarify, its geometry gathering itself into relations he felt rather than understood.

For just an instant, he knew how small he had once been.

“It was always this,” he whispered.

The thought followed him back to the board. The formula lingered just beyond articulation. The notations crowded the margins with the infuriating composure of a solution that had mistaken his participation for a courtesy.

He uncapped a blue grease pencil to add the remaining names from the midnight symposium, only for the tip to stop above the spread.

One date remained that he had somehow neglected to plot.

The omission seemed almost noble until he realized it was his own.

Days spent arranging the births of geniuses, visionaries and assorted historical inconveniences across four centuries, and only now had it occurred to him that he, too, possessed a birthday.

Taking care to preserve the same coding, the same notation, the same standards he had applied to everyone else, he grudgingly wrote his name across the top of an empty card.

David Lang

The card looked immediately less respectable.

There. The contamination had occurred.

He copied the date and aligned it against the cycle.

SOLAR MAXIMUM

For several moments he stared at it with mounting irritation. The result possessed a certain vulgarity. Ordinarily this would have been the point at which a responsible scientist reminded himself that human beings are remarkably gifted at manufacturing evidence in support of their preferred conclusions. David had delivered that lecture often enough to graduate students, colleagues, funding committees and, on one memorable occasion, a dean.

So he removed himself.

Without his card the curve was smooth, clean, adequate. With it, the curve improved. He withdrew the card again and held it between two fingers above the table.

There are experiments one repeats to test a result, and others one repeats because the first result has frightened you.

An so, he put the card back.

The difficulty was that the argument cut both ways. How convenient to discover a passion for objectivity the moment the data started implicating him. And who, exactly, was he to dismiss it? The same institutions that had spent decades paying him, promoting him, publishing him and handing him increasingly expensive opportunities to continue being himself had apparently concluded he belonged in the conversation.

Was he now expected to develop a principled objection?

The thought struck him as equal parts absurd and alarming. Stripping himself from the dataset could be interpreted as rigor. It could also be interpreted as cowardice.

Scientific caution and self-protection had become difficult to distinguish.

The universe, if it had an opinion on the matter, appeared to have filed it already.

As he returned to the larger pattern, the dates resolved like a fugue's final cadence, establishing one begrudging truth: the universe kept infinitely better notes than he did.

Across the page the pattern gathered into a single arc, peaks, intervals, eruptions of genius rising from background noise. The arrangement began to resemble something less accidental than a collection of lives.

Certainty arrived in the only form David trusted: incomplete, argumentative and sufficient to proceed.

SOLAR MAXIMUM HYPOTHESIS

He underlined it twice. But as he lifted the pen, he noticed something he had not seen before. Third line from the top: his own derivation. He was certain of it. The compressed, left-leaning hand, the habit of closing the loop on the g, were all clearly his hand, but the transformation it showed was not the one he remembered writing yesterday.

The chalk was neither smeared nor overwritten. The marks carried the same pressure against the slate, the same hand, yet arrived at a different conclusion from the same premises.

Yesterday's version remained intact on the legal pad, but the board clearly had something else.

Without a single conscious thought, he copied the altered derivation into his notes, circled it, and returned directly to his work.


15.3  Findings and Implications

Twilight gathered in the study. Lang hadn't bothered with the overheads. The lamp clipped to the bookshelf afforded him just enough light to read, the scratch of graphite and the occasional turning page the only disturbances in the quiet.

He worked with a patience that would read to the outside world as obsession at best, and at worst…

Fuck at worst. You're not normal, Lang. This isn't normal work. Fuck what it looks like.

The words sounded foolish even in his head. But David had become increasingly aware of how little the adjustments toward normalcy had done to advance anything in his life. With each passing hour, the ordinary obligations of appearances, decorum and reasonable conduct seemed less relevant to the work before him.

Whether that was suppression, exhaustion or simple erasure no longer seemed particularly important.

The tuning fork sat where he'd left it, at the center of the cards. Still warm, he thought. Or perhaps that was him. Occasionally his attention returned to the cello in the corner, to the open A, the fifth, the overtone that seemed to linger after the room itself had finished listening.

Next came an entry altogether different. Not a person but an object.

THE MESSIAH. 1716. CREMONA, ITALY.

The violin to end all violins.

The thing occupied an awkward position in the theory. There was no birth to plot, no cortex to map, no human life for the sun to impress itself upon. Yet rumor clung to it heavier than names. The language surrounding its preservation, its construction and its extraordinary reputation seemed invariably to drift toward revelation.

Antonio Stradivari. Born around 1644, separated from Newton by scarcely two years. The Messiah emerged in 1716, in the immediate aftermath of the Maunder Minimum, as the long solar famine began to loosen its grip.

David began pulling references.

Alpine spruce. Tree-ring chronologies. Claims, contested, incomplete, maddeningly suggestive, that wood grown through the long cold interval might have acquired structural properties later generations had struggled to reproduce. Other studies complicated the picture. Density alone did not explain it. Treatment, aging, grain structure, microclimate: each paper seemed to open another door behind the one it claimed to close.

He read them twice, then a third time. None of it represented actual proof. Certainly not. But it was enough to bother him.

Newton had lived within the hush. Stradivari had lived there too. One had carried something out of it in thought. The other, perhaps, in wood.

The possibility shifted something important in the theory. Solar activity might not merely coincide with extraordinary minds. It might alter conditions: growth, material, development.

Flesh or timber made under one sky and not another.

That was different.

This wasn't genius bestowed from above by some celestial finger tapping Newton on the skull, but the work of constraint: systems shaped by what was available and what was withheld.

A transduction of hunger into voice.

He placed the Messiah card into the pattern and watched the surrounding relations settle around it.

He could no longer ignore the fact. It fit. Inconveniently, it fit.

The room had darkened around him. Only a thin smear of horizon light remained beyond the window, a handful of stars emerging through the city's haze. Behind him the blackboard had faded into shadow, but the pattern remained ghosted across his vision: curves, peaks, silences, connections.

Then came the hum, quiet enough that he almost missed it. For a moment he mistook it for the building: the old pipes, the radiator, some low vibration moving through the floor and into his teeth.

Recognition arrived only when he realized the rhythm was already in his mouth.

He had been humming it for some time without knowing: the same wandering line he remembered from his grandfather's office, from evenings when the old man stood at the sink at dusk, fingers tapping the counter in time with something nobody else appeared capable of hearing.

David stopped working and listened to himself.

The melody followed the path it always had: dusk, solitude, the mind outrunning itself. For years he had treated the habit as inheritance, one more unconscious piece of family debris carried forward without permission.

Now the distinction between inheritance and transmission seemed less certain.

He tried to locate where the tune had begun this time and could not. It was simply there in his chest, as if it had been running beneath the work all afternoon.

He wrote the opening interval on the legal pad, then followed it with the next and the next. The melody unspooled from memory, measure by measure, until bar eleven, where it diverged from everything he thought he remembered.

David went back and tried again.

Bar eleven remained unchanged: a descent in a key he would not have chosen, a shape he did not recognize as something he knew.

His attention moved to the blackboard: third line from the top. The altered transformation from the afternoon used the same intervallic motion.

For several seconds he did nothing. Then he drew a line between the two on the legal pad and sat with the pencil still touching the paper, listening to the lullaby continue beneath his breath.

Suddenly, an intrusion: the phone lit on the desk beside him.

Celeste, 9:14 PM: David. Please call me. I know you're in there.

He looked at the screen until it dimmed.

Outside, darkness had gathered fully across the city. Inside, another boundary seemed to soften. The cards on the desk, the fork, the blackboard, the remembered melody. Newton beneath a silent star. Stradivari shaping wood born within the same long silence.

Threads that should have remained separate were drawing toward one another with the effortless confidence of things returning home.

David turned back to the legal pad and drew the next line, the lullaby lilting on beneath his breath, slightly different each time, as though the melody itself were still sounding towards a more satisfying resolution.


15.4 Lossless Transfers

Chalk dust hung in the amber beam of the desk lamp, caught in the stale convection of the radiator until it settled into the cuffs of David’s unbuttoned shirt, into the open grain of the oak floorboards, and into the pores of his knuckles. For seven hours time had lost its social divisions, marked only by the irregular clanking of steam pipes beneath the floor and the slow, bruised ache in his left ribs whenever he drew a deep breath. Above him, all three black panels stood open from floor to ceiling, crowded with the remnants of six attempts to carry the 3:2 perfect fifth and its overtone intervals across the solar-cycle boundary, each coming apart at the identical zero-crossing.

In the textbooks he taught sophomores on Tuesday mornings, a crossing was an abstraction, frictionless and clean; beneath the pressure of this room, every attempt to carry the overtone into the lower phase tore the parameter apart. With a two-inch stub of Hagoromo trembling in his fingers after thirty-six hours of unrelenting calculation, he wrote through the closing bracket. For one breathless half-second the structure appeared to hold, until a three-cycle flutter at the threshold brought the symmetry apart. David leaned forward until his forehead met the cold slate, the mineral chill against the bruise above his left eyebrow almost welcome, and dragged his palm downward through three hours of labor. He stood with his eyes shut, breathing chalk dust in the silence of a house where nobody had spoken aloud since Sunday.

A chill moved up his spine as he turned from the wall, and from the grated bridge sixteen feet above the boards, an arid voice sifted down through the dust.

“If you are a scientist, you cannot stop such a thing.”

David’s hand went rigid against the slate, the Hagoromo stub slipping from his numb fingers to bounce twice on the oak and roll beneath the desk. He stayed still, knowing that to look up would grant the room permission to complete what it had begun, while the voice traveled through brickwork and copper risers and returned from the iron grate above, carrying the magnetic hiss of an old reel-to-reel tape whose brown oxide had flaked from decades of rewinds.

“If you are a scientist, you believe that it is good to find out how the world works… it is good to turn over to mankind at large the greatest possible power to control the world and to deal with it according to its lights and its values.”

Below the loft line, the apartment lay in quiet strata of low firelight, darkened kitchen, and city-washed window, with the spiral staircase rising toward the rafters.

“No,” David whispered to the black slate.

Down through the grating the voice came again, patient and merciless: “It is not possible to be a scientist unless you believe that the knowledge of the world, and the power which this gives, is a thing which is of intrinsic value to humanity, and that you are using it to help in the spread of knowledge and are willing to take the consequences.”

“No!”

The shout tore out of his throat, breaking the lock on his limbs as he drove himself up the spiral stairs, the cold cast iron biting his palms. Finding the bridge empty, he lunged around the top landing and onto the hollow grating where the chalkboards dropped away beneath his feet.

“Where are you?”

He threw open the balcony door, admitting a rush of subzero air that smelled of frozen mud and diesel from the gorge below, and stumbled onto the terrace, catching himself against the frozen iron rail above the sleeping streets.

Ithaca lay below him in an amphitheater of frost, East Hill rising in dark blocks of faculty houses and libraries while Cayuga Lake held the reflection of a winter moon so clinical and bright it seemed self-authored. The old physical truth occurred to him: the moon was dead rock, an emissary scattering the violent radiance of Her Majesty the Sun, hidden somewhere beyond the curve of the Earth.

As a wisp of cirrus cloud drifted across the lunar disk, the razor edges of the cornices dulled and the shadows in the gorge thinned. David watched the light spread outward through the vapor until the cloud cleared and lunar white returned.

Force continued. It had shifted, diffused, redistributed.

Breathing through the deep shudder in his diaphragm, David stepped back onto the catwalk and pulled the heavy glass shut until the brass latch clicked. The arrangement of furniture below had stayed the same, yet the charge of the room had shifted, as though the fire had been tended or a page on the table turned in his absence.

The apartment felt taken.

Leaning over the railing at the head of the stairs, he looked down toward the leather armchair he had dragged from Massachusetts four years ago. A figure sat against the orange glow of the hearth, broad through the shoulders, head slightly canted, one arm resting along the leather the way Arthur used to let his arm fall at two in the morning with a part book open across his knees and a yellow drafting pencil tucked behind his ear.

“Dad?”

The word escaped him with such naked expectation that he scarcely recognized his own voice.

Slowly, the figure lifted its head into the firelight. For one suspended heartbeat David’s body insisted on Arthur before the light shifted across the brow and sorted the details into an older register: looser skin at the throat, silver hair thinned to down at the temples, the same stubborn line in the neck.

“Son,” the old man said.

David tightened his grip on the railing. He could see the old man's eyes searching his face with an expression so painfully familiar that he allowed the mistake to stand a little longer.

Twelve feet of space separated the catwalk from the chair.

And in that distance Arthur was alive. As interval.

David began to descend. With each turn of the staircase, the hearth disappeared behind the central column and returned, illuminating some new arrangement of the features below. At one angle it was Arthur's mouth, drawn inward in the familiar effort to suppress a smile; a step farther down, the chin had acquired the softness of an old man. When the firelight returned, David recognized his father's hands.

The man in the chair leaned forward.

Something in his expression changed each time David emerged from behind the column, the eagerness with which he received each new glimpse gradually giving way to bewilderment. Once, as David turned toward him, his lips parted with such unguarded happiness that David nearly stopped descending.

At the bottom of the staircase, where the light held steady, the old man looked at him for a long while.

“David?”

He nodded.

Elliot blinked once, slowly, letting his gaze travel from David’s graying temples down to his mismatched wool socks, and a dry, crooked crease deepened at the corner of his mouth.

“Gray at the temples,” Elliot said softly. “Still walking a drafty house in your socks.”

A helpless laugh caught in David’s throat, pulling at the bruised skin above his eye. “They’re heavy wool.”

“They’re wet.”

“Only a little.”

“Well, that's all right then.”

The familiar severity with which his grandfather delivered the absurd conclusion brought David's hand to his mouth. He remembered the peppermint lozenges pressed into his palm during suffocating faculty dinners at Harvard, and the long afternoons Elliot had spent teaching him to square the face of a cello bridge with sandpaper and a flat block of maple.

“You’re in my chair,” David said, his voice cracking.

“If I’m here, it’s my chair.”

Elliot shifted, the old leather creaking in familiar protest. David lowered himself onto the arm of the chair, uncertain what he might disturb by coming any closer.

For a little while Elliot simply looked at him.

“Let me see you properly.”

David turned toward him, and the old man reached up to brush the graying hair away from his forehead. His fingers stopped when he discovered the bruise above David's eyebrow.

“Oh, dear boy.”

The tenderness of the reprimand was almost unbearable. David lowered his head, allowing his grandfather to examine him as he had after every childhood collision with a doorframe or the low branches of the apple trees in Massachusetts.

“I'm all right.”

“Apparently.”

David laughed again, and this time Elliot joined him, his shoulders lifting beneath the old wool coat. Sitting there beside him, David found himself absurdly unwilling to move, as though even the small domestic obligations of putting another log on the fire or fetching his grandfather something to drink might cost him a portion of an evening for which he had waited twenty-two years.

“You used to hum,” David said softly. “In your office at Harvard. I’d sit on the rug under the kneehole of the mahogany desk while you worked the boards.”

“You were a dreadful nuisance under that desk.”

“I was very quiet.”

“Until I wanted an eraser.”

David smiled. He had forgotten the erasers, which he used to line up like freight trains along the edge of the rug, assigning them elaborate destinations between the legs of his grandfather's desk.

Looking past David's shoulder, Elliot noticed the three wide panels of slate and the pale smudges of wiped labor.

“Your handwriting hasn’t changed since you were twelve. You still crowd the right margin every time the algebra corners you.”

David glanced back at the boards, feeling a sharp, childish heat rise in his ears.

“It's a little more complicated than it was when I was twelve.”

“I should bloody well hope so.”

Bracing his forearms on his knees, Elliot leaned forward.

“Show me.”

The invitation fell into the room with an ache David had not known he was carrying. For twenty years, conversations about this work had ended in warnings or negotiations, and here, with the fire burning low behind them, his grandfather was asking to see the mathematics for the simple pleasure of seeing what he had done.

David helped him out of the chair, and together they crossed the floor.

Elliot stood before the center panel with his hands folded behind his back, rocking gently on his heels as he followed the derivation from the upper left corner. David reached for the chalk tray, eager to explain the substitutions, but his grandfather lifted a finger, requesting another moment.

“Ah,” Elliot murmured.

He moved closer, tracing the progression with the tip of his index finger until he reached the boundary term.

“Is this where you're losing it?”

“Every time.”

For several seconds Elliot studied the neighboring terms, his mouth working slightly as he considered their relationship.

“You’re treating the fifth as a static symmetry,” he said, tapping the 3:2 ratio twice. “David. Why on earth are you pinning a live interval to a dead wall?”

“Because the solar-cycle boundary keeps breaking at the zero-crossing.”

“Yes, I can see that.”

“Every time I carry the overtone across the turn, the phase slips out from under me.”

Elliot leaned closer to the slate. “Then why have you assumed the same relation has to hold on either side?”

David opened his mouth to answer and stopped. He looked back along the derivation, following the terms to the point at which the restriction had first entered the proof.

“Oh, for Christ's sake.”

Elliot chuckled.

David wiped away a small section of the bracket and began again, allowing the relative phase to vary through the crossing while retaining the interval relationship between the coupled terms. His grandfather watched over his shoulder, occasionally inclining his head when David reached a substitution he particularly liked.

At the next bracket David stopped, looked at Elliot, and found the old man smiling.

“You see it?”

“Go on.”

With an excitement that temporarily overcame his exhaustion, David carried the substitution through the remaining terms until the offending singularity disappeared. The boundary held long enough for him to follow the coupling into the next line, where he drew two parallel strokes beneath the resulting relation.

68.48 Hz.

For three seconds they stood shoulder to shoulder, sleeves gray with chalk, grinning like conspirators who had beaten a house rule.

Then David reached across the slate and set down the private double-bracketed mark from his oldest notebooks. Turning toward his grandfather, he waited with an almost boyish anticipation.

Elliot's smile faded.

The old man moved so close to the board that his breath disturbed the chalk dust along its surface.

“Where did you get that sign?”

David had imagined answering that question a hundred different ways. Standing beside him now, he found that none of them would come.

“I thought you might recognize it.”

Elliot looked at him. For a moment his expression was so altered that David glanced back toward the fireplace, half expecting to discover that the man with whom he had been working was still sitting in the leather chair.

“How long have you been using it?”

“A long time.”

Elliot returned his attention to the board, following the relation downward from the mark until he arrived at the figure David had underlined. He took hold of the edge of the chalk tray.

“Have you done this before?”

“Not with the boundary closed.”

His grandfather's fingers tightened around the wood.

“Erase it.”

David gave a small, incredulous laugh.

“Take the felt, David. Wipe the panel.”

“We've just got it to hold.”

“I can see what you've done.”

“Well, look at the next term.”

Elliot caught his wrist before the chalk reached the slate. The force of the old man's grip startled him.

“Put it down.”

David looked from his grandfather's hand to his face, and something he had spent much of his adult life trying to understand suddenly felt intolerable.

“You haven't even looked at the rest of it.”

“I've seen enough.”

“You always had.”

Elliot released his wrist.

David stepped away from him, rubbing the place where his grandfather's fingers had been.

“Do you have any idea what it was like, standing outside that bloody door? I could hear you in there. I could hear you walking about at three in the morning, and every time I asked Dad what you were doing, he'd tell me to leave you alone.”

“David.”

“And now you're standing here, and for once in my life I can actually show you something I've done, and you want me to wipe it off the board.”

The old man's mouth tightened. Behind him, the fire settled with a small collapse of ash.

“I should have come out of that room,” Elliot said.

The simplicity of the admission took David entirely by surprise. For a moment he could think of nothing to say, and in that silence Elliot looked older than he had at any point since David had first seen him in the chair.
