6.1 SUBJECT | En Route to Buffalo, NY · Present Day

The seats aboard the Cornell charter coach to Buffalo looked comfortable enough. Deep cushions, generous legroom, upholstery that promised a civilized journey. But after the first hour, David had begun to suspect that the suspension was conducting a private experiment on his spine.

Upright in the seat ahead, the cello case stood braced between armrest and window, settled into place with the unremarkable patience of ordinary luggage. At the wheel, the driver kept his eyes on the road and seldom troubled the rearview mirror.

Above the windshield, a laminated sign read:

WELCOME TO THE NORTHEASTERN

SCIENTIFIC EDUCATORS ANNUAL CONSORTIUM.

NO FOOD. NO DRINK. NO QUESTIONS.

David's phone vibrated against his thigh.

Roger: Almost forgot. There's a wonderful old string shop somewhere in Buffalo. Haven't been there in years. Can't remember the name for the life of me, but if it's still there, they'll have a tungsten-wound C. Just what that ridiculous beast of yours needs. Ask your hotel concierge.

David smiled.

Thanks, Rog. Will do.

He put the phone away.

Through the vents ran a persistent buzz. Whenever the road supplied a sympathetic frequency, the cello found the narrow interval between that pitch and one of its own strings, trembling faintly inside the case. David pressed his knee against it. The hum retreated, returned, then retreated again, like a conversation he was not part of.

According to the onboard display, the three-hour journey had lasted twelve minutes; according to David's watch, three hours and seven. He found no reason to trust either.

At the hotel, the coach drew smoothly to the curb, as though arriving exactly on time for something important that had already happened without them. The other passengers gathered their belongings and moved toward the exit while David remained in his seat, looking out at the road they had traveled.

Only then did the driver glance into the rearview mirror.

“Professor?” he called.

David remained turned toward the rear window.

“Professor?”

Nothing, like he was watching something no one else could hear.


6.2   ANSWER   |   MIT - Cambridge, MA       April 11, 2005

“You're not listening,” David said. “I'm not saying music is mathematics. I'm saying they're dialects. Same language. Different mouths.”

Marcus McGrath occupied the room's only chair, his legs hanging over one arm, a beer balanced against his chest with the assurance of a man who had never once spilled anything consequential.

“Dialects.”

“Yes.”

“And what's the native tongue?”

David looked at him.

“Consciousness.”

“Oh, for Christ's sake.”

“I'm serious.”

“I know. That's what frightens me.”

Marcus sat upright, rescued the beer from his chest, and placed it on the desk beside a precarious stack of notebooks.

“David, you have a perfectly respectable mathematical model. A genuinely interesting model. You could publish the coupling work, establish the experimental basis, spend the next ten years making everyone's life miserable at conferences, and probably come out of it with a very handsome appointment somewhere that doesn't smell like this room.”

He glanced toward the radiator.

The radiator answered with three violent knocks.

“Instead, you want to tell the American Physical Society that consciousness is the language in which the universe conducts its private correspondence.”

“That's not remotely what I said.”

“It is exactly what you said. I'm improving the grammar.”

Celeste laughed into her beer.

She sat cross-legged on the floor near the radiator, her back against its unreliable heat, listening to them with an expression David had already learned to distrust. It meant she had reached a conclusion and was deciding how much of it either man deserved.

“You're both avoiding the interesting question,” she said.

Marcus turned.

“Wonderful. There's another one.”

She ignored him.

“David, what does your model actually predict that the conventional treatment doesn't?”

He began to answer, but she lifted a finger.

“Not what you think it means. What it predicts.”

He considered this.

“The response persists beyond the expected decay interval.”

“Under what conditions?”

“That's what we're trying to establish.”

“Then establish it.”

Marcus spread his arms.

“Thank you.”

“Oh, don't celebrate. You're being an ass.”

“I'm being an employed ass. There's a difference.”

David rose and crossed to the window. Outside, a bus labored past the building, its engine dropping in pitch as it pulled away from the intersection.

“There's something wrong with the assumption,” he said.

“Which assumption?” Celeste asked.

“That the systems are independent until we couple them.”

Marcus stared at him.

“Go on.”

David turned back.

“We treat the interaction as something introduced. An external condition. But what if it isn't? What if we're measuring the effect of a relationship that was already there?”

For once, Marcus did not answer immediately.

David saw it: the small movement of his friend's eyes toward the notebooks, the momentary suspension of professional skepticism while his mind pursued the implication.

Then Marcus shook his head.

“Fine. Beautiful. Now prove it.”

“I intend to.”

“No. You intend to announce it. There is a difference.”

David's expression hardened.

“You think I don't understand how this works?”

“I think you understand the mathematics better than anyone I've ever met. I also think you have the professional instincts of a man who brings a cello to a knife fight.”

“That's an absurd analogy.”

“It's a fantastic analogy. You'd insist on tuning it first.”

Celeste laughed again, but David did not.

Marcus saw the change.

“Dave.”

“What?”

“I'm trying to keep you from destroying your own work.”

“By keeping it hidden.”

“By giving it a chance to survive.”

David looked down at the scattered pages, at the columns of ratios and correction terms he had spent the better part of a year dragging into agreement. Beside the notebooks lay a page in Celeste's hand, musical intervals translated into a system of proportional relationships he could not have devised without her.

The work was theirs.

That was the trouble.

Marcus pushed himself out of the chair.

“You know what happens if you take this to somebody before we're ready? They'll ask one question you can't answer. One. And the entire thing becomes a joke. Not because the mathematics is wrong, but because you've given them permission to stop listening.”

David turned toward him.

“And how long do we wait?”

“As long as it takes.”

“Ten years?”

“If necessary.”

“Thirty?”

“David—”

“What if it takes thirty years?”

His voice had risen. He heard it and hated that he could not seem to bring it down.

“What if I prove it and nobody's left who remembers I was crazy for thinking it?”

Marcus studied him.

“Then you'll have been right.”

David shook his head.

“That's not the same as being heard.”

Celeste set her beer on the floor.

“David.”

He did not look at her.

“Come here.”

He remained by the window for another moment, watching the bus disappear behind the neighboring building. Then he returned to the floor and sat beside her.

She reached for the notebook nearest his knee.

“These ratios,” she said.

He glanced down.

“What about them?”

“You keep describing the system as though it's waiting for someone to discover the correct relationship.”

“Yes.”

“But it isn't waiting. It's already doing whatever it's doing.”

“Obviously.”

“Then stop trying to make the universe agree with you.”

Marcus gave a short, delighted laugh.

“God, I've been trying to tell him that for two years.”

“And you've been doing it badly.”

Celeste turned the notebook toward David.

“Find out what the relationship is. That's enough for now.”

He followed the columns with his eyes.

“And if we can't?”

“Then we can't.”

He looked at her.

She held his gaze.

“You don't have to win tonight.”

“We'll remember,” she said. “Marcus and I. We'll know what you were trying to say before you could say it.”

The radiator knocked again.

Marcus reclaimed his beer.

“For the record, I'd settle for one respectable publication before either of you rewrites natural philosophy.”

“Coward,” David said.

“Tenured coward, if everything goes according to plan.”

“You're not tenured.”

“Details, Dave. Mere details.”

He raised the bottle.

“To the lunatics.”

Celeste reached for her beer.

David hesitated, then lifted his own.

“To the lunatics.”


6.3   SUBJECT   |    Buffalo, NY       Present Day

The registration table was arranged with military precision, except for the line marked:

LAST NAMES BEGINNING WITH

UNCERTAINTY PRINCIPLE.

David hovered before deciding he belonged in the H–R line, though the volunteer behind the table glanced at his badge and said, “Oh. You’re one of those,” in a tone suggesting the category was well known but rarely defined.

The exhibitor hall buzzed with intellectual self-importance. Posters leaned on collapsible frames, each promising incremental advances in fields that seemed to repel one another. A theorist lectured animatedly to a graduate student who nodded while staring into the middle distance.

A senior physicist explained topological insulators to his winter coat.

The coat seemed unwilling to change its mind.

David kept hearing the shuttle’s hum whisper through the projector fans, carrying the same unresolved interval. He listened to the opening plenary with the attention one reserves for a fire alarm test: dutiful, doubtful, already bracing for the second round.

His own talk was scheduled for tomorrow afternoon. Location: The Morning Room.

Could he really be the only one hearing it?

The frequency seemed to flutter out of the objects he had blamed, gathering overtones until he could no longer tell which tone was the source and which the harmonic.

He grabbed a Local Tourist Info brochure from the rack beside the registration table and began sketching the harmonic relationships on the rear panel. He needed to map what he was hearing. If anyone else was to confirm his sanity, he would need data.

The moment the volunteer handed him a pen, another source, ambient and unknown, joined the chorus at precisely 136.96 Hz.

An exact doubling of the root.

One octave above 68.48.

And then he saw him.

Thirty feet away, near the poster session entrance. His beard owned twenty years of grey now, thicker through the middle, reading glasses perched on his nose as he squinted at a conference program.

Dr. Julian Kline, Astrophysics Chair at N.Y.U.

The hum surged in David’s chest with a density that sounded like recognition, as though the frequency had found something to resonate against and was doubling back on itself.

Kline looked up.

Their eyes met.

For exactly 2.47 seconds, neither moved.

David watched his own name flood Kline’s face, the recognition cascading from confusion to clarity and then to something that looked remarkably like dread.

Kline turned sharply, unwilling to risk a casual, even knowing second glance. He folded the conference program, tucked it beneath one arm, and started toward the opposite corridor with the stride of a man who had just remembered an urgent appointment that never was.

The phone at the registration desk rang out.

Two octaves above the root frequency.

David heard it before it rang. Or perhaps it had been ringing all along and only now had risen into audibility, doubling through one octave and then the next.

As the ring climbed, Kline telescoped forward as though someone had yanked a wire running through his spine.

He caught himself against the doorframe, one hand shooting out. The program fluttered to the floor.

He froze there for half a breath.

Then, slowly and intentionally, careful to give no one reason to notice, he straightened. He picked up the program without looking at it and disappeared down the opposite corridor.

David looked down at his own brochure.

His hands were shaking.

His left, gripping the paper, shook violently.

The volunteer had seen all of it. With Kline gone, he looked at David.

“Sir? Are you all right?”

David flashed him a slick smile of assurance, then looked down.

The brochure was covered in notation: frequencies, ratios, harmonic intervals spiraling out from a central axis.

The work was unmistakably written in his hand.

He had no memory of executing a single line of it.


6.4   ANSWER   |   NYU - New York, NY       May 24, 2005

Celeste’s voice hit the line like a rupture.

“You did what?”

“Exactly what I just told you,” David shot back, barreling out of the NYU student union, phone nearly slapped from his hand as he pushed into the crowds on Washington Square South.

“I did what had to be done,” he snapped. “I showed him.”

“Showed who?”

“Dr. Kline.”

A pause. Then her breath hitched. A soft, involuntary intake, as if the floor beneath her had shifted.

“David… you didn’t. Not the data?”

“I did. I showed him everything,” David said, voice thinning now. “The plots. The resonance curves. I thought… God, I thought he could take it.”

“You thought?”

In the background, a rustle of footsteps on tile, a muffled exhale. Then Marcus’s voice tore through the line:

“What the fuck, David?”

“Marcus, just hold on now. The moment Kline saw the data, he froze. Went white.”

“David, man. We talked about this. We agreed. No acoustic harmonic data. Not at NYU.”

“You weren’t there. It was a judgment call. I told you, I had to know. Had to see if he… I thought I had him.”

“Fuck.” Marcus’s voice went off like a shot.

“I’m telling you, something hit him. He staggered like the numbers had punched him.”

“You are unbelievable. Do you have any idea what this means?”

David knew exactly what it meant. He stepped off the curb and nearly went under a blur of cabs cutting across LaGuardia Place.

“Julian Kline? And you gave him resonance?” Marcus was past fury now. His voice curdled into mockery. “Congratulations, genius. It’s not theory anymore. You just took it live. You opened the goddamn door.”

“It’ll hold,” David said.

“You don’t know that! Jesus, David, you’re so naïve. The whole damn world is going to come flooding in! You’ve fucked us. All of us!”

David could hear him pacing, the voice jerking sideways as Marcus turned on Celeste.

“Your boy just dragged us off a cliff.”

“Marcus, listen to me. It’s going to be okay. You have to trust me.”

“Trust you?” Marcus snapped. “I’m not doing this over the phone. Get your ass home. Now.”

“Come on, Marcus. You know I would never—”

“No, David. The only thing I know is that you’re an idiot.”

Celeste's voice returned to the line before David could answer.

“Idiot?” David was spiraling now. “Who’s the idiot here, huh? Me, or—”

She didn’t yell. She never did. Not when her father vanished. Not when David cracked the ceiling. Not even now.

“David,” she said, soft. Precisely.

And that was all it took. He froze. Exhaled. Silence pressed in like gravity.

“I’m on my way.”
