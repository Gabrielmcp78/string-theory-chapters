9.1 AFTERSHOCK | Cornell — Ithaca, NY · Monday Night

The cab let him out into the slush a little after nine-thirty, its hazard lights ticking against the dark. David hauled two days of Buffalo luggage through the building's lobby, past the green glass door and down the long entrance hall, Arthur's navy zip-up fleece pulled over his clothes against the cold. He kicked off his boots and collapsed across the brown leather sofa without unpacking a single pocket.

Sleep took him before the pipes had finished their first percussive cycle. At eleven twenty-eight, he awoke fighting for air. The fleece had twisted around his neck in the dark, fabric wedged into his mouth, the familiar smell of his father's cedar shavings, stale pipe tobacco, and dry-cleaning solvent sealing off his throat. He had been dreaming of the old man because of the smell, though already the dream was dissolving in his skull before his eyes could find the ceiling plaster.

Yanking the zipper down until his teeth caught on the pull, he tore the fleece over his head and stumbled in his socks onto the cold oak of the kitchen, kicking the pedal of the trash can down with a sharp plastic clatter to shove it into the dark. Yet five seconds back on the couch, staring numbly at his stocking feet in the gray room, was all the distance he could bear; an instant later he was over the bin again, dragging the fleece back out from beneath coffee grounds and crushed eggshells. Wet grounds smeared across the ribbed cuffs as he buried his face into the collar, inhaling with a desperate, furious greed until his ribs ached with the musk of a man who no longer existed anywhere on earth. Shuddering, he rammed the filthy bundle back beneath the flap and let the lid slam down.

On the counter beside the tap sat Arthur’s chipped white mug, its scarred ceramic rim catching the dull light from the courtyard. His fingers reached for the handle with an instinct to shatter it against the sink, but the motion stalled an inch from the porcelain; instead, he ran the cold water until it roared, filled the mug to the brim, and drank in four violent swallows that spilled down his bare chest.

He refilled it. This time he drank slowly, holding the cold ceramic between both palms. His thumb found the familiar chip in the rim, and he remembered Arthur turning the mug around before handing it to anyone, always making sure the broken edge faced himself. David had teased him about replacing it for years. Arthur had never seen the point.

A reluctant smile came to him. He ran his thumb once more along the chipped porcelain, finished the water, and set the mug carefully beside the tap.

Back on the couch, wrapped in a woolen throw, he thumbed through his phone simply to put a screen between his eyes and the empty room. What followed was the mindless, frictionless catalog of the algorithmic feed: a stubborn goat refusing the rear gate of a hatchback; an overfed retriever belly-flopping into a laundry basket; a kid failing a backflip off a picnic table and landing flat on his back in gray mud. A sudden, undignified bark of laughter tore out of David’s throat, followed by another, until he was laughing openly into the cushions, watching the kid hit the mud a second time, then a third, laughing so hard his eyes watered and his chest burned with the sheer, idiotic safety of it. A man trying to clear a drift with a leaf blower only to have the gale blow it straight into his face sent him into another helpless spasm against the leather.

He swiped again. Kitchen renovations, chefs sharpening knives, vacuum cleaners. Another swipe, then another, until he stopped bothering. The videos continued without him, voices and music passing through the little speaker as his eyes closed, opened, and closed again.

Then, from the drift between waking and sleep, came a familiar and beloved voice.

“And so there is born what we now call tonal music…”

David’s eyes opened.

He lifted the phone, and nearly forty years collapsed in an instant. Leonard Bernstein stood at a piano before an audience at Harvard in 1973, hands sweeping across the keys in The Unanswered Question.

Lenny.

David turned up the volume.

“And that fifth interval really does dominate,” Bernstein declared, striking the keys again. The piano answered him, and David found himself smiling at the familiar extravagance of it.

Suddenly David was nine years old again, curled onto the top step of the staircase in Belmont with his cheek pressed against the cold turned spindles of the banister, listening into the bright, smoke-thick well of the parlor below. The house was full of the people who inhabited his father’s actual life. On almost any weekend during the season, the house would spontaneously fill with orchestral soloists, guest conductors, Ozawa laughing by the mantel with a glass in hand, coats piled four deep on the hall bench. Though forbidden from joining them, young David had never once been prevented from listening; night after night he had sat in the dark at the landing, straining to follow their fierce, overlapping arguments about Mahler’s tempos and distant conservatories, entirely certain that as long as his father’s voice remained audible among them, the world was a coherent, luminous place that would one day welcome him in.

The memory loosened, leaving only the small glowing screen. Bernstein was still talking.

“A work of art does not answer questions, it provokes them; and its essential meaning is in the tension between the contradictory answers.”

David smiled at the glass. God, he was wonderful.

He pressed share, selected Cassandra, and sent the recording without a message to his mom. She would know why. And with the magical musings of uncle Lenny floating in the background, he pulled the throw over his shoulders and fell into a dead sleep.

At twelve twenty-one, his phone lit up: a message from Mom.

My God! Where did you find this? Are you awake? Call if you are. Ha! Fat chance. Love you.

David did not stir.

9.2 THE CLASSROOM | Feynman Hall · Following Day

Thirty-seven students. David counted them twice from the podium while two sophomores at the back conducted an energetic dispute over midterm credit and a young man near the windows slept with his cheek flattened against a laptop, apparently confident that quantum mechanics could be absorbed through the skin.

The walk up the hill had already shaken him. Crossing the Arts Quad forty minutes earlier, the frost-killed grass near Goldwin Smith had flashed saturated magenta in the corner of his eye. When he stopped to look directly at it, the turf was merely dormant brown; when he turned away, the impossible hue flared back across his periphery, disappearing the moment he swung his chin around. He had briefly considered photographing the lawn, then realized the camera would only record the color the grass actually was.

Now, beneath the lecture lights, the crisp snap of a fresh cylinder of Hagoromo chalk against slate was an old, tactile comfort. He wrote the wave equation, established the fixed boundary conditions, and worked through the sinusoidal solutions with the steady fluency of someone who could still derive a vibrating string without assistance from sleep, memory, or a reliable nervous system.

“A remarkably efficient arrangement,” he observed, stepping back with dusted palms. “All the excitement of velocity, with none of the inconvenience of travel. I suppose for the gamers among us, that sounds rather familiar.”

Several students laughed, and he turned back to separate the harmonics into their constituent terms. Alongside the wave equation he wrote the familiar ratios for the octave, fifth, and fourth:

(2:1 \qquad 3:2 \qquad 4:3)

“Professor Lang?” Rebecca sat in the second row, her pencil poised with that intense, suspicious gravity she brought to every derivation. “Are those the ratios between successive harmonics?”

“Yes. Quite right,” David said, bracketing the figures with a quick stroke. “An important distinction. One I apparently expected you to infer while I stood here admiring my handwriting.”

The class laughed, and as he looked at the notation, an idea caught him.

“Actually, let's try something.” He drew three vertical lines on the board. “Suppose I generate a composite signal containing energy at three hundred, four hundred, and five hundred hertz. No physical component at one hundred. What pitch do you perceive?”

A student in the front row raised a hand. “Three hundred? The lowest component present?”

“Intuitive, logical, and entirely mistaken,” David said, sketching a broken coordinate axis beneath the harmonics. “Those frequencies share a periodic spacing of one hundred cycles per second. Under suitable conditions, your auditory system recovers the missing fundamental. You perceive a pitch corresponding to one hundred hertz even though the waveform contains no sinusoidal component at that frequency.”

Rebecca’s hand went up immediately. “So the listener hears something that isn't really there?”

“The pitch is a real perceptual experience. What isn't there is the corresponding spectral component in the air,” he said, tapping the chalk against the dotted line. “The information is present in the relationships, but the fundamental component itself is absent. Your auditory system has answered a question the signal never explicitly asked.”

He paused, catching the puzzled tilt of several heads. “Which sounds rather more philosophical than the syllabus warrants. Speaking of intellectual misbehavior—Leonard Bernstein.”

He erased a corner of the board and drew a short musical staff. “Bernstein gave a famous series of lectures at Harvard in 1973 called The Unanswered Question, borrowing from Chomsky's linguistic theories to explore whether music possesses anything resembling an innate universal grammar.” When only a single hand went up in the rear, David smiled. “Splendid. The rest of you have six lectures of spectacular intellectual misbehavior awaiting you. My mother sang in the chorus for Bernstein, which either gives me the hereditary right to sing in front of you or constitutes gross pedagogical negligence. We shall find out.”

He sang the familiar childhood taunt into the room:

Sol – Mi – La – Sol – Mi.

From the back came an immediate drawl: “Nanny-nanny boo-boo.”

Laughter rolled through the tiers. “Precisely,” David said. “Humanity's most durable contribution to acoustic communication.”

In the second row, Rebecca's pencil stopped.

“A descending minor third,” David continued, marking the interval on the staff. “And you've supplied something I didn't give you: the familiar words and their peculiar little sentiment.”

A student near the aisle raised her hand. “But isn't that learned? Children hear other children sing it, so it doesn't prove there's a universal musical grammar.”

“Certainly,” David said. “No, it doesn't. Though it does demonstrate that undergraduate skepticism is alive and well.”

The class laughed, and though she had exposed a flaw in the example, David was enjoying the diversion. He sang the opening notes again. “Now. Answer me.”

A student echoed the interval, another joined from the rear, and within seconds the entire lecture hall took up the melody. David raised his hands, egging them on. “Again!” The phrase returned louder, students turning toward one another across the desks, someone supplying an absurd bass part while two girls near the windows sang the response an octave higher.

David laughed aloud. “Merciful heavens. This is what tuition buys.”

He gave them another entrance, the room locking into phase, shouting the final cadence in a burst of collective competence. But as David raised his hands to cut them off, his eyes crossed the second row.

Rebecca sat with her knees pressed together beneath the desk, her hands buried in the sleeves of her cardigan. Her head was bowed, dark hair falling across her face, and when the boy behind her sang the refrain toward someone across the aisle, she flinched.

Then she looked up.

Their eyes met, and for an instant the lecture hall gave way to a playground in the hard sunlight of an autumn afternoon. A little girl stood beside a chain-link fence, her lunchbox hanging from one hand, while a circle of children sang the same five notes around her. Their faces were bright with the pleasure of belonging to something together, and the girl, perhaps seven years old, searched them for somewhere to go, some opening through which she might pass without having to hear the melody again.

A bell rang.

David blinked.

The lecture hall returned around him, thirty-seven students singing at his invitation, his own hands still raised above the podium. Rebecca was looking at him, her face pale beneath the dark fall of hair.

He lowered his arms.

“All right,” he said, quietly.

The singing faltered, one voice carrying on a beat beyond the others before falling silent.

“That's plenty. Thank you.”

He looked at Rebecca again, but she had lowered her eyes, and whatever question he might have asked seemed certain to make her more conspicuous. He turned toward the board.

“Now, before we all start demanding royalties from one another,” he said, writing beneath the staff, “the tonal center you expect in a melody is not the same thing as a missing fundamental in an acoustic signal. One concerns musical expectation; the other, periodicity. Do keep them separate, however tempting the resemblance.”

He resumed the derivation at a brisk, punishing pace, changing the boundary conditions and correcting a student rather sharply when the boy confused a fixed end with a free one: “Those are different physical constraints. You cannot simply exchange them because the algebra looks more attractive.”

He drove the class harder than he needed to; the mathematics was clean and he wanted the shelter of it. When Rebecca raised her hand to ask whether the solution would hold if tension varied along the string, he answered her with crisp, formal precision, then noticed that only three students in the entire room were still taking notes.

Setting down the chalk, he sighed. “Well. I've abused you sufficiently for one morning.”

A few students smiled in relief, and Rebecca looked up from her desk, her pencil resting across the notebook.

“Professor Lang? Should we copy that second proof?”

“Only if you're deeply devoted to gratuitous academic suffering.”

She gave a small, genuine smile. “I'll skip it, then.”

“A sensible career move. Class dismissed.”

The amphitheater emptied in its usual chaotic flood of backpacks and chatter. David remained at the podium wiping chalk dust from his hands, his gaze returning to Rebecca’s empty seat. For a moment the chain-link fence came back to him, sunlight bright along the wire and the little girl still holding her lunchbox. He set the chalk in its tray and left the equations on the board.

9.3 THE MESSAGE AND THE CORRIDOR | Later That Day

By late afternoon, the department office smelled of scorched coffee and overheated copier belts.

Mrs. Gable sat behind the counter, stamping requisitions with heavy, indifferent thuds.

“Professor Lang,” she said, sliding a square slip across the laminate. “Your mother called at eleven-thirty. Sounded worried. Asked if you’d received a parcel from Boston.”

“A parcel?” David asked.

“From Boston. That’s what she said.”

David looked down.

The Post-it was fluorescent magenta.

The same impossible color that had flashed across the frostbitten grass that morning. On the lawn it had vanished whenever he looked directly at it; here, between an ink pad and a tape dispenser, it stayed.

He picked it up and turned it beneath the fluorescent light. Magenta had no wavelength of its own, yet the paper kept its color when he looked straight at it.

Cassandra called at 11:30. Please ring her back when you’re out of class.

“Want to use my phone?”

“No, thank you. I’ll call from home.”

He folded the note, hiding the color, and slipped it into his coat pocket.

The corridor was empty, its long stretches of cinder block dim beneath the emergency lights.

David walked toward the south exit.

In his head, more sounds, more voices of other nights at the banister in Belmont.

“The second fiddle. I can get plenty of first violinists, but to find someone who can play the second fiddle with enthusiasm—that's a problem.”

Lenny was sprawled across the parlor armchair with a Scotch, arguing with Arthur about an old Philharmonic rehearsal in New York. Arthur maintained that Bernstein’s beat had been impossible to follow.

“Nonsense, Arty,” Lenny protested. “Darling, you simply weren’t watching me!”

“I was barely twenty-one!” Arthur shot back. “Watching you? Christ, Lenny, I was worshiping you!”

“Oh, stop that. No, go on,” Lenny joked, grabbing Arthur by the back of the neck and kissing him on both cheeks, the long butt of ash dangling from his lit cigarette like a trapeze artist on the wire.

“Besides, that baton of yours was on fire back then!” Arthur added, laughing.

Cassie passed through carrying a bowl of ice.

“That’s why you watch his cigarette, sweetheart. Everybody knows that.”

Bernstein nearly lost his Scotch. He doubled over, pounding the armchair, laughing so hard he began to cough.

David smiled at the empty corridor.

He had nearly reached the south doors when one swung inward. A young woman came through under the weight of an overstuffed backpack, still drawing the last of a fountain soda through its straw. The cup caught David’s coat, its lid sprang loose, and a handful of ice scattered across the tiles.

“Oh! God, I’m sorry, Professor Lang.”

“Not at all.” He caught the door before it struck her and glanced at the ice. “As it happens, I’m quite accomplished at ice removal.”

With the edge of his shoe he sent the cubes skittering over the threshold onto the frozen steps. She laughed, relieved, and recovered the lid.

“Thanks, Professor.”

“My pleasure.”

He held the door as she slipped inside, then stepped out into the cold.

9.4 RECOGNITION (CODA) | Arts Quad · Late Afternoon

Late afternoon had settled cold and gray over the Arts Quad. David turned up his collar as he passed beneath the darkening windows of Goldwin Smith, Arthur’s gold Omega ticking beneath his coat sleeve. An owl called somewhere among the bare trees, and he lifted his head, listening for another, when a passing current of air brought the rich, unmistakable sweetness of cigar smoke.

He stopped, half expecting Professor Wexler to be enjoying his customary post-lecture indulgence beneath the south portico, and looked back toward the building with the beginnings of a greeting already forming on his lips.

The portico stood empty, its stone columns pale against the gathering dusk, and the scent was gone. Across the Quad came the rush of the gorge rapids, the voices of students crossing the lawns, and, from somewhere behind him, a burst of laughter that lingered above the water before fading into the evening.

He listened as he walked on, and only as he descended the steps toward the road did he become aware of the melody moving softly beneath his breath.

Elliot’s melody.
