24.1  The Ultimatum

Morning arrived through the window in bands of gold and ferrous blue, sifting through the half-drawn Venetian blinds.

David's cheek registered the day's first warmth before the rest of his body agreed to wake. He flexed the right hand and watched blue-black stains, cerulean really, if you caught the light just right, flourish across the veins for a moment and then simply vanish. Two fingers still twitched at intervals, settling and stilling.

The cello case in the far corner stood open. Even now, as the apartment settled with the change in barometric pressure, the tailpiece and endpin hummed a microtonal interval.

He slumped into the rolling chair, a cartilage-grinding creak sounding from both man and machine, and swiveled toward the radiator where the television had perched. It was gone. He reached to his pocket and felt the reassuring weight of his phone.

He sat with that for a moment. The room around him was strangely, specifically quiet, a space that had already exhaled. The walls were bare plaster in the morning light.

His eye went to the desk.

The tuning fork lay where it always lay. But something was different about the space beneath it, a fold of paper, white against the wood. He crossed to the desk and lifted the fork with one hand. With the other he picked up the note.

Her handwriting. He knew it before he fully saw it, the particular economy of her letters, the way she pressed harder on the consonants than the vowels.

He unfolded it.

One sentence. Two lines. The ink slightly faded now at the crease.

If you become the face, they will see only the man. If you remain the sound, they will hear the universe.

He stood there a long moment, the note open in his hand, the fork warm from his grip.

She had written this before any of it. Before the broadcast. Before the solar event. Before the world had an opinion about David Lang.

He folded it once, carefully, along the original crease, and set it on the desk beside the fork.

Infuriating woman.

The thought pulled a reluctant grin from him. He reached for a pen and a blank sheet of staff paper.

Let them watch. Let them wait.

The universe could hold a note longer than any man.


24.2  The Final Score

He allowed the world to recede, like a tide withdrawing its threat, leaving him alone on a beach of ash and possibility. The noise outside (the chorus of reporters, the fizz of portable generators, the distant wail of someone’s outrage) collapsed to a point just beyond the window. In this low-pressure silence, David could hear the precise interval between his own heartbeats, the rhythm of breath scraping at the back of his throat, the microscopic creak of tendon as he flexed the phalanges in his right hand.

He gathered his implements: the weighted mechanical pencil, a fresh sheaf of staff paper, a glass of tap water, and the tuning fork. The fractured cello rested against the desk, its shattered bridge and sprung seams held together only by habit and shadow, the fork beside it offering the only pitch that remained true.

He began, as he always did, with a single phrase: six notes, rising, each spaced in an interval suggested by the contour of his own voice when he’d first spoken as a child. He wrote them out, as a stack of ratios, columns of fractions implying harmony, but never quite resolving. It was a structural imperative.

The pencil moved across the page, sometimes in precise lines, sometimes in a staccato jitter that implied hesitation. He found that the act of writing had become easier. The pressure outside fell away. The more the world clamored, the more he burrowed into the detail, the infinitesimal, the micro-states of possibility. He wrote with abandon, and the pages accumulated in a stack that, even to him, seemed improbable.

The composition, when it took on mass and inertia, revealed itself as a passacaglia, a structure of endless variation over a fixed, repeating bass. To a musician, it would sound strange but beautiful.

At intervals, he struck the fork against the wood. The instrument’s cracked body, even without tension, answered with a hollow sympathetic shudder that set the glass of water trembling. He tested the limits of the resonance, exploring what happened when the vibrations moved through fractured grain, or when the hollow cavity answered the fork between bridge and nut.

The staff paper filled, measure after measure, until the desk could not contain it. Two hundred and sixteen measures took shape across the pages. In the white spaces between systems, he scribbled notations in a private code, decipherable to no one but himself.

He reached for the glass, drank deeply, and in the moment of swallowing felt a conviction crystallize. The work could not be anonymous, but it could be unclaimable. He could encode himself into the structure, the DNA of the piece, in such a way that the world would know he was there, but never find the man to pin it on. It was a last act of defiance, a gesture toward the principle that knowledge, once loosed, belonged to no one.

Near the end, he wrote a single, cryptic message in the margin. The act took longer than the rest, as he weighed every glyph, every implication. He inscribed a single symbol: a fermata, the musical notation for "hold indefinitely."

He stared at the page for a minute, maybe more, the silence in the apartment now absolute. He imagined, for the briefest instant, what it would be like for someone else to find this, someone bright, or lucky, or desperate enough to decode its implications. Would they curse the author for not signing? Would they laugh at the arrogance of so much effort spent only to vanish into the footnotes of history?

He closed his eyes and heard the final note sustain, unresolving, held indefinitely.


24.3  The Catwalks

When the last line of the score settled and the pencil left the page, the apartment exhaled with him. The tuning fork lay, quiet now, precisely where he had set it. The air no longer visibly thickened around its metal stem, yet something in the geometry of the space remained fractionally misaligned, as though the world were waiting for him to notice that the center of gravity had shifted.

He became aware of it as pressure first: a faint tightening along the bones of his inner ear, a suggestion of pitch that had not yet decided whether to exist. He rose, feeling unexpectedly lighter, shoulders no longer braced against the invisible weight. He had carried this burden so long, his body now mistook its weight for posture. As he crossed the room, this pressure resolved into something more precise. A single tone, somewhere between memory and intention, entwined itself faintly through the spinal column of the building, humming along pipes and plaster, calling with the unmistakable authority of a note struck in exactly the right place.

It was his motif, the voice bent through another throat, recognizable to him nonetheless.

He did not argue with it. He was simply already putting on his coat.

The night outside was brittle and high; the hill rose dark against a sky scraped clean of stars.

The Music School complex; chapel, tower, the stack of rehearsal halls and practice rooms; the timbered amphitheater that had grown around them like crystal accreting, loomed ahead, its tall glass and brick faces catching the sodium glare of the lampposts. Light leaked through seams and clerestories where, officially, there should have been none.

As he crossed the last stretch of path, the tone strengthened. The whole structure seemed to vibrate lightly.

He took the familiar side door, the one that had always stuck a little in damp weather, and entered into the dim corridor that ran along the underbelly of the sanctuary. The smell washed over him at once: rosin and old wool, brass polish and the mineral tang of concrete that had known fifty winters. The air here had always felt denser than elsewhere on campus, thickened by the accumulated breath of students rehearsing etudes and fugues and their own futures hour after hour in the practice rooms below, by the sermons and quartets and whispered arguments the chapel above had hosted for a century. Now, that density had a direction. The tone was no longer vague; it was rising.

He followed it up the stairwells that had once been part of his daily migration between physics office and rehearsal hall, his hand grazing the rail polished by generations of nervous palms. At each landing the sound became more localized, less environmental and more specific, as if the building were narrowing him toward a single point.

By the time he stepped onto the loft level, where the lattice of catwalks stitched together the upper air above the main rehearsal spaces, he knew exactly where she would be.

Celeste stood at the far end of the central bridge, a dark figure edged in the pale luminance that rose from the hall below. She was facing away from him, toward the massive, suspended acoustic clouds and the timber ribs of the roof, hands resting lightly on the rail as if she were listening to the beams themselves. For a moment he simply watched her there, as he had watched her so many times in this building when they were young and unmade and everything they believed still fit inside a late-night argument.

She turned when he took the third step out onto the catwalk, recognizing the pitch she had been hearing approach for hours.

“Of course it was you,” she said, and the sound of his name in her mouth found the exact old place in his chest.

“Did you—”

“Hear it?” She gave a small, almost incredulous smile. “I think it’s been calling all night. I just finally admitted it was real.”

He came to stand beside her at the rail, both of them looking down into the dark shell of the hall. The parquet floor below, scuffed by decades of chair legs and stand feet, glowed faintly; acoustic clouds hung above like sleeping creatures, their undersides catching the slightest halo from the emergency lights. Somewhere deep in the fabric of the place, sound moved: the low, layered murmur of the building’s memory, all the dissolved rehearsals and recitals and experiments still reverberating in grain and joint and plaster.

“Remember the custodian?” she said.

“Chalk everywhere.”

“You were going to prove consciousness with a resonant ceiling.”

“You calculated the load tolerances.”

“I wanted to drill into the chapel floor.”

He laughed, low. “They threatened to revoke our keys.”

“Best week we ever had.”

The laughter dissolved something between them. Without needing to discuss it, they slipped into the old rhythm: overlapping sentences, half-finished references, ideas tossed and caught on instinct. They talked about the catwalk as instrument, about the Hilbert space of the ensemble that had played here last year, and so many other strange, inconsequential tethers and connections between them. All of them at once, as silly and foundational as ever they had been.

She stopped mid-sentence, turned to look at him full on.

“It’s gone, you know,” she said.

“What is?”

“That thing you’ve been dragging. You’re lighter.”

He started to dismiss it, tenure, aging, viral absurdity, but the words wouldn’t form. She was right. The weight was gone.

His hand found the rail, old dents under his palm. “Apparently the universe can manage without me.”

“Took it long enough to notice.”

“You know this was always the real work,” she said eventually, her voice softening as they reached the far end of the loft. “This.” She gestured broadly at the hall, at the catwalk, at the invisible river of sound moving through the timber and glass. “Reweaving things that were never meant to be separate.”

“And yet here we are,” he said. “The disappearing cellist and the stubborn theorist, orbiting our old cathedral like it hasn’t already made up its mind about us.”

She stopped walking then, turning to face him fully, and in that moment something in the between-space tilted. The hum under their feet seemed to grow more precise, collapsing from environmental noise into a single, slowly rising chord.

She stepped closer, with none of the hesitation that had shadowed their recent encounters; she took his hand, then his other, and drew them together as if closing a circuit.

The knowing arrived with the contact.

It arrived as physical fact, a pattern of interference he recognized instantly because his whole life had been spent listening for such things. Under the steady beat of her pulse there was another rhythm, quieter, smaller, but insistently there: a second oscillation nested inside the first. A new frequency, shattering the locality of the world around them.

His breath caught.

He knew without needing to ask; she knew that he knew without needing to speak. For an instant they simply stood there above the empty hall, hands braided, the air still between them.

“How long?” he managed.

“Week. Maybe more. Wasn’t sure until this morning.”

The unfinished phrase stood for riots and broadcasts and solar storms and men in suits quoting view counts like scripture.

“And then the building called. Thought if you were still in there, you’d hear it.”

He looked down at their joined hands, at the place where three pulses now beat in imperfect harmony. His thumb moved without permission, tracing a small circle over the fabric of her coat, over the place where cells were dividing, where a life was taking form.

“A son,” he said quietly.

“Yes.”

He tried to imagine it: the weight of an infant in his arms, the particular pitch of a child's voice calling him father, the slow accumulation of a shared life. He tried to feel what it would be like to stay, to choose the daily miracle of presence over the impossible thing pulling at the edges of his form.

He tried, harder than any try he'd ever known. Pushed toward the vision of staying. Forced himself to see it, to believe it possible.

He felt the immense gravity of the work he was abandoning, the frequency pulling him away. He knew that to stay meant returning to the limitations of a man, allowing the resonance to die with him.

“I wanted—” his voice broke. He stopped, breathed, tried again. “I wanted to teach him about the harmonic series. About Kepler and Newton and all the stubborn bastards who refused to believe the world was smaller than it looked.”

“You still can,” she said. “Just not in person.”

His forehead came to rest against hers again, and for a long moment they simply breathed together, her hand still pressed between both of his, the three-part rhythm steady beneath their palms.

She reached up and, with a grace so slight it hardly seemed like touch, laid her fingers across his mouth.

“Listen,” she said. “Just this once. Really listen.”

He closed his eyes because that had always made it easier, because she had taught him long ago that sight was sometimes just another distraction from the more difficult forms of perception. A hum underfoot rose, a loose music stand in some unseen corner vibrated at a sympathetic pitch.

“You’re already halfway gone,” she said. “You know that. Your body is a courtesy now. Whatever opened in you, it’s not closing.”

The hum came then, massive, deeper than sound, rising through the building's bones like a wave gathering itself. He felt it in his teeth, in his spine, in the space between his cells.

His form flickered.

He let himself lean into it, his form blurring at the edges as he consciously relaxed his grip on the physical world. He was choosing the frequency.

She stared at him, at the space he was beginning to vacate.

Her voice cracked, went sharp.

“Can't you give me one last moment? Goddamn you.”

The silence stretched. The hum settled back to its lower register, patient, inexorable.

David looked at her—at the stillness in her face, the terrifying, familiar composure that had steered them through decades of rehearsals and physics departments and madness.

“How can you just stand there?” he whispered, his voice cracking. “How can you always be okay? So confident. So sure.”

Her chin jerked up. A sharp, ragged breath tore out of her.

“I'm not okay, David. Jesus! I'm furious!” Her voice was shaking with something beyond fear. “I'm furious, and I'm terrified, and I have already yelled at the ceiling, at the gods, at you, about the timing.”

Her voice cracked. A hard, sharp sound.

“Do you have any idea—” She stopped. Started again. “I wanted this baby. I wanted you. I wanted both. And instead I get to be the widow-who-isn't-a-widow, raising a child alone while explaining that his father didn't die, didn't leave, just became too cosmically important to stick around for diapers and fevers and first days of school.”

Her hand tightened over the place where the pulse lived.

“I'm going to spend the rest of my life answering questions I don't have answers to. ‘Where's daddy?’ ‘Why can't he come back?’ ‘Doesn't he love us?’ And what do I say? ‘He loved us so much he turned into a dimensional frequency’? That'll go over great at parent-teacher conferences.”

She was shaking now. Shaking with the force of holding it together.

“And the worst part, the absolute worst part is that I understand. I get it. I know you could try to stay. You could drag your heels and fight every hour of it, and you'd end up tearing yourself and this room to pieces while our son watched. I know the math doesn't care about custody arrangements. I know all of that.”

She looked up at him, and her eyes were bright and furious and devastated all at once.

“But I still get to be angry that the universe is taking you. I get to hate that our son will never know your hands. I get to be fucking furious that everything we ever did that mattered led here, to you leaving before you've even met him.”

A brief, wet laugh finally slipped through.

“But everything we ever did that mattered, we refused to collapse the wave just because it frightened us.”

Her voice dropped.

“Even when it should have.”

He opened his eyes. There was a sheen to hers, but behind it no blur.

“You’re becoming something that doesn’t fit anymore,” she said. “And the world needs someone who understands what you touched but can still stand in a doorway and say no. Someone to hold the line. Someone to make sure they don’t just build prettier cages.”

“You,” he said quietly.

“Me.” The word hung in the air like firelight. “With people who don’t flinch when the math sings back.” She gestured around them. “This is what we started. You lit it. I’ll tend it.” She looked at him with ferocious clarity. “And he,” her hand pressed more firmly against her coat, “will grow up knowing you loved him before you even knew his name.”

She stepped closer, closing the space between them, and for a moment they were again what they had been decades ago: two graduate students held together by curiosity and stubbornness and the vigor of their arguments, except now layered over that was everything they had endured and lost and learned.

He leaned his forehead against hers and let himself, for three full breaths, want everything he could not have.

To stay.

To teach him the cello, watching small fingers struggle with the bow hold until the first clean note emerged after weeks of scratching. To argue with him as a teenager about string theory and tempo markings and whether music was discovered or invented, watching him grow tall and strange and entirely himself. To be a small human life instead of a vast, terrifying possibility.

To know his son's face.

The wanting was so sharp it felt like a wound, like something tearing open in his chest where the second pulse beat steady and small and utterly indifferent to his longing.

On the fourth breath, the hum underfoot swelled.

“You can’t stay,” she whispered, her voice carrying only recognition. “If you try, it will tear you to pieces. And I am not going to let our child watch that.”

He laughed then, unexpectedly, a sound that contained equal parts grief and admiration for her relentlessness. “You’re bossier than you used to be.”

“Than I used to be?” She drew back just enough to see his face. “You mean back when I wasn’t the boss? Well, someone has to be.” She smiled something like benevolence and kissed him gently on the cheek. “Go while you can still choose. Let it take you on your feet, not clinging to the doorframe.”

David stood there on the cold steel bridge. He could feel the threshold pulling at him, the low vibration singing in his marrow. He knew she was right. To stay would be an act of cowardice, a slow, selfish contamination of the lives he loved most. Relinquishing them was the only clean act of love left to him.

He nodded.

They walked back along the catwalk together, neither hurrying nor delaying, their steps automatically matching. At the threshold to the stairwell he stopped and turned to her one last time.

“Will you—”

“I’ll tell him everything that matters. And forget the rest in whatever direction makes him… kinder.”

A small smile crossed his face. “Good. Kinder is good.”

She waited, knowing there was more.

He looked down at her abdomen again, at the place where the second rhythm lived. When he spoke, his voice was quieter than she'd ever heard it.

“I'm so sorry, my son. So sorry that I won't know you. Sorry I won’t be there to…” He stopped. Swallowed. “Your mother is the bravest person I've ever known. And so very much wiser than me. Listen to her about everything except maybe tempo markings. She always rushes a good adagio.”

Celeste laughed once, wet and sharp.

“If I’d have known you were coming, I would’ve stopped all this searching a long, long time ago.”

He reached out and, with infinite gentleness, touched two fingers to the place on her abdomen where he had felt the second rhythm.

“Go well,” he whispered. To the child. “Go far.”

Then he stood, and with a kiss upon her cheek, he stepped past her, then on through the stairwell door, and began to descend.

“Go, my darling David,” she said to the empty space where he'd stood. “Go.”

Celeste stood alone on the bridge for a time, her hands on the rail, the cold from the metal gradually seeping into her palms, the warmth from his touch lingering in deeper places. She closed her eyes and quietly, with the measured attention of a scientist calibrating a new instrument, listened to the echo of the moment just passed, tracking how it moved through the structure, how it lodged in certain beams and slipped cleanly through others. She thought of the child inside her, already participating in this field of newly altered gravity.

“Someday,” she murmured, the word: an understanding and a vow.

Then she turned and walked lightly back along the catwalk and disappeared into the stairwell on the opposite side, her footsteps absorbed quickly by the old wood.

She did not feel the small square slide from the inner pocket of her coat as she went.

It fell slowly, caught for a moment on the rough lip of the beam, then freed by a stray draft of heated air from the ducts. The photograph spun once, twice, the image catching what little light there was: a tuning fork half-buried in frost-whitened soil, the ground fog curling around its prongs like breath. It drifted down through the open volume of the rehearsal space, past the sleeping acoustic clouds, past the empty music stands, past the front row of chairs, riding a gentle current of displaced warmth toward the lower door.

By the time David reached the lobby and pushed his way out into the thin early light, the building had already done its part. The glass doors sighed closed behind him, and as he took his first step down onto the path, something brushed his ankle and skidded to rest against the toe of his shoe.

He looked down.

The photograph lay face up on the damp concrete, edges curled slightly from years of being handled. He recognized the image immediately; it knocked the breath from him.

He crouched and picked it up. The paper was cool and faintly warped. For a moment he simply held it there, the past and present overlaying so perfectly that he could not tell which way time was flowing.

Then he turned it over.

On the back, in her handwriting, only one word:

Someday

The air seemed to thin around him. The campus soundscape: early-delivery trucks, a distant dog, the whisper of branches, receding under the steady, enormous quiet of that small inscription.

He straightened slowly and turned back toward the building, half-expecting, half-hoping to see her silhouette framed in one of the high loft windows.

There was nothing.

Only the odd, luminous stillness of a structure that had just witnessed a private sacrament and was now folding the memory of it into its beams.

He slipped the photograph into the inside pocket of his coat, over his heart, where the resonance of both pulses might find it, and then he turned away from the hill and began to walk towards home, his steps already drifting toward the impossible frequency of disappearing.


24.4  Disappearance

Dawn flexed its muscles over the horizon, peeling shadows from the buildings and laying down a mesh of light that crept, inch by inch, across the apartment floor. For the first time in days, maybe years, David found the silence restorative, a clean salve. The city’s collective pulse, so frantic in the blue hours, seemed to have forgotten him, or at least agreed to leave him alone for a moment of grace.

He sat back from the desk, vertebrae protesting with a full suite of pops and grinds, and regarded the finished work: two hundred and sixteen measures of score, each bar annotated with sidereal notations and the occasional wry aside in Latin. The message, inked in the lower margin of the final page, glowed in the dawn’s oblique angle. Even now, the fermata at the bottom, an arc with a single dot, struck him as a joke that only he and the universe would ever fully appreciate.

He rolled the stack with care, not wanting to crease the pages, and slid them into the manila envelope he’d scavenged from a neighbor’s recycling bin the night before. The act of sealing the flap with a strip of masking tape felt less like closing a container and more like completing a ritual.

There wasn’t much to pack. A change of clothes, a half-full bottle of prescription anti-inflammatories, the wallet (stripped of ID but heavy with unspent laundry tokens), and the tuning fork. The cello case, emptied of loose mutes and pencils, waited at the center of the floor, a pale stain of wood dust beneath its curved back. He circled it, slow and reverent, as if unsure how to begin a goodbye.

He knelt, balanced the instrument on one thigh, and ran the pad of his thumb along the neck, from nut to endpin. The vibrations that answered were dry, spectral, a reminder that the thing could still sing but preferred, for now, to rest. He found himself thinking not of the performances, not even of the viral videos, but of the long hours before the first note, when the world contracted to the simple geometry of hand, string, and the space in between.

He gathered what remained of the cello into its case, nestled the fractured neck into the velvet supports, and pressed down on the lid until the latches clicked shut. The sound, sharp and final, echoed through the empty room, setting a few loose sheaves of paper trembling on the desk.

He stood, envelope in hand, and hesitated. He propped the envelope against the closed cello case, aligning the seam with the instrument’s scroll, as if the two objects were in conversation.

He set the backpack down by the door. Here, in the wedge of light from the hall, he allowed himself a last audit of the space: the empty glass, the ghostly rectangle where the laptop had been, the smudged path his feet had worn into the hardwood. It was all as he’d left it, only lighter.


24.5  The Call

The apartment had gone hollow around him.

The cello was packed. The envelope was where it needed to be. His backpack waited by the door.

David went into the kitchen and called his mother.

She answered almost immediately.

“David, thank God. Tell me something. Can olive oil go bad?”

“Hi, Mom.”

“Because this bottle says March, which seems dramatic. March was practically yesterday. Also, what happens to olive oil when it goes bad? Does it become dangerous or merely disappointing? Those are very different situations.”

David leaned back against the wall beside the counter.

“I think it just tastes bad.”

“Well, that is useless. Everything tastes bad eventually.”

He smiled.

She had already moved on.

“Mrs. Bell is feeding that cat again. The alleged feral cat.”

“The one with the collar?”

“Rhinestones, David. Rhinestones. This animal has a social calendar.”

“Maybe it belongs to someone.”

“It belongs to everyone. That’s how cats avoid taxes.”

David let his head rest against the plaster.

“And your father’s printer made that noise again.”

“The printer.”

“Yes.”

“What noise?”

“That throat-clearing thing. Like it has an announcement.”

“Did you unplug it?”

“Of course I unplugged it.”

“And?”

“It stopped.”

He laughed softly.

“Well, what did you expect?”

“I don’t know. Something more theatrical. Your father bought it. I assume it has opinions.”

She was moving around her kitchen as she spoke. He could hear cupboards opening, something set on the counter, the small domestic percussion of a life continuing without permission.

“Oh, and I found my slipper.”

“The blue one?”

“Yes.”

“Where was it?”

“The freezer.”

David opened his eyes.

“The freezer.”

“Behind the peas.”

“How did your slipper get behind the peas?”

“If I knew, I wouldn’t have spent twenty minutes accusing a dead man.”

He laughed again.

“Poor Dad.”

“Poor nothing. Your father bought frozen peas like he had advance knowledge of a national shortage. Six bags at a time. Six. Who needs six bags of peas?”

“You apparently did. They found your slipper.”

“Do not encourage them.”

David smiled and listened.

That was all he did.

Cassy wandered from the slipper to Mrs. Bell, from Mrs. Bell to the pharmacist who had called her ma’am twice, from the pharmacist to a lamp she had decided was leaning even though lamps, according to David, did not generally lean unless someone had damaged them.

She was talking to him.

She was also talking to the room.

To the stove.

To the dead man who had once occupied the chair beside the window.

To whoever happened to be there.

David didn’t interrupt.

Eventually she noticed.

“You’re awfully agreeable this morning.”

“I’m listening.”

“That’s what worries me.”

He smiled.

“Mom.”

“No, really. Normally by now you’ve corrected at least one fact.”

“I’m trying something new.”

“Terrible idea.”

A little silence passed.

Not an uncomfortable one.

David looked toward the empty doorway.

Then he said, “Was Dad scared when you told him about me?”

Cassy stopped moving.

He could hear the quiet change on her end.

“Scared?”

“When you were pregnant.”

“Oh.”

David waited.

Then she laughed.

Not sadly. Not yet.

“Oh, sweetheart. Your father was completely useless.”

David smiled.

“Really?”

“I told him I was pregnant, and he sat down on the kitchen floor.”

“The floor.”

“Right there.”

“Why?”

“I never found out. We had perfectly good chairs.”

David looked down at his boots.

“What did you do?”

“I handed him a plate of piccata and sat down with him.”

David laughed.

“You did not.”

“I did. We ate dinner right there, slumped against the Frigidaire.”

Her voice had gone warm with it now. He could hear the smile all the way through.

“Infuriating man.”

David pictured them.

Young.

On a kitchen floor somewhere.

His father with a plate balanced on his knees.

His mother beside him because apparently if Arthur had decided this was where life had put them, Cassy had simply brought dinner.

“What did he say?”

“Almost nothing.”

“That must have been alarming.”

“Terrifying. I thought he’d suffered neurological damage.”

David smiled.

“He finally looked at me and asked if I was sure.”

“Of course he did.”

“Then he asked how sure.”

David laughed.

“What did you say?”

“I told him extremely sure, and if he asked again I was going to hit him with the piccata.”

“That seems fair.”

“I thought so.”

She went quiet for a moment.

Then, softer:

“He cried eventually.”

David’s smile faded.

“Dad?”

“Oh, constantly. He was just private about it.”

David looked across the kitchen.

He hadn’t noticed himself shifting.

Hadn’t noticed his knees bending, or his shoulder moving down the wall.

He only realized when his coat caught against the plaster and pulled slightly at his neck.

He adjusted it.

Kept listening.

Cassy had started telling him something about Arthur insisting that unborn children could hear Bach and her threatening to put headphones on her stomach and play the Rolling Stones out of spite.

David smiled.

And slowly, without deciding to, slid the rest of the way down.

His back came to rest against the wall.

His knees rose in front of him.

The kitchen floor was cold beneath him.

Cassy stopped talking.

Not abruptly.

Her sentence simply thinned out and disappeared.

“David?”

“Yeah?”

“Where are you?”

He looked around.

“The kitchen.”

Silence.

“Where in the kitchen?”

David looked down at himself.

A strange little smile crossed his face.

“On the floor.”

Nothing came back for a moment.

Then:

“You’re sitting on the floor.”

“Yeah.”

“In the kitchen.”

“Yeah.”

He heard her breathe.

“Celeste?”

David closed his eyes.

“Yeah.”

The silence that followed was different from all the others.

It did not need filling.

When Cassy finally spoke, her voice had lost all its scatter.

“Pregnant?”

“Yeah.”

“Oh.”

David rested his head back against the wall.

“How is she?”

“Good.”

“And the baby?”

“Good.”

“How far?”

“Not far.”

Cassy breathed slowly into the phone.

David could hear her sitting down.

He didn’t know how he knew.

He just knew.

“It’s a boy,” he said.

Her breath caught.

Only once.

“A boy.”

“Yeah.”

“Oh, David.”

Neither of them spoke for a while.

Then Cassy said, “Your father would have bought him a violin.”

David smiled.

“No.”

“Oh, immediately.”

“I’d burn it.”

“He’d buy another.”

“I’d burn that too.”

“A very expensive family tradition.”

David laughed quietly.

Cassy did too.

The sound faded between them without either trying to hold onto it.

After a while she said, “What’s his name?”

“He doesn’t have one.”

“Good.”

David opened his eyes.

“Good?”

“You shouldn’t name someone before you’ve met him. Seems presumptuous.”

“That’s surprisingly reasonable.”

“Don’t get used to it.”

He smiled.

Another silence.

Longer this time.

Comfortable.

Almost.

Cassy said, “Your father was afraid, you know.”

David looked toward the window.

“I know.”

“No. I mean after.”

David waited.

“After you were born. After we brought you home. He used to sit beside your crib and listen to you breathe.”

David’s fingers tightened loosely around the phone.

“He thought something was wrong?”

“He thought everything might be wrong.”

A faint smile touched her voice.

“That was Arthur.”

David lowered his head.

“He’d count the spaces between your breaths. Then you’d change the rhythm and ruin all his work.”

David smiled.

“What did he do?”

“Started over.”

Of course.

“Sometimes he’d pick you up.”

“Did that help?”

“No. You screamed.”

David laughed under his breath.

“Good.”

“Terrible baby.”

“So I’ve heard.”

“But eventually you’d grab his finger.”

Cassy’s voice had become very quiet.

“And then he’d just sit there.”

David looked at his hand.

“How long?”

“Until you let go.”

The kitchen seemed very far away.

Cassy did not explain the story.

She didn’t need to.

After a while she said, “There’s that sound again.”

David looked up.

“Sound?”

“The one you don’t make.”

“Mom?”

“The one that tells me too much.”

David closed his eyes.

“Mom, I never meant to—”

“David, you don’t need to.”

Her voice stayed even.

Then it softened.

“Just…”

The line fell silent and heavy.

David waited.

“Mom?”

Nothing.

“You there?”

“Yes.”

“Just what?”

Cassy breathed in.

When she spoke again, she sounded almost ordinary.

“When you get where you’re going, just remember to call your mother, okay?”

David pressed the phone tighter against his ear.

“Okay.”

“I love you, son.”

He looked across the empty kitchen.

“I love you too, Mom.”

Neither of them hung up.
